Spider Guard

Spider guard uses sleeve grips and one or both feet on the opponent’s upper arms to manage distance and balance. Understand the connections before studying sweeps or submissions.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 21 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Spider combines sleeve control with foot contact on the upper arm.
  • Double spider uses both arms; mixed configurations use one.
  • Adjusting leg length and hip angle helps manage distance.
  • A lost connection calls for recovery or a deliberate transition.
  • A sweep or submission requires its own entry and controls.

What Is Spider Guard?

Spider guard is a gi open guard combining sleeve control with a foot on the opponent’s biceps area. Double spider uses both feet on the arms. Other configurations use one spider connection while the free leg frames the hip or forms another hook. The sleeve grip and foot contact work together; a foot touching an arm without control is not the same connection.

How Spider Guard Works

The sleeve grip helps retain an arm while the foot changes its distance from your hips. Extending one leg and bending the other can change the opponent’s balance and your angle. Keeping both legs rigid is not the goal. Notice whether you can adjust your hips while retaining the connection, and whether the opponent can withdraw or circle an arm.

Sweeps and submissions require additional control. A partner’s step or post can create an opening, but does not guarantee a triangle, omoplata, or sweep. Learn the specific entry as its own lesson rather than treating the guard position as a finish.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve grips provide the fabric connection used in conventional spider guard. A foot on one biceps and a different connection on the other side is still a useful configuration; both feet need not remain on the arms throughout the exchange.

No-gi. Wrist control and leg frames share some distance-management ideas, but lack the same sleeve attachment. Use those positions on their own terms rather than expecting a sleeve-based sequence to work unchanged.

What Does Spider Guard Look Like in Practice?

Start by observing a cooperative double-spider setup. The passer shifts their weight or circles an arm slowly. The bottom player adjusts one foot and their hip angle while maintaining the other connection. Pause when contact is lost and identify whether the sleeve, foot, or angle changed first. Add a coach-selected attack only after that distinction is clear.

Types / Variations

Double spider

Both sleeves are controlled and both feet contact the upper arms. The legs can perform different jobs rather than extending symmetrically.

One spider / one hook

One spider connection combines with another frame or hook. Describe the free leg’s role to distinguish the configuration.

Spider-lasso

One leg makes a spider connection while the other wraps around an arm in a lasso. Each side has a different leg path.

Sitting spider

A more upright variation changes the distance and hip position. Follow the specific configuration shown by your instructor rather than assuming one posture fits every opponent.

Entries, Counters, and Passes

Entry. Establish sleeve control and a usable frame as you move into open guard. The order and foot placement depend on your starting position.

Top player. Work to free an arm or remove its foot connection before moving beyond the legs. The bottom player may replace the frame as you move.

Bottom player. Reconnect the foot, recover distance, or change to lasso or another open guard when appropriate. A lasso transition needs its own grip and leg path; it does not happen automatically whenever an arm circles.

Spider Guard vs Lasso Guard

Factor Spider Lasso
Foot position Sole on bicep Leg wrapped around their arm/sleeve
Control feel Posture break via extension Tighter entanglement, harder to clear
Mobility Higher foot mobility Lower; more sticky
Common pair Often combines with lasso Often combines with spider

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Check the current rules for your event, age, belt, and gi or no-gi division. A familiar guard name does not establish whether every connected grip or submission is permitted. The official rules resources are linked below.

Safety

Train under qualified instruction. Do not hyperextend partners' elbows by violently kicking the biceps; apply controlled pressure.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming foot contact is enough without sleeve control.
  • Locking both legs rigidly and losing the ability to adjust distance.
  • Chasing a submission after the supporting frame has disappeared.
  • Forcing a lasso transition without the required grip and leg path.

Check your understanding

Can you distinguish double spider from one spider connection combined with another frame? Watch one slow exchange and identify the first connection lost, rather than judging the whole attempt only by whether a sweep happened.

Further reading

Stephan Kesting and Brandon Mullins: Unlocking BJJ provides a named instructional reference. Use the current IBJJF rules resources for competition requirements; guard names alone do not establish whether every connected technique is allowed.

FAQ

Questions about identifying the position and reviewing it after class.

Do both feet have to be on the biceps?

No. Double spider uses both feet on the arms, while mixed configurations keep one spider connection and use the other leg elsewhere.

Can I play spider guard in no-gi?

The conventional position depends on sleeve grips. Wrist control and foot frames can serve related purposes, but create a different connection.

Why does my foot slip off the arm?

Look at sleeve tension, contact placement, hip angle, and the opponent’s movement. The symptom can have several causes; review the moment contact is lost with your coach.

How is spider different from lasso?

Spider uses a foot pressing on the arm with sleeve control. Lasso wraps a leg around the arm. They can be combined, but the leg paths and controls differ.

Should I keep both legs fully extended?

Do not treat maximum extension as the objective. The useful distance depends on the configuration and the opponent’s reaction. Preserve the ability to adjust your hips and leg position.

What should beginners learn first?

Identify the sleeve-and-foot connection, practise maintaining a frame cooperatively, and learn one recovery when contact is lost. Add a specific sweep or submission under instruction.
