Open Guard

Open guard is the Brazilian jiu-jitsu family of bottom positions where your legs are not locked around the waist—using feet, hooks, and frames to manage distance, retain against passes, and sweep, with gi systems favoring sleeves and lapels and no-gi systems favoring butterfly and SLX.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Open guard = unlocked legs; a family, not one shape.
  • Retention first, sweeps second.
  • Gi: spider, lasso, worm, collar-sleeve DLR; no-gi: butterfly, SLX, RDLR, seated frames.
  • Standing vs kneeling passers demand different hooks.
  • Compare with closed guard when you need connection vs range.

What Is Open Guard?

Open guard is any bottom position where your legs are not locked around the opponent's waist in closed guard—feet on hips, biceps, hooks outside the legs, or seated frames. It is a family, not a single shape: De La Riva, spider, lasso, butterfly, X-guard, and more all live here under the parent guard concept.

Open guard trades the security of locked ankles for range, mobility, and modern passing resistance.

How Open Guard Works

You manage distance with feet and frames so the passer cannot glue chest-to-chest. Hooks off-balance them; grips (sleeves, collars, ankles, wrists) steer their posture; hips move constantly to retain when they clear a foot. Retention (keeping them in front of your legs) is the first skill; sweeps and submissions come second.

Standing vs kneeling passers. Against standing passers you use long hooks (DLR, RDLR) and ankle control. Against kneeling passers you elevate (butterfly, X) or frame (knee shield / seated).

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi open guard thrives on sleeve and collar grips: spider, lasso, worm, collar-sleeve DLR. Fabric slows the passer and creates lapel puzzles.

No-gi open guard replaces sleeves with wrist control, body triangles of the arms, seated butterfly, SLX, RDLR without pants, and K-guard entries. Retention relies more on frames and hip movement because grips slip.

Meta. IBJJF gi sport still showcases spider/lasso/worm. ADCC-style no-gi pushes athletes toward butterfly, SLX, and wrestling-aware seated guards.

What Does Open Guard Look Like in Practice?

Jordan stands to pass Alex's closed guard. Alex opens, plants a foot on the hip, hooks a De La Riva, and off-balances Jordan into a sweep—or transitions to spider when sleeve grips appear (gi).

No-gi. Same stand-up; Alex frames the biceps, sits to butterfly, elevates, and enters X-guard or SLX instead of hunting sleeves.

Types / Variations

Hook guards (DLR / RDLR)

Outside or reverse hooks on a standing passer's lead leg—see De La Riva.

Sleeve guards (spider / lasso)

Feet on biceps or wrapped sleeves—gi native. See spider and lasso.

Elevation guards (butterfly / X / SLX)

Hooks that lift and tangle—excellent in no-gi. See butterfly, X-guard, single-leg X.

Lapel guards (worm and cousins)

Gi-only puzzles using fed lapels—see worm guard.

Seated vs supine

Seated open guard (butterfly-heavy) vs playing off the back; both are open guard.

Entries, Counters, and Passes

Entries. Opening closed guard on purpose, pulling guard to seats, recovering from half, inverting under a pass.

Retention. Follow their pass with hip heist, shoulder roll, frames, and re-hooking—Chewjitsu and Gordon Ryan both stress retention habits before fancy sweeps.

Common passes. Torreando, knee cut, body lock, long step, smash—each attacks a different open-guard structure.

Open Guard vs Closed Guard

Factor Open Guard Closed Guard
Connection Feet free; lower lock, higher range Legs locked at waist
Best when Standing passers, modern sport Posture break, beginner home base
Gi bias Spider, lasso, worm dominate Collar-sleeve closed still classic
No-gi bias Butterfly, SLX, RDLR homes Often temporary without overhooks

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Open guard is legal across IBJJF and ADCC. Sweeps score; playing open guard itself does not. Some lapel and reaping situations have rule nuances—know your event when combining open guard with modern leg entanglements.

Safety

Train under qualified instruction. Inversions and heel exposure need mat awareness. Tap early in scramble leg locks that start from open-guard entanglements.

Common mistakes

  • Static feet with no hip movement—easy to pass.
  • Opening closed guard with no frames ready.
  • In no-gi: relying on spider/lasso mechanics that need sleeves.
  • In the gi: ignoring sleeve grips that make open guard sticky.
  • Chasing sweeps before you can retain.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Should beginners start with open guard?

Usually after you can break posture and move hips from closed or half. Jumping straight to complex open systems without retention habits is frustrating.

What open guard works best in no-gi?

Butterfly, SLX, RDLR, and seated frames transfer best. Spider and worm need fabric.

Why do I get passed the second I open my closed guard?

You opened without frames or a hook ready. Place a foot on a hip or bicep and move your hips as you unlock the ankles.

Is De La Riva open guard?

Yes—DLR is a major open-guard subtype for standing passers.

How important is guard retention vs sweeps?

Retention is the foundation. Sweeps without retention just reset you under a better passer.

Seated or on my back?

Both are open guard. Seated butterfly-heavy games shine in no-gi; supine hook games shine against standing passers in both.

The Bottom Line

Open guard is the unlocked-leg family of bottom games: manage distance, retain, then sweep. Explore De La Riva, spider, butterfly, and neighbors; compare with closed guard when you need a locked connection.
