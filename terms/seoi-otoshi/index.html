Seoi Otoshi

Seoi otoshi is the shoulder drop: forward kuzushi, a seoi-shaped fit, then a pull-down finish with one or both knees on the mat—Kodokan Te-Waza, not merely a nickname for modern drop seoi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Kodokan Te-Waza: pull-down with knee contact at kake.
  • Parent Seoi Nage (Judo); compare Ippon Seoi Nage.
  • Do not conflate with competition Drop Seoi Nage slang.
  • Needs forward Kuzushi before knees hit.
  • Follow to Ne-Waza / Osaekomi.
  • Score literacy: Ippon, Waza-Ari, yuko.

What is Seoi Otoshi?

Seoi otoshi (背負落 / shoulder drop) is a Kodokan te-waza in which tori unbalances uke forward, takes a seoi-shaped posture, and finishes by pulling downward while one or both knees contact the mat. Classical teaching—especially Daigo Toshiro’s Kodokan nage-waza notes—draws a conceptual line: seoi-nage lifts and throws; seoi-otoshi pulls down with the knee or knees grounded at the moment of execution. Martial Index nests this page under the Seoi Nage (Judo) hub for family browsing while recognising its catalogue home among hand techniques on Te-Waza.

Gyms and World Tour commentary often say “drop seoi” for any knee-drop shoulder attack. That slang is useful for film study and useless for taxonomy. When you need competition language, open Drop Seoi Nage. When you need the one-arm standing classic, open Ippon Seoi Nage. When you need two-hand upright loading, open Morote Seoi Nage. This page stays with the Kodokan otoshi name and the pull-down idea.

Search intent for “seoi otoshi judo” is definitional and comparative: how it differs from seoi-nage, whether knees must touch, and how it scores under modern IJF rules. Filed under Nage-Waza on the judo style hub. BJJ cousin seoi-nage remains BJJ-labelled when you cross-train.

Historically, seoi-otoshi sits with other hand drops and wheels that emphasise hand levers over pure koshi drive. That does not make it “weak”—it makes timing and downward vectors the point. Athletes who only muscle a standing lift will mis-teach this throw forever.

How it works

Begin with forward Kuzushi. A straight or slightly right-forward break puts uke onto the toes; without it, dropping to the knees only invents a new injury mechanism. Enter as for seoi—morote sleeve-and-lapel or ippon trapped-arm shape—then drop the lead knee (or both) while the hands continue a downward pull over the shoulder line.

Keep elbows glued to the ribs so the back remains a loading surface. Track the head along the throw path; do not spear the tatami. If the knees leave the mat mid-throw and the action becomes a lift, teachers may reclassify toward seoi-nage. That pedantry is literacy, not snobbery: it tells athletes what they are actually drilling.

When uke posts a hand or sprawls the hips, stop yanking the neck. Switch to Kouchi Gari, step across for Tai Otoshi, or keep chest contact into Osaekomi. Amplitude and back-landing quality still decide Ippon versus Waza-Ari—a knee drop that parks uke on the seat without rotation is not automatically a highlight.

Tsukuri details that survive pressure: the hikite sleeve never goes slack after the knees hit; the hips stay under the falling center; the free leg still contributes drive instead of collapsing into a dead kneel.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

In the Judogi, cloth makes otoshi honest. Sleeve and lapel (or the trapped arm in ippon shape) supply continuous downward vectors that no-gi wrist control only approximates. Competition jackets that are overly long or grips that drift to the belt line change levers—start from standard mid-sleeve and workable lapel height.

No-gi “shoulder drop” keeps the level change and turn but loses friction. Expect more wrestling-like finishes and different success rates. Transfers: the idea of pulling down after a failed standing load. Does not transfer: importing IJF grip-stalling habits into rulesets that punish inactivity differently.

Prefer qualified coaching over video-only learning. Martial Index is a map, not unsupervised curriculum—especially for two-knee drops that demand excellent Ukemi.

What it looks like in practice

In Randori and competition, seoi-otoshi appears when standing seoi stalls against height, stiff posture, or a posting free hand. You see the snap, the fit, then the knees claim the mat as the pull continues. Kata-oriented rooms teach the same mechanics with slower tempo and clearer knee placement—useful for athletes who only ever learned “just drop.”

After landing, rotate into Kesa Gatame or Yoko Shiho Gatame depending on the fall line. The transition concept Seoi to Osaekomi covers pin follow-ups across the seoi family. If the arm is extended on the way down, some athletes hunt Juji Gatame—see also Ippon Seoi to Juji Gatame.

World Tour commentary may still say “drop seoi” over clear otoshi mechanics. Use Martial Index to keep coaching vocabulary sharp even when broadcasters blur names.

Types / variations

One-knee seoi-otoshi

Lead knee down from ippon-seoi or morote shape with continuous pull—the standard teaching progression and the most common randori version.

Two-knee drop otoshi

Both knees contact as tori pulls straight over the shoulder. High commitment and high ukemi demand; progress intensity slowly.

Application from ippon-seoi position

Trap the arm as in Ippon Seoi Nage, then drop and pull—bridges upright one-arm work to mat finishes without inventing a new family.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter only when forward weight already exists—otoshi is not a rescue throw from a sideways tug against a rooted partner. Build the entry in moving Uchi-Komi so the decision to stand or drop is practiced, not improvised under fatigue.

Counters include sprawling the hips back, peeling the sleeve, posting on the back, and circling behind the loading shoulder. Combinations that survive: standing seoi feint → otoshi; otoshi miss → pin; sleeve snap → Deashi Harai if uke steps wide to recover balance.

Against lefty/righty mismatches, adjust which knee leads and which lapel you prefer—do not force a right-handed textbook onto every grip fight.

Compare

Versus Ippon Seoi Nage: standing one-arm lift classic versus knee-contact pull-down. Versus Drop Seoi Nage: Kodokan te-waza name versus modern competition umbrella slang. Versus Morote Seoi Nage: two-hand upright seoi versus otoshi classification. Versus Uki Otoshi: floating hand throw without the seoi back-load. Versus Kata Guruma: shoulder wheel / fireman’s carry family rather than seoi back-load.

IJF notes & safety

Seoi-otoshi is legal classical judo when tori controls direction and uke can take ukemi. Uncontrolled diving somersaults that load the neck are treated as dangerous actions—coach them out under live Judo Rules. This encyclopedia page does not teach illegal diving.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Dropping the knees before creating kuzushi.
  • Arms-only pull while hips stay far from uke.
  • Diving the head as a substitute for hand control.
  • Calling every knee-drop “Korean seoi” or “morote.”
  • Skipping partner communication on two-knee nage-komi.
  • Abandoning chest contact and losing the pin transition.

Training notes

Pair standing seoi and seoi-otoshi in the same training week so athletes feel lift versus pull-down in their hands, not only in vocabulary. Film the sleeve hand after the knees hit—if it goes slack, the throw dies.

Progress two-knee volume only after Ukemi looks honest. Revisit Te-Waza when drop culture starts crowding out other hand techniques like Tai Otoshi and Uki Otoshi.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is seoi-otoshi just drop seoi?

Not exactly—see Drop Seoi Nage for competition slang.

Must the knees touch?

Kodokan distinguishes pull-down with knees grounded at execution from lift-oriented seoi-nage.

How does it compare to ippon-seoi?

See Ippon Seoi Nage for the standing one-arm classic.

Is it te-waza or seoi family?

Kodokan Te-Waza; also nested under Seoi Nage (Judo).

Safe for beginners?

Only after solid Ukemi and with coached intensity.

What pin follows?

Kesa Gatame or Yoko Shiho Gatame.

Related hand throws?

Tai Otoshi and Uki Otoshi.

The Bottom Line

Treat seoi-otoshi as Kodokan pull-down te-waza: knee contact, downward kake, clear kuzushi—and keep Drop Seoi Nage as the competition-language sibling rather than a synonym dump.
