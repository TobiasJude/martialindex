Ne-Waza

Ne-waza is judo’s groundwork system: hold-downs, strangles, and elbow locks used to score ippon by pin time or submission on the tatami.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Osaekomi + shime + kansetsu (elbow).
  • Pins: yuko 5–9s, Waza-Ari 10–19s, Ippon 20s.
  • Throws feed ground entries.
  • Gi loves lapels; no-gi loves Hadaka Jime.
  • Tap early.
  • Start with Osaekomi and Kesa Gatame.

What is ne-waza?

Ne-waza (寝技) is judo groundwork: pins, strangles, and elbow locks once both athletes are on the tatami.

It matters because Ippon still comes from a 20-second pin or a tap—and because BJJ athletes want Kodokan names for skills they already feel.

Katame-waza splits into osaekomi-waza, shime-waza, and kansetsu-waza (elbow only in IJF).

Pair this pillar with Nage-Waza via the judo hub: throws create; groundwork cashes.

How it works

Control precedes submissions. Valid Osaekomi needs uke’s back/upper back down with pressure from a recognised hold.

Under IJF 2026–28 rules, osaekomi scores yuko at 5–9s, waza-ari at 10–19s, and ippon at 20s. Yuko is live again and does not add up to waza-ari; two waza-ari make ippon.

Shime attacks blood/air (Hadaka Jime, Okuri Eri Jime); kansetsu attacks the elbow (Juji Gatame, Ude Garami).

Switch pins when escapes start; do not die on a broken angle.

Judogi vs no-gi

Gi: lapels feed Okuri Eri Jime and sleeve control into armbars.

No-gi: Hadaka Jime and figure-four shoulder locks carry; collar feeds disappear.

BJJ contrast: longer bottom games. Judo’s mate/pin incentives reward efficient holds.

In practice

Throw with Tai Otoshi, land in Kesa Gatame, ride toward Waza-Ari/Ippon while killing the near elbow escape.

If the scarf fails, switch Yoko Shiho Gatame or isolate for Juji Gatame.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter from throws, turtle turns, and failed submissions that recover to pins.

Escapes: bridge-roll, shrimp-frame, trap-leg—drill both roles.

Chains: pin→armbar; choke threat→pin; bent-arm Ude Garami↔straight Juji Gatame.

Compare with nage-waza

Nage-Waza wins by landing; Ne-Waza wins by clock or tap.

IJF notes & safety

Under IJF 2026–28 rules, osaekomi scores yuko at 5–9s, waza-ari at 10–19s, and ippon at 20s. Yuko is live again and does not add up to waza-ari; two waza-ari make ippon.

Elbow kansetsu only in IJF contest judo.

Tap early; never ego-crank necks; keep Ukemi sharp so throw-ins stay safe.

Common mistakes

  • “Pinning” with the back off the mat.
  • Hunting taps without control.
  • Ignoring the pin clock.
  • Skipping escapes practice.

Kodokan groundwork is not “BJJ with a jacket.” The shared techniques are real—juji-gatame is an armbar cousin; hadaka-jime is a rear-naked cousin; ude-garami covers figure-four shoulder locks—but the contest grammar differs. Mate calls, pin clocks, and standing restarts punish slow bottom play that might be normal under other rule sets.

Pinching the basics matters more than collecting rare submissions. If you can hold kesa-gatame and yoko-shiho-gatame, escape both, and threaten juji-gatame, you already have a functional ne-waza floor for club shiai.

From turtle, do not only yank the head. Create wedges, roll through toward chest exposure, and land in a hold the referee can recognise as osaekomi. From a failed throw, inhibit uke’s near elbow and far hip before you invent a new move.

For 2026–28, short pins still matter: a 6-second hold is a yuko, not “nothing.” Athletes who ignore that leave scoreboard value on the mat. Read judo rules and osaekomi together with this pillar.

Safety culture: choke practice needs supervision; joint locks end at the tap, not at the sound you wanted to hear. If your club skips groundwork, you are training half of judo—find partners who will stay down for controlled rounds.

Training notes

Program this topic beside the judo style hub and the live siblings in Related notes. Prefer qualified coaching over video-only learning; use Martial Index pages as maps, not as unsupervised curricula.

If you are building a personal syllabus, pair concept pages with one throw and one ne-waza skill each week, then test in constrained randori. Track whether your ukemi, grips, and pin clock awareness improve—not only whether you “hit” a highlight throw.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Same as BJJ?

Overlapping skills, different incentives.

Pin for ippon?

20 seconds valid Osaekomi.

Leg locks?

Not in standard IJF.

Skip ne-waza as a beginner?

No—learn Kesa Gatame early.

Yuko on the ground?

Yes, 5–9s pins.

Gi chokes first?

Learn both collar and Hadaka Jime ideas.

Throw but can’t hold?

Drill throw→pin in nage-komi/Randori.

Where to start?

Osaekomi, then Yoko Shiho Gatame.

The Bottom Line

Cash out Nage-Waza with honest Osaekomi and clean submissions. Read Kesa Gatame next.
