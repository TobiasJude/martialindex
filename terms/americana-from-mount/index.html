Americana from Mount

The americana from mount is the top americana (keylock / paintbrush) from mount: pin their wrist to the mat at roughly 90°, thread a figure-four under the tricep, and rotate the shoulder by sliding the hand toward their hip while lifting the elbow.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Pin the wrist first; the figure-four is useless if the hand is still swimming.
  • Elbow of your head-side arm stays glued beside their ear so they cannot turn in.
  • Parent: americana. Opposite rotation: kimura.
  • If they hide the elbow, switch to armbar from mount or a triangle from mount.
  • Works gi and no-gi; no-gi just needs cleaner wrist pins.

What Is Americana from Mount?

The americana from mount is many academies’ first mount submission after the armbar. You flatten an arm on the mat in an L, lock a figure-four, and rotate the glenohumeral joint by dragging the knuckles toward their hip (the “paintbrush”) while the elbow lifts.

It belongs to the mount cluster because the flat back is what makes the lock honest: they cannot easily roll away from the rotation the way they can in side control. That is why the side-control cousin needs more setup.

Martial Index keeps the parent americana for theory and this page for the mount path, with a sibling link to americana from side control.

How Americana from Mount Works

From low or technical mount, catch a framing wrist and drive it to the mat. Keep their elbow bent near 90°—too straight becomes a different lock; too tight to the ribs hides the thread.

Place your head-side elbow next to their ear to block the turn-in. Slide your other hand under the tricep and catch your own wrist (monkey grips, thumbs alongside fingers).

Finish slowly: slide their hand toward the hip as if painting the mat, and lift their elbow with your figure-four. Chest stays heavy so a bridge cannot dump you.

If the elbow never leaves the ribs, abandon the americana and isolate for an armbar, S-mount, or triangle rather than muscling a dead angle.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve pins make the initial wrist freeze easier; you can also use their own lapel as a temporary clamp.

No-gi. Head-on-wrist and two-on-one pins replace cloth. Keep hips low—rising to “muscle” the lock invites the upa.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex pins a frame in mount, drops an elbow by the ear, threads the figure-four, and paintbrushes a tap without ever sitting up tall.

Jordan cannot get the elbow off the ribs, so they switch to S-mount and an armbar instead of forcing the keylock.

Types / Variations

Classic paintbrush

Wrist pinned, 90° elbow, slide to the hip, lift the elbow.

High-mount americana

Same lock after you have already climbed; more space to thread, more upa risk if your base is high.

Failed americana to armbar / triangle

The isolation becomes the setup for the next mount attack.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Triggered by a frame on your chest or neck. Also after they defend an armbar by bending the arm back to the mat.

Counters: elbow glued to ribs, turn into the lock, or bridge before the figure-four closes. Escapes late: clasp hands and hide the elbow—tap if the paintbrush is already on.

Americana from Mount vs from Side Control

Factor From mount From side control
Back position Flat—rotation has nowhere to go They can roll away from the lock
Percentage Higher as a direct tap Often a switch / secondary
Base risk Upa if you sit tall Losing the pin if you chase the arm
Read with Armbar / triangle from mount Kimura from side control

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Adult americanas are standard legal shoulder locks. Always verify the current IBJJF and ADCC (or event) rulebooks for division, belt, age, and gi vs no-gi. Martial Index gives orientation—not invented illegal-move lists.

Safety

Shoulder locks injure quickly. Paintbrush slowly in training and release on the tap. Never crank a clasped-hands stalemate.

Common mistakes

  • Sitting upright to finish—inviting the bridge-and-roll.
  • Threading before the wrist is pinned.
  • Straightening their arm into a sloppy shoulder crank.
  • Ignoring the head-side elbow that blocks their turn-in.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Why is the mount americana easier than side control?

Their back is flat, so they cannot rotate away from the paintbrush as easily.

What angle should the arm be?

About 90° at the elbow. Too straight or too tight both kill the lock.

Monkey grip or thumbs?

Monkey grips (thumbs with the fingers) hide peels and stay tighter.

What if they are very flexible?

You still need the wrist pin and elbow lift. Flexibility without space still taps—do not chase a bigger crank.

Gi needed?

No. The lock is no-gi native.

Chain if it fails?

Armbar, triangle from mount, or S-mount. Keep isolating that arm.

Same as a kimura?

Same figure-four family, opposite rotation. Kimura sends the hand behind the back; americana paintbrushes toward the hip/head line.

The Bottom Line

Americana from mount is pin, figure-four, paintbrush: keep your base low and their wrist glued. Read the parent americana and the side-control americana so both platforms stay in one system.
