Double Collar Tie

The double collar tie (plum) puts both hands behind the opponent’s head with elbows tight to break posture and feed clinch knees.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Both hands behind head/crown.
  • Elbows pinch; don’t flare.
  • Break posture down to knee.
  • Child of {thai-clinch}.
  • Never interlace fingers.

What Is Double Collar Tie?

Double collar tie is the technical name for the plum grip inside Thai Clinch (Plum). Both hands control the head; forearms and elbows frame the neck/collarbone line.

It is the parent position for many Clinch Knee sequences.

How Double Collar Tie Works

Swim hands to the head, clasp (palm-to-hand, not fingers laced), elbows in, forehead pressure, pull posture down, hips close, knee.

If hips stay far, they knee you first.

Turn and off-balance when knees are stuffed.

Mechanically, successful double collar tie work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Muay Thai Stance with hands alive.

If Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into elbows or clinch threats you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Kick Check awareness; pocket strikes need Long Guard or tight covers; clinch tools need posture.

Stadium vs Gym / Rules Notes

Under traditional stadium Muay Thai, double collar tie lives inside a full Eight Limbs rule set—elbows, knees, and clinch time change how often you invest in this tool.

Amateur cards, kickboxing crossovers, and some promotions restrict elbows or clinch duration. Gym culture may still drill double collar tie daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit.

Confirm the bout agreement before importing stadium frequency into restricted rules or MMA, where wrestling reframes clinch incentives.

Scoring emphasis varies. Some judges still reward hard kicks and balance; others read more like kickboxing. Let the card’s eyes shape volume.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

From single collar, add the second hand to complete plum.

On the ropes, lock double collar and knee.

They lock you; hand-fight inside and posture up.

Fight picture: double collar tie appears when Range and Timing say so, then yields to the next limb in the Eight Limbs chain.

Gym test: can you apply double collar tie after a Jab feint (or a teep feint) and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Finger interlacing.
  • Wide elbows.
  • Hips away.
  • Holding without knees.
  • Chin up in the pocket.
  • Treating double collar tie as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox double collar tie inside full rounds; return to Muay Thai Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up (punch, kick, or clinch) so double collar tie connects to Eight Limbs.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so Timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether double collar tie appears in the right Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for elbows, flying knees, and catch-heavy games.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe double collar tie as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Range, or a stance that collapses when kicks and clinch enter the round.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put double collar tie behind heavy boxing; others behind teeps and body kicks; stadium-oriented rooms may emphasize clinch delivery. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Teep (Push Kick), Thai Clinch (Plum), and Muay Thai Stance into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for elbows, knees, and catch-kick dumps where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “clinch,” “guard,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Muay Thai entries keep double collar tie scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on double collar tie, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where Timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for double collar tie under your next card (elbows allowed? clinch time? scoring bias?) so training intensity matches the bout, not a generic Instagram Muay Thai.

FAQ

Palm grip vs fingers?

Palm-to-hand or similar—avoid interlaced fingers.

Same as wrestling collar tie?

Related idea; Thai version is optimized for knees/elbows.

Break someone’s plum?

Posture, peel, underhook—drill with a coach.

Elbows from plum?

Possible on angles/exits—see elbow pages.

Sparring intensity?

Control neck carefully; neck injuries are real.
