Ippon Seoi to Juji Gatame

Ippon seoi to juji gatame is a transition concept: finish or scramble from Ippon Seoi Nage into Juji Gatame—not a separate Kodokan throw name, but a high-percentage ne-waza bridge.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Concept under Ippon Seoi Nage.
  • Finish target: Juji Gatame.
  • Needs throw control that leaves the arm attackable.
  • Also compare pin paths via Seoi to Osaekomi.
  • Tap early—armbars punish pride.
  • IJF ne-waza time and scoring literacy matter.

What is Ippon Seoi to Juji Gatame?

Ippon seoi to juji gatame is not a classical throw title; it is a transition concept Martial Index catalogues because athletes search the pathway constantly. Parent technique: Ippon Seoi Nage. Finish: Juji Gatame (ude-hishigi-juji-gatame). The idea: the one-arm seoi already isolates and wraps an arm—when the throw lands incompletely or with the arm extended, the armlock is often closer than a reset.

This sits beside Seoi to Osaekomi, which prioritises pins. Smart athletes choose pin versus juji based on uke’s arm position, chest contact, and clock. Parent throwing culture still lives under Seoi Nage (Judo) and Nage-Waza.

BJJ armbar-from-throw cousins exist; keep BJJ pages labelled BJJ. This entry is judo contest and randori framing on the judo hub.

How it works

Throw with the trapped arm controlled—ippon-seoi’s identity. As uke falls, do not abandon the arm to celebrate a maybe-score. If the back landing is clean, you may already have Ippon and the match ends; transitions matter most on waza-ari landings, incomplete throws, and edge scrambles.

Typical bridge: maintain the arm, step a leg across for juji posture, hips tight, finish the armlock with classical control—or reverse into a pin if the arm slips free but chest contact remains. See Juji Gatame for finish details and Osaekomi for pin clocks.

Common failure: diving off the throw and losing both the score and the arm. Another failure: forcing juji when Kesa Gatame was free money.

Safety: armbars end careers when egos delay taps. Train partners, not highlights.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

Gi sleeves can help or hinder arm isolation. In the Judogi, do not let sleeve gripping become a stall on the ground. No-gi juji from seoi-like entries uses wrist control—same idea, less cloth.

What it looks like in practice

Drill the pathway as a two-step uchi-komi: entry → controlled landing → immediate juji or pin decision. In Randori, reward the correct decision, not only the flashy armlock.

Film whether you still hold the arm one second after impact. If not, you are throwing and hoping.

Types / variations

Clean throw then optional juji

When ippon already likely—transition is bonus, not requirement.

Incomplete seoi into immediate juji

Primary use case—arm extended, back not flat.

Seoi scramble into pin instead

Correct choice when arm escapes—see seoi-to-osaekomi.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter from real ippon-seoi attacks, not from starting already on the ground. Counters for uke: extract the elbow, turn into tori, hide the arm. Combinations: seoi → juji; seoi → yoko-shiho; seoi → ude-garami if the arm bends the other way—see Ude Garami.

IJF notes & safety

Transitions occur inside ne-waza time after a throw attempt. Pins score yuko (5–9s), waza-ari (10–19s), ippon (20s) under 2026–28 rules. Submissions end the contest on tap or referee stoppage. Follow Judo Rules.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Abandoning the arm after the throw.
  • Forcing juji when a pin is free.
  • Cranking without control.
  • Practicing only from perfect seated starts—never from seoi landings.
  • Ignoring uke’s tap culture.

Training notes

Weekly: ten controlled ippon-seoi landings into juji entries at 50% speed. Alternate days into pin entries via seoi-to-osaekomi so decision-making is trained.

Study official juji mechanics on the finish page before inventing crank variations.

Study notes

Decision tree for the landing second: (1) Is the throw already ippon? Stop celebrating and wait for the call. (2) Is the arm extended and trapped? Hunt juji. (3) Is chest contact better than arm isolation? Hunt osaekomi. (4) Did uke turn belly-down? Switch problems—do not force a dead juji.

Beginners often learn juji only from closed-guard or seated drills. Adding seoi-landing starts once a week connects tachi-waza to ne-waza the way contests actually flow.

Referee awareness: do not crank through a partner’s shoulder while waiting for a late waza-ari call. Control first; finish second.

Deep notes

Advanced detail: after a right-handed ippon-seoi, uke’s right arm is often the trapped limb—juji configurations must account for which arm you actually own. Switching arms mid-scramble is how submissions evaporate. Call the arm aloud in training (“right arm!”) until the habit sticks.

Contest psychology: athletes who only chase highlight armlocks give away waza-ari pin opportunities. Build a culture where the correct boring pin is praised as loudly as a flashy juji. That culture wins team events and tight pools.

FAQ

No—concept linking Ippon Seoi Nage to Juji Gatame.

When choose juji over pin?

The Bottom Line

Train ippon-seoi-to-juji as a decision skill: keep the arm, read the landing, finish Juji Gatame or switch to pins via Seoi to Osaekomi.
