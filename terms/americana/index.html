Americana

The americana (also called the keylock or paintbrush) is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu shoulder lock that uses a figure-four grip to rotate the opponent’s arm upward toward their head—most often finished from mount or side control in both gi and no-gi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Americana = figure-four rotating the arm up (paintbrush), usually from top.
  • Slide the elbow toward the hip before you rotate—height kills the finish.
  • Works in gi and no-gi; gi sometimes adds collar help while you isolate.
  • Opposite rotation from the kimura.
  • Legal adult IBJJF; tap early on shoulder pressure.

What Is the Americana?

The americana is a top-position figure-four shoulder lock. You isolate an arm bent at roughly ninety degrees, grip their wrist, complete the figure-four, and rotate their hand toward the mat near their head—like painting the floor—while keeping their elbow pinned. It is closely tied to mount control.

How Americana Works

Pin the wrist low toward their hip line (not up by their ear), complete the figure-four, keep weight on the chest/face line, and rotate. Thumbless finishing grips often appear in instruction to deepen control.

Gi vs No-Gi

Both. Gi may use collar ties to hold posture while you dig the arm. No-gi relies on head pressure and chest weight. The rotation mechanic does not need fabric.

>What Does the Americana Look Like in Practice?

From mount, Jordan isolates Alex’s right arm, slides the elbow down, locks the figure-four, and paints the hand toward the mat until Alex taps.

Types / Variations

From mount

Highest-percentage classroom finish; S-mount variants exist.

From side control

Isolate the near arm carefully so you do not lose the pin.

As a threat

Use the americana to open arms for mount transitions, armbars, or back takes when they defend hard.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Enter when elbows flare from mount/side. Escape by hugging your elbow to your ribs early, bridging, or turning into them—late stages require taps.

Americana vs Kimura

FactorAmericanaKimura
RotationUp toward the headBehind the back
Typical homeMount / side controlGuard / side / turtle
NicknameKeylock / paintbrushKimura / chicken wing

Mount hierarchy. High mount and S-mount make the americana more available because the elbows are farther from the ribs. If you cannot isolate the arm from low mount, improve the mount first—chasing the figure-four from a loose mount is how people get bridged.

Hand fighting. When they clasp hands to defend, you can peel fingers, switch to an armbar threat, or use the americana grip as a control while you advance position. The submission identity remains the upward-rotation shoulder lock; the practical game is often positional.

Naming note. Keylock, americana, and paintbrush refer to the same sport BJJ finish in most gyms. Some wrestling rooms say “keylock” for related figure-fours—confirm visuals when you cross-train.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal adult IBJJF/ADCC. Youth divisions: verify joint-lock rules.

Safety

Tap early. Controlled drilling only—no explosive cranks.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to finish with the elbow too high on the chest.
  • No weight on the opponent while rotating.
  • Abandoning the position entirely when they defend instead of transitioning.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is keylock the same as americana?

In most BJJ gyms, yes—same paintbrush shoulder lock.

Why doesn’t mine finish?

Elbow too high. Slide it toward the hip first.

Americana from guard?

Rare as a primary finish; kimura is the usual bottom figure-four.

Useful at higher belts?

Yes as a threat and occasional finish—especially from strong mount.

Gi needed?

No.

How do I not get bridged off?

Keep weight down and hips low while you isolate.

Compare to kimura?

Opposite rotation. See kimura.

The Bottom Line

The americana is the upward-rotation figure-four shoulder lock from top control. Slide the elbow low, keep weight on them, and use it as both finish and threat—alongside the kimura.
