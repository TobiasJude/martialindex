Wristlock

A wristlock (often called mão de vaca in Portuguese gyms) is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu joint lock that hyperextends or torques the wrist—common from closed guard, mount, and grip fights, with important safety and belt/rules awareness.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Isolate the hand against a surface (body/mat) before folding.
  • Warning time can be short—tap early culture is mandatory.
  • Legal in many rule sets with beginner restrictions—verify.
  • Chains with americana/kimura grips and posted-hand defenses.
  • Concept sibling: wrist control.

What Is Wristlock?

Wristlocks attack the radiocarpal complex by forcing the hand past comfortable flexion, extension, or deviation while the forearm is trapped. In BJJ they are opportunistic: posted hands on the chest, stiff arms in closed guard, and americana-style grips that redirect to the wrist.

They are small-joint submissions: effective, sneaky, and easy to injure with if partners are careless.

How Wristlock Works

Trap the hand so it cannot retract (against your chest, their body, or the mat). Control the forearm line, place pressure on the back of the hand or knuckles, and fold toward the inner forearm or the chosen vector.

Two-point idea. Control near the wrist and somewhere up the forearm so the elbow cannot simply unload the pressure.

Finish gradually—many wrists tap with little travel.

If flexibility eats the wristlock, climb to shoulder locks (kimura/americana) rather than ripping.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve grips create constant wrist-lock opportunities and also defenses.

No-Gi. Posted hands and framing wrists are prime targets; sweat makes retention harder—clamp structure first.

Culture varies by gym: some emphasize them early; others treat them as advanced etiquette weapons.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

From closed guard, Jordan posts a stiff hand on Alex’s chest. Alex clamps the hand, folds the wrist, and taps Jordan before the posture returns.

From side control, an americana grip redirects into a wrist fold when the elbow will not lift.

Types / Variations

Posted-hand / closed-guard

Classic beginner-visible setup against stiff posts.

Americana-to-wrist

Shoulder-lock grips that finish at the wrist when the elbow stays pinned.

Standing / grip-fight wristlocks

Judo-influenced counters when someone overcommits a grip.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: closed guard posts, mount pressure, side-control americana battles, and standing grip fights.

Counters: relax the wrist, rotate toward the thumb escape line, tuck the elbow, and avoid stiff posts.

Escapes: peel early; once folded against a surface, taps come fast.

Wristlock vs Kimura

Factor Wristlock Kimura
Joint Wrist Shoulder
Warning time Often short Usually clearer
Opportunism High (posts/grips) Positional setups
Etiquette Extra care required Standard upper-body lock care

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Wristlocks are commonly legal in adult IBJJF divisions with important restrictions for beginners/juveniles in many rule eras—verify the current IBJJF rulebook for your age and belt. Same caution for other promoters.

Safety

Short warning time. Agree on slow application. Never crank on white belts learning posts. Prior wrist injuries are a hard stop.

Common mistakes

  • Ripping without isolating the hand.
  • Surprising unprepared partners.
  • Ignoring the climb to kimura/americana.
  • Stiff-arm posting that gifts the lock.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is a wristlock in BJJ?

A joint lock folding/torquing the wrist past safe range while the forearm is controlled.

Are they legal?

Often yes for adults with beginner limits—check current rules.

Why do people call them cheap?

Short warning time and surprise factor—etiquette matters more than the label.

Best positions?

Closed guard posts, mount, side control, standing grips.

How do I defend?

Avoid stiff posts; rotate to the thumb; tuck elbows.

Same as wrist control?

No—wrist control is the grip concept; wristlock is the submission.

Gi or no-gi?

Both.

The Bottom Line

Wristlock is the small-joint tax on stiff hands: isolate, fold, tap early. Pair it with wrist control literacy and climb to kimura/americana when wrists are rubbery.
