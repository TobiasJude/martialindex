Stack Pass

The stack pass (often fed by double-unders) is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu pressure pass that folds the bottom player’s legs toward their head, compressing their hips and clearing a path to side control or north-south—effective in gi and no-gi when pressure is angled correctly and the neck is not abused.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Stacking folds their legs toward their head so hips cannot retain guard.
  • Double-unders are the common entry; the pass is the pressure and exit, not just the grips.
  • Works in gi and no-gi; gi pants help you hold the stack, no-gi needs tighter shoulder connection.
  • Safety first: pressure the hips/diaphragm angle—do not crank necks to finish.
  • Compare with knee cut: stack goes under/through pressure; knee cut wedges across a thigh.

What Is the Stack Pass?

The stack pass compresses the bottom player by driving their legs toward their head until the hips can no longer hold a functional guard, then clearing around to a pin. Double-underhooks on the legs are the classic setup, which is why gyms often say “double-under pass” and “stack pass” in the same breath. It sits inside the broader guard passing family.

It is taught early because the pressure is intuitive—and refined later because flexible guards and modern retention make lazy stacks fail.

How the Stack Works

Win under the legs. Both arms (or a strong single-under into stack) control the legs so feet cannot return to your hips easily.

Angle the pressure. Drive shoulder and hips so the load goes into their hips/diaphragm line—not purely into the neck.

Exit. When they cannot hold the shape, step around to side control, north-south, or even a back take if they turn.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Pants grips secure the stack; collar assists can pin a shoulder during the exit.

No-gi. Hold the legs with clamps and shoulder pressure; expect faster hip escapes and more neck-hunt counters (guillotine) if you posture poorly.

Flexibility. Very flexible bottom players can “accept” a shallow stack. Raise the pressure toward the shoulders or change to over-under / knee cut rather than muscling a bad angle.

Closed guard. Stacking is a primary way to open closed guard before other passes begin.

What Does the Stack Look Like in Practice?

From open guard, Jordan shoots double-unders, lifts Alex’s hips, and drives forward into a stack. Alex tries to shrimp; Jordan follows the angle, steps to Alex’s right, and lands north-south before settling side control.

Against a flexible partner, Jordan shifts pressure higher on the shoulders instead of only folding the lower back—then finishes the same exit.

Types / Variations

Double-under stack

Both arms under both legs into the classic fold-and-clear.

Pressure vs flexible guards

Change the shoulder target and hip angle when lumbar flexibility nullifies a low stack.

Stack to back take

When they turn to relieve pressure, chase the back instead of insisting on side control.

Entries, Counters, and Retention

Entries. After opening closed guard; from failed toreando when legs elevate; as a chain from over-under.

Counters. Bottom: shrimp early, frame on shoulders, put feet back on hips, or attack arms/neck when they overcommit. Top: if they recompose, switch to knee cut or body lock rather than forcing a dead stack.

Opening closed guard into a stack. A common white-belt path: open the ankles, shoot double-unders, stack, pass. Blue belts refine the same path with better angles and exits. Do not confuse “I opened the guard” with “I passed”—the stack still has to clear and settle.

When not to stack. If you cannot win under the legs without losing posture, choose toreando or knee cut instead. Forcing double-unders into a seated player’s elevation is how stacks become sweeps.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal. Referees may warn for stalling if you hold a stack without progress, especially in some no-gi settings—advance or reset.

Safety

Do not neck crank to pass. Angle into hips and shoulders. Partners should speak up if cervical pressure feels wrong. Tap early in drilling if breath or neck complains.

Common mistakes

  • Stacking into the neck instead of the hips.
  • No exit plan—holding the fold forever.
  • Shallow double-unders that lose both legs on one shrimp.
  • Ignoring flexible guards instead of changing angle.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is stack pass the same as double-under?

Double-under is the common grip entry; stack describes the pressure and finish. People use the names interchangeably in gyms.

Why doesn’t my stack work on flexible people?

A low lumbar fold is not enough. Drive pressure higher toward the shoulders or switch passes.

Is stacking dangerous?

It can be if you load the neck. Coached correctly—hips/diaphragm angle, controlled exits—it is a standard sport pass. Communicate with partners.

Gi or no-gi—where is it better?

Both. Gi pants help you hold; no-gi rewards tighter shoulder connection and faster exits.

Can I stack from closed guard?

Yes—after you open the ankles and win underhooks on the legs. Opening is part of the job.

What if they put me in a guillotine while I stack?

Posture and head position failed. Free your head before you re-engage the fold, or switch to a safer pass.

Stack or over-under?

Stack often loads both legs symmetrically; over-under splits under/over. Many pressure passers use both. See over-under.

The Bottom Line

The stack pass folds the bottom player’s legs toward their head—usually from double-unders—then clears to a pin. Use hip-and-shoulder pressure, not neck cranks. It complements the knee cut and over-under in a complete pressure-passing game.
