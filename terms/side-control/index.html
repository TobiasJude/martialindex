Side Control

Side control is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu pin where the top player chests the opponent from the side, legs clear of the guard, using crossface and hip control to stop re-guarding—then transitions to mount, knee on belly, north-south, or submissions with different grips in gi versus no-gi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Side control is the default pin after many passes: chest-to-chest from the side, hips heavy.
  • IBJJF scores the pass (3), not the side-control pin itself.
  • Gi adds collar, belt, and baseball-bat attacks; no-gi leans on underhooks, kimuras, and north-south chokes.
  • Primary jobs: kill frames, then climb to mount/KOB or finish.
  • Bottom players escape by reconnecting elbow-knee frames or winning an underhook.

What Is Side Control?

Side control (also called side mount or kesa-adjacent pin in gym slang) is a top position in Brazilian jiu-jitsu where you chest-to-chest the opponent from the side, legs clear of their guard, controlling the near hip and far shoulder so they cannot recover guard or sit up freely. Your torso is roughly perpendicular to theirs; your hips stay heavy and low.

It is the default landing zone after many guard passes. IBJJF does not award points for side control itself; the three points come from the pass that got you there. The position still matters because it is where you kill frames, set knee on belly, climb to mount, rotate to north-south, or finish submissions.

How Side Control Works

Side control works by combining crossface pressure (turning their face away), underhook or hip block on the far side, and chest weight that prevents shrimping back to guard. The near-side elbow and knee of the bottom player are your main threats; if they reconnect elbow to knee, the pass is already dying.

Kill the frames. Bottom players win by inserting a forearm on your hip or neck and bringing the near knee in. Top players win by walking hips toward the head, switching base, or trapping the near arm so those frames never return.

Switch base, do not stall flat. Classic side control, kesa gatame (scarf hold), reverse kesa, and Twister-side / scarf variants all change which arm is free and which submission appears. Static chest pressure without a plan invites the underhook escape Lachlan Giles and others teach from bottom.

Transition map. Far underhook plus head control can take the back if they turn in; knee slide to mount if they flatten; north-south if you walk past the head; knee on belly if you need space or points.

Gi vs No-Gi

Grips. Gi side control adds belt, pants, and far-collar tools. You can pin a sleeve, feed baseball-bat grips, or use the lapel to freeze the near shoulder. No-gi replaces those with underhooks, shoulder pressure, head position, and wrist control.

Submissions. Gi favorites: baseball bat choke, paper cutter, armbar from step-over, kimura on the near or far arm, Ezekiel. No-gi favorites: kimura, armbar, D'Arce / anaconda entries when the head pops up, north-south choke, arm triangle transitions. Collar chokes vanish without the jacket.

Escapes. Same framing logic; gi bottom players can grip the belt or lapel to create frames, while no-gi players use the pillow defense (hand behind own head) and underhook sit-ups more often.

Passing meta. After a gi torreando or knee cut, side control often includes a pants grip that slows their hip escape. In no-gi, body-lock passes and smash passes dump you into a tighter, slipperier pin where chest angle matters more than fabric.

What Does Side Control Look Like in Practice?

Alex knee-cuts through Jordan's open guard and lands chest-to-chest on Jordan's right side. Alex's left arm crossfaces; the right hand blocks the near hip. Jordan tries to shrimp; Alex walks hips toward Jordan's head, clears the near elbow, and loads weight on the face.

Gi. Alex grips the far collar and belt, pops to knee on belly, and threatens a baseball bat choke. When Jordan frames the knee, Alex drops back to side control and steps to mount.

No-gi. Alex cannot hang on a collar. Instead they dig a far underhook, drive the shoulder into the jaw, and when Jordan pushes the head, Alex spins to north-south for a choke or steps over for an armbar on the near arm.

Types / Variations

Standard side control

Chest on chest, hips low, crossface + hip block. The teaching default.

Kesa gatame / scarf hold

Head wrapped, hip tight to their armpit, free leg posted. Strong against bridging; classic kimura and arm attacks.

Reverse kesa / twister side

Facing their legs more than their head. Sets twister entries, near-side armbars, and back takes when they roll.

Knee-on-belly transition

Not a separate pin forever, but the standard mobility tool from side control when you need air, points, or a baseball-bat angle.

North-south rotate

Walking past the head into north-south to reset frames and hunt the north-south choke.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries. Guard passes (knee cut, over-under, torreando, body lock), failed mount escapes that dump you to the side, turtle turnovers, and scramble wins where you clear both legs.

Bottom escapes. Frame the hip and neck, connect elbow to knee, shrimp to half guard or full guard; underhook and sit to single-leg; bridge and turn to knees when they are light. Prevention beats cure: fight the pass before side control settles.

Top counters to escapes. When they frame the hip, walk toward the head or switch to kesa; when they underhook, glue the hip down and threaten mount or the back; when they invert, keep chest pressure and follow the hips.

Side Control vs Mount

Factor Side Control Mount
IBJJF points Pass scores 3; pin itself scores 0 4 points for stabilized mount
Mobility High; easy to KOB / N-S / back Lower once committed on the torso
Pressure style Crossface + hip control Chest + knee posts on hips
Common finishes Kimura, baseball bat, N-S choke Cross choke, arm triangle, armbar
Best use Control after the pass while frames die Score and finish once they are flat

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Side control is legal everywhere. Remember: IBJJF points are for the guard pass, not for lingering in side control. Moving from mount down to side control does not score again. ADCC rewards advancement; camping without submission attempts can be stalling.

Safety

Train under qualified instruction. Crossface pressure should turn the face, not crank the neck laterally for its own sake. Tap early to kimuras and Americana locks from side control.

Common mistakes

  • Chest on them but hips floating, inviting the shrimp.
  • Leaving the near elbow free so they reconnect to guard.
  • In the gi: ignoring belt and collar tools that freeze escapes.
  • In no-gi: reaching for lapels instead of underhooks and shoulder pressure.
  • Holding side control forever without a transition plan.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Do I get points for side control in IBJJF?

No. You get points for passing the guard. Side control is where you often stabilize after the pass, but the pin itself is not a scoring position.

Should I go straight to mount instead of side control?

If frames are dead and you can stabilize mount, yes—especially in IBJJF for the four points. If they still have underhooks and knee frames, side control is the honest place to break those first.

Why does my side control feel light in no-gi?

You are probably relying on gi habits. Drop your hips, use shoulder pressure and a crossface with the bone of the forearm, and dig underhooks instead of looking for collars.

How do I stop them recovering guard?

Block the near hip, keep chest pressure, and never let elbow reconnect to knee. Walk toward the head when they shrimp; switch base when they frame your neck.

Is kesa gatame still useful in modern BJJ?

Yes. It remains a strong control against bridges and a clean kimura platform. Pair it with awareness of their far-side underhook so you do not get reversed.

What submissions should I learn first from side control?

Kimura and Americana for joint locks; in the gi, baseball bat choke; in no-gi, north-south choke and armbar step-overs. Master control before collecting rare tricks.

Side control or knee on belly after the pass?

Knee on belly can add two IBJJF points and create breathing pressure; side control is often stabler for control. Many competitors flash KOB for points, then settle back into side control or mount.

The Bottom Line

Side control is the workhorse pin after the guard pass: kill frames with crossface and hip control, then move to mount, knee on belly, north-south, or a submission. Gi athletes add collar and belt; no-gi athletes smother with underhooks and shoulder pressure. Compare with mount when you are ready to score and finish on the torso.
