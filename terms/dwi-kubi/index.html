Dwi Kubi (Back Stance)

Dwi kubi is the back stance: most weight rear, feet often near L-shaped—compare with Juchum Seogi (Horse Stance) and forward Ap Kubi (Forward Stance).

What Is Dwi Kubi (Back Stance)?

Dwi kubi (뒷굽이) is the back stance emphasizing rear-leg weight and a narrower track than horse stance.

Common for knifehand guarding blocks and certain pattern segments.

Parent cluster: Taekwondo Stance.

Compare with Juchum Seogi (Horse Stance): horse stance is wider and more even-weighted.

Martial Index files Dwi Kubi (Back Stance) under Taekwondo so searchers get definition, mechanics, WT/ITF context, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Taekwondo style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Dwi Kubi (Back Stance) behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Dwi Kubi (Back Stance) Works

Place feet roughly perpendicular; bend the rear knee; keep front leg lighter; torso upright; eyes forward.

Knees track safely—no collapsing inward.

Settle the stance before completing the hand technique in Poomsae.

When moving, preserve the weight idea rather than accidentally becoming a forward stance.

Mechanically, successful dwi kubi (back stance) work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, dwi kubi (back stance) is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill dwi kubi (back stance) daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Pattern: knifehand guarding block in back stance—judges watch rear-knee bend and foot angle.

Drill: alternate Ap Kubi (Forward Stance) punches with Dwi Kubi (Back Stance) blocks down the floor.

Fight picture: dwi kubi (back stance) appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply dwi kubi (back stance) after a feint and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Even weight that turns it into a weird horse.
  • Front heel lifting without standard.
  • Feet on a single tightrope line (unstable).
  • Leaning back excessively.
  • Rushing hand techniques before the stance lands.
  • Treating dwi kubi (back stance) as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowkick dwi kubi (back stance) inside full rounds; return to Taekwondo Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so dwi kubi (back stance) connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether dwi kubi (back stance) appears in the right range band for Kyorugi (Sparring) or forms context.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for spinning kicks, axe kicks, and PSS-targeted Palding (Trunk Scoring) work.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe dwi kubi (back stance) as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor distance, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put dwi kubi (back stance) behind heavy Olympic pressure kicking; others behind traditional Poomsae precision. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.
