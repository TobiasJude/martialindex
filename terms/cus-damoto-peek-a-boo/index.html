What Is Cus D’Amato Peek-a-Boo?

Cus D'Amato peek-a-boo names the training system Cus D'Amato built—popularly associated with fighters like Mike Tyson—where the Peek-a-Boo guard (gloves high beside the face) pairs with a deep crouch, pendulum footwork, constant Head Movement, and explosive short-range punching.

Martial Index keeps the inventory slug cus-damoto-peek-a-boo (typo damoto) for catalog stability, but the visible title is Cus D'Amato Peek-a-Boo.

Parent entry Peek-a-Boo defines the guard shape; this child explains system intent, history, and how it differs from a generic Earmuffs Guard or High Guard (Boxing).

How Cus D’Amato Peek-a-Boo Works

Crouch lower than a tall orthodox pose; gloves at cheekbone/ear height (Earmuffs Guard picture); elbows glued; chin buried (Chin Tuck).

Head never parks—Slip, bob, and weave create angles while the legs stay spring-loaded for hooks and uppercuts from below the opponent's eyeline.

Offense explodes from the crouch into close Combination work; defense is active, not a passive helmet (Active Guard over Passive Guard).

When the pocket breaks or you are tall/long, you may stretch back toward classic Boxing Guard—peek-a-boo is a system choice, not a mandatory forever stance.

Mechanically, successful cus d’amato peek-a-boo work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair this tool with the rest of the defensive layer: Slip/Parry for straights, Bob and Weave literacy for hooks, and honest Guard Hand Position always.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, cus d’amato peek-a-boo lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows as weapons—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill cus d’amato peek-a-boo daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Amateur scoring and energy cost: constant crouch and head movement gas fighters; coaches periodize intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Slip-bag rounds in a peek-a-boo gym: rhythm slips with hands glued, then a burst of hooks—compare to a tall high-guard boxer who jabs at range.

Fight picture: cus d’amato peek-a-boo appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply cus d’amato peek-a-boo after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Copying Tyson's gloves without the footwork or chin tuck.
  • Standing tall and calling it peek-a-boo.
  • Passive earmuffs with no head movement.
  • Ignoring the parent Peek-a-Boo page.
  • Treating cus d’amato peek-a-boo as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Coach-led only for intensity: slip bag, bob-weave rope, short-burst mitt work from crouch.

Always keep a path back to safe Boxing Guard recovery.

Shadowbox cus d’amato peek-a-boo inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so cus d’amato peek-a-boo connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether cus d’amato peek-a-boo appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shells, high/low guard switches, and counters off the roll.
