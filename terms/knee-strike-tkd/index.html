What Is Knee Strike (Mureup)?

Mureup (무릎) is the knee. A strike (chigi) drives the knee upward or forward into a target.

Taegeuk chil jang and other Poomsae include a knee strike, often followed by uppercuts or pulls.

WT Kyorugi (Sparring) is not a knee-clinch sport. Do not paste eight-limbs knee culture onto Olympic footage.

ITF Taekwondo and self-defense curricula keep knees alive as close-range tools beside Elbow Strike (Palkup).

Martial Index files Knee Strike (Mureup) under Taekwondo so searchers get definition, mechanics, WT/ITF context, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Taekwondo style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Knee Strike (Mureup) behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Knee Strike (Mureup) Works

Pull the opponent’s head or shoulders (in self-defense pictures) or strike a held target; the hip lifts the knee on a line.

Foot is relaxed or pointed per school; the weapon is the kneecap/upper shin junction, not the toes.

Hands either pull or guard. A knee with both arms dead is a hop.

Plant back into Taekwondo Stance. A knee that becomes a fall is not a technique.

Mechanically, successful knee strike (mureup) work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, knee strike (mureup) is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill knee strike (mureup) daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Poomsae: pull, knee, double uppercut.

Shield: one step, knee, recover—no clinch wrestling unless the class is actually teaching it.

Fight picture: knee strike (mureup) appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply knee strike (mureup) after a Feint Kick and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to Kick Combination work instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Upward knee

Classic lift into midsection or face-line in self-defense pictures.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives sparring pressure, not only pad stations.

Common Mistakes

  • Kicking with the toes and calling it a knee.
  • Leaning so far you fall.
  • Using knees in WT as a strategy.
  • No hands.
  • Confusing this with {ap-chagi} because both start with a knee lift.
  • Treating knee strike (mureup) as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowkick knee strike (mureup) inside full rounds; return to Taekwondo Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so knee strike (mureup) connects to a Kick Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so Counter Kick timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether knee strike (mureup) appears in the right range band for Kyorugi (Sparring) or forms context.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for spinning kicks, aerials, and PSS-targeted Palding (Trunk Scoring) or Head Kick (Olgul Chagi) work.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe knee strike (mureup) as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor distance, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put knee strike (mureup) behind heavy Olympic pressure kicking; others behind traditional Poomsae precision. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Taekwondo Stance, Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick), and Kyorugi (Sparring) into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for spinning kicks and head-height targets where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “roundhouse,” “cut kick,” “hook,” and “feint.” Martial Index’s Taekwondo entries keep knee strike (mureup) scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Muay Thai or Boxing articles that share casual English labels.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on knee strike (mureup), one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for knee strike (mureup) under your next card (WT vs ITF, PSS vs paper scoring, headgear) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Mureup vs ap chagi?

Ap Chagi (Front Kick) extends the foot. A knee strike stops at the knee.

Mureup vs elbow?

Elbow Strike (Palkup) is the upper close-range tool; the knee is the lower.

Olympic?

Not a kyorugi scoring identity.

ITF?

More likely to appear in self-defense and some sparring rulesets.

Safe training?

Pads and control. Knees injure partners quickly.

The Bottom Line

Keep the knee as a close-range lift—do not import a full clinch game your rules never promised.
