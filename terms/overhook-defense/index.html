Overhook Defense

Overhook defense is the Whizzer-family answer to an underhook: clamp the overhook, hip in, and either stall the drive, hit knees, or circle off the fence.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Clamp above the elbow and hip in.
  • Primary job is killing the underhook drive.
  • On the fence, buy time to frame and circle.
  • Not a forever hold—exit or reverse.
  • Read the Batch 1 {whizzer} pillar.

How Overhook Defense Works

On the fence, overhooks stop body-lock connections long enough to Frame and Circle or reverse with an underhook hike.

Overhooks are not forever holds—gas and strike exposure rise if you never separate or reverse.

Mechanically, successful overhook defense work is a chain: establish grip or angle → remove a base or frame → apply the scoring action → recover posture with hands alive.

If distance or fence angle is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close without a pin and you collide into frames, whizzers, or submission threats.

Pair with Clinch Break when you have already scored or when judges reward open fighting.

Common Mistakes

  • Soft overhook at the wrist.
  • Hips out (they run the pipe).
  • Holding forever while eating short hooks.
  • Forgetting to hand-fight the free side.
  • Treating overhook defense as a highlight instead of a position-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down or hips light.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe overhook defense as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor fence awareness, or grips that collapse when strikes enter the round.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put overhook defense behind heavy wrestling; others behind dirty boxing; submission-heavy rooms may leave GNP light. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.
