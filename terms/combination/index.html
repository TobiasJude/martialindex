Combination

A combination strings punches together so each shot sets the next—while balance, guard, and an exit stay intact.

  • Purpose + balance + exit.
  • One-two is the seed.

What Is Combination?

Parent of One-Two. Uses Jab, Cross, Hook, Uppercut, Feint, and Boxing Footwork.

Martial Index files Combination under Boxing so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Boxing style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Combination behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Combination Works

Choose a purpose (open the guard, move them back, set a hook). Throw 2–5 punches with weight transfer that leaves you able to defend. End with a step-out, Pivot, or cover—don’t pose.

Mechanically, successful combination work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: straights need Slip/Parry awareness; hooks need Bob and Weave literacy; power shots need a clean Power Line.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, combination lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill combination daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

1-2-3-2. Double jab into cross. Body-body-head. Feint then 1-2.

Fight picture: combination appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply combination after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Furrying with chin up.
  • No exit.
  • Only head hunting.
  • Treating combination as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox combination inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so combination connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether combination appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shell defenses, check hooks, and pull counters.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe combination as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Boxing Range, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put combination behind heavy boxing pressure; others behind movement and counters. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Boxing Stance, Jab, and Slip into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for head movement and power punching where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “pressure,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Boxing entries keep combination scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on combination, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for combination under your next card (glove size, round length, amateur vs pro judging) so training intensity matches the bout.

Related reading inside this batch should feel like a cluster, not a random walk: return to Boxing Stance when balance fails, and to Boxing Range when techniques “stop working” for mysterious reasons.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

How long should a combo be?

Long enough to achieve the purpose—often 2–4 punches.

What’s the first combo to learn?

The One-Two.

Do combinations need feints?

Not always, but feints multiply their success rate.

The Bottom Line

Combine with intent—every punch should either score, set, or free you to leave.
