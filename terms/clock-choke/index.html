Clock Choke

The clock choke attacks turtle from the top with a far-collar feed and rib pressure, then walks the legs around like clock hands to finish—a gi-only cousin of the loop choke.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Feed the far collar across the throat, put ribs on the neck, walk the circle.
  • Gi-only. Primary punishment for static turtle.
  • Control a wrist or second lapel to break their post.
  • Bottom answers: turtle escape, granby, hand-fight collars early.
  • Related to sliding lapel chokes from the back.

What Is Clock Choke?

The clock choke is the standard gi answer when someone turtles: collar across the neck, weight on the back of the head/neck, circular walk that tightens the strangle—named for the clock-hand path of the legs.

It sits with turtle/back attacks and the collar-choke family, opposite turtle-escape and granby on the defensive side.

How Clock Choke Works

Open the far lapel path, feed the choking hand thumb-in across the throat, shift ribs onto the back of their neck, post the head if needed, and walk the legs around while pulling. Optional: control their far wrist to collapse the shell.

Bottom. Fight the collar immediately, keep chin position, and exit turtle—do not wait for the walk.

Finish feel. Many tap before a full circle if ribs and collar are correct.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi — required.

No-Gi. Use seatbelt back takes, front headlocks, and bodylocks instead of clock mechanics.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Opponent turtles after a pass: feed far lapel, drop ribs, walk clockwise/counterclockwise until the choke finishes—or they expose hooks for a back take.

Types / Variations

Basic clock choke

Collar feed + ribs + walk.

Wrist-control clock

Pull the far wrist to break the post before walking.

Sliding lapel variant

Second lapel as backstop—advanced teaching detail.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: turtle after guard pass, sprawled turtles, failed back takes that leave collars free.

Counters: peel collar, granby/roll to guard, sit-out, stand, or tight shell with active exits.

If the clock fails, insert hooks and switch to bow-and-arrow or RNC (no-gi).

Clock Choke vs Loop Choke

Factor Clock Choke Loop Choke
Position Top turtle Often bottom / transitional
Finish motion Circular walk Head pull + curl
Gi-only? Yes Yes
Defensive twin Turtle escape Posture / collar peel

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal in IBJJF gi. Not applicable in no-gi. Turtle itself scores nothing—finishes and back takes do.

Safety

Ribs on neck can be intense—control the walk in training. Tap early.

Common mistakes

  • Walking without a deep enough collar.
  • Staying on their back instead of the neck line.
  • Ignoring the free hand that posts and stalls the choke.
  • Letting them clear the collar mid-walk without a backup back take.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is the clock choke?

A gi choke from top turtle finished by walking the legs in a circle while a collar cuts the neck.

Why is it called clock?

The walking path of the legs resembles clock hands.

Is it gi-only?

Yes.

How do I escape it?

Peel the collar early and execute a turtle escape—do not freeze.

Clock vs bow and arrow?

Clock is usually top turtle; bow and arrow is typically back control. See bow and arrow.

Can I take the back instead?

Yes—if hooks appear mid-clock, switching to back control is often better.

No-gi turtle attack?

Seatbelt and front headlock families—not clock.

The Bottom Line

Clock choke is turtle’s gi tax: deep collar, ribs on the neck, walk the circle. If they escape the collar, take the back—do not invent a no-gi clock.
