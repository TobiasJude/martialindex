Posture Break

What Is Posture Break?

Posture Break is the closed-guard prerequisite of collapsing the top player's upright base—usually via collar/sleeve pulls and leg curls—so sweeps and submissions become available. In gym language you may also hear breaking posture / pulling them down. Train it as a named pattern with clear entries and exits—not a vague vibe.

How It Works

Lock closed guard, establish collar and/or sleeve grips, crunch knees to chest while pulling elbows, and buckle the opponent's spine forward until hands post on the mat. Control beats speed: establish the grips or frames that make the next decision obvious.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi vs no-gi notes. Collar and sleeve grips make posture breaks easier; no-gi uses head control, overhooks, and two-on-one wrist control.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

In live training, posture break usually appears as a short window: you recognize the grips/angles, commit the mechanics above, and either finish the position change or chain to a sibling technique when they defend.

Types / Variations

Primary pattern

Double-collar buckle when both hands are low.

Competition variation

Strip the high post first when they play one-high/one-low.

Related option

Chain immediately into scissor, flower, triangle, or armbar once posture dies.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries come from the parent position(s) (Closed Guard). Counters include framing early, removing the key grip, and forcing a different cluster (for example recovering guard or changing the pin). Escapes prioritize creating space before the structure fully sets.

How It Compares

Opening move under closed guard before sweeps and chokes.

Special Considerations

Rules & sport context

Generally legal in BJJ; check IBJJF Rules and ADCC Rules for crank/reaping/slam nuances when relevant to this pattern (especially neck, spinal, and knee pressure). Verify the current rulebook for your event—IBJJF and ADCC diverge on several finishes.

Safety

Communicate, tap early, and avoid ballistic force on the neck, shoulders, and knees. This page is reference—not live coaching. Neck-crank-adjacent patterns (can-opener family) stay conceptual: prioritize blood chokes and positional control over spinal torque.

Common mistakes

Rushing without grips; stalling without a next move; confusing posture break with a nearby sibling name.
