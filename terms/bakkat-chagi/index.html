What Is Bakkat Chagi (Outside Crescent)?

Bakkat chagi (바깥차기) refers to outside crescent-style kicking used in basics and some Poomsae applications.

The foot travels on a circular path rather than the tight turning-kick chamber of Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick).

Compare with An Chagi (Inside Crescent): inside crescent mirrors the arc direction.

Sport Kyorugi (Sparring) uses crescents less than cutters and turning kicks, but they train hip mobility and timing.

How Bakkat Chagi (Outside Crescent) Works

Lift the leg and arc the foot across the target line, striking typically with the sole or edge per coaching, then recover.

Keep the base foot stable; do not spin off the line unintentionally.

Eyes stay on the target; arms frame.

In forms, match the exact height and facing your pattern requires.

Mechanically, successful bakkat chagi (outside crescent) work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, bakkat chagi (outside crescent) is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill bakkat chagi (outside crescent) daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Basics line: alternating Bakkat Chagi (Outside Crescent) and An Chagi (Inside Crescent) on a mid pad.

Self-defense drill: crescent to clear a guard hand then follow with a hand technique (school-dependent).

Fight picture: bakkat chagi (outside crescent) appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply bakkat chagi (outside crescent) after a feint and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Muscling a huge circular swing that telegraphs.
  • Hitting with the ankle bone carelessly.
  • Confusing crescent with turning kick mechanics.
  • Losing balance on the base leg.
  • Mixing up inside vs outside naming across schools.
  • Treating bakkat chagi (outside crescent) as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Bakkat vs an chagi?

Opposite crescent directions—see An Chagi (Inside Crescent).

Is crescent a roundhouse?

No—different path and coaching intent than Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick).

Used in Olympics?

Rare as a primary scorer compared with sport staples.

Sole or blade?

School-dependent; ask for test standards.

Why practice them?

Hip mobility, coordination, and pattern literacy.
