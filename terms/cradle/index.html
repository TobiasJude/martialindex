Cradle

A cradle locks the head toward a knee—near-side or far-side—to turn the opponent for near falls and pins.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Cradle locks head to knee for turns and pins.
  • Folkstyle near-fall scoring loves cradles.
  • Near-side and far-side are both required literacy.
  • Break posture before you reach.
  • Hold for counts when the pin is slow.

What Is Cradle?

The cradle is among folkstyle's highest-percentage pinning combinations: bring head and knee together, lock, and turn.

It thrives in Folkstyle Wrestling because Near Fall points reward the exposure even when the pin is not finished.

Cradles appear after scrambles, Front Headlock (Wrestling) transitions, and breakdowns from top.

In Wrestling, freestyle uses related compression turns, but the cradle language is most at home in American folkstyle rooms.

Read this beside the Wrestling style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Cradle behaves across freestyle, folkstyle, and Greco-Roman contexts.

How Cradle Works

Drive the opponent's head toward the near or far knee, lace your arms to lock the ball, and pull them onto their back while keeping chest pressure.

Near-side cradles attack the near knee; far-side cradles reach across to the far knee—both require hip position and timing.

Once locked, run the pin angle and hold for near-fall counts if the fall is not immediate.

If the lock fails, do not release wildly—transition to Half Nelson or re-breakdown.

Mechanically, successful cradle work is usually a chain: Wrestling Stance integrity → Level Change → entry or control → finish or recovery.

If stance collapses or the Penetration Step stalls short, even textbook grips fail—too high and you meet a Sprawl (Wrestling); too loose and you invite a Whizzer or scramble.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: shots need sprawl literacy; ties need hand-fighting; pins need Near Fall awareness in folkstyle.

Rules / Style Notes

Under United World Wrestling freestyle rules, cradle is judged through takedowns, exposure, and activity—not riding time.

Under NCAA/NFHS folkstyle, control, escapes, reversals, and near falls reshape how cradle is valued once the wrestlers hit the mat.

Greco-Roman bans holds below the waist, so leg-attack cousins of cradle either disappear or migrate into upper-body variants.

Confirm the rule set before importing gym habits into contest intensity—folkstyle riding does not equal freestyle par terre.

Potentially dangerous compression can be stopped—listen to the referee.

Know current near-fall timing charts for NFHS/NCAA.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Askren cradle series teaching: find cradles from multiple starts.

Top ride: crossface, force the knee, lock cradle, turn.

Match picture: cradle appears when stance, ties, and timing say so, then yields to a finish, a Go-Behind, or a reset to neutral.

Room test: can you apply cradle after a Collar Tie or Russian Tie exchange and still recover posture? If not, chain it instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Near-side cradle

Lock head to near knee—common from top breakdowns.

Drill this variation with live motion—circle, change levels, and reset—so it survives hand-fighting pressure, not only cooperative reps.

Far-side cradle

Common Mistakes

  • Reaching for the cradle without breaking posture first.
  • Locking soft—elbows not tight.
  • Stopping at the lock without turning.
  • Giving up on near-fall counts when the pin is slow.
  • Treating cradle as a highlight instead of a stance-and-timing skill.
  • Shooting or turning without a recovery plan when the opponent sprawls or bridges.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadow-wrestle cradle inside full rounds; return to Wrestling Stance after every attempt.

Partner drills: technical → moving → forced follow-up so cradle connects to a chain (shot → finish, or ride → turn).

Live situational wrestling beats drill-only fluency; feed realistic hand fights so timing is part of the skill.

Film practice weekly and ask whether cradle appears with a real Level Change and clean hip position.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for throws, front headlocks, and pinning combinations.

Cradle turning drills from belly and from turtle.

Live top: only cradle attacks score for two minutes.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe cradle as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under hand-fighting stress, a collapsed Wrestling Stance, or a Level Change that never gets under the opponent’s hips.

When you compare rooms, you will see stylistic accents: freestyle rooms push exposure chains; folkstyle rooms push ride-and-turn; Greco rooms push upper-body locks. The encyclopedia definition of cradle stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Freestyle Wrestling, Folkstyle Wrestling, and Wrestling Stance into siblings, then return here after live rounds to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for throws, neck ties, and pinning combinations where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “clinch,” “sprawl,” and “single.” Martial Index’s Wrestling entries keep cradle scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language—compare pages are labeled when useful.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on cradle, one situational live theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing or hip position failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for cradle under your next event (UWW freestyle, NCAA folkstyle, or Greco) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Near-side vs far-side?

Which knee you lock to the head—near or far. Both score when they expose the back.

Cradle vs half nelson?

Cradle compresses head-to-knee; Half Nelson turns via head/arm lever from top.

Freestyle cradle?

Related turns exist; folkstyle near-fall culture makes cradles especially decisive in the U.S.

How do I defend?

Keep elbows in, do not let head and knee meet, fight hands, and explode to base.

From front headlock?

Yes—common transition when head and knee close.

Points?

Near-fall scoring when exposure criteria are met—see Near Fall.

The Bottom Line

Hunt cradles on purpose—force head and knee together, lock tight, and cash near falls even when the referee's hand is slow on the fall.
