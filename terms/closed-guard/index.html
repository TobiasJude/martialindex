Closed Guard

Closed guard is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu position in which the bottom player lies on their back, wraps both legs around the opponent’s waist, and crosses the ankles to control distance and posture—with different grips, threats, and incentives in gi versus no-gi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 20 Sep 2026

If they post tall — shorten the spine. If they rise — attack the rise or leave. Jacket: collar-sleeve. Rashguard: overhook / wrist. Leave when you can re-close on purpose.

    The entry

    Key Takeaways

    • Closed guard (full guard) keeps the bottom player’s legs locked around the top player’s waist.
    • Primary jobs: control distance, break posture, create angles for sweeps and submissions.
    • Gi games lean on collar-and-sleeve, pants, lapels, and loop chokes; no-gi leans on overhooks, wrists, head control, and high-elbow / Williams structures.
    • Classic attacks include armbar, triangle, and kimura; classic sweeps include scissor and pendulum.
    • The leg position works in gi and no-gi; permitted grips, guard-pull rules, and scoring depend on the format.

    What Is Closed Guard?

    Closed guard is one of the foundational bottom positions in Brazilian jiu-jitsu (BJJ). The practitioner on the bottom is on their back. Their hips face the opponent. Both legs wrap around the opponent’s waist so that the ankles cross behind the opponent’s back. In common gym language this is also called full guard.

    The position sits in the larger family of guard games: bottom positions that use the legs to manage the top player. When the ankles are no longer locked around the waist and the feet work from the outside—on the hips, biceps, or floor—the position is usually classified as open guard rather than closed guard—see the parent entry on guard.

    Closed guard is commonly taught as a way to connect posture control, hip movement, sweeps, and submissions. It is one part of a broader guard game in both gi and no-gi. The order in which beginners learn guard positions varies by school and curriculum.

    How Closed Guard Works

    Mechanically, closed guard works by limiting the top player’s hips and upper-body posture. The crossed ankles and squeezing thighs make it harder for the top player to stand freely, back away, or drop their weight into a clean pass. The bottom player’s hands and frames then attack the opponent’s head, neck, sleeves, wrists, or elbows—depending on gi or no-gi—to pull the posture down or create an angle.

    Distance control

    Distance control is the first layer. Crossed ankles and squeezing thighs make it harder for the top player to stand freely, back away, or drop into a clean pass. A lock around the waist emphasizes hip connection; climbing higher changes the control into a high-guard configuration. If the top player sits tall with a straight spine and heavy base, submission angles shrink. If the bottom player can pull the head down, flatten the posture, or hip-escape (shrimp) to create space on one side, the legs can open briefly to climb for a triangle, swing for an armbar, or set a sweep.

    Posture breaking

    Posture breaking is the second layer. Pull with the legs first—a reverse crunch / knee-pull toward your chest—not just with the arms. In the gi, collar ties and sleeve grips are the default handles once the legs have stolen posture. In no-gi, overhooks, underhooks, wrist control, and head position replace fabric. All of them serve the same strategic goal: keep the top player from stacking, standing into a pass, or planting both hands on the mat to pin the hips. Once posture is broken, classic finishes become available—especially the armbar, triangle choke, and kimura.

    Hip angle

    Hip angle is the third layer. Attacking square against a strong passer is a losing bet. A small shrimp to one side puts your knee and a hand on different sides of their centerline, changes the line of the spine, opens the path for a triangle climb or armbar pivot, and makes the scissor / pendulum family viable. Flat hips plus locked ankles without an angle is how closed guard becomes a rest position for the passer.

    Gi vs No-Gi

    The defining leg position stays the same in gi and no-gi. Fabric grips change how athletes control posture and connect attacks.

    Grips and upper-body control. Gi closed guard is built on collar-and-sleeve. You can break posture by climbing the collar, pinning a sleeve to the mat, and combining both with the knee-pull. No-gi replaces those with overhook / wrist / head control. High-elbow closed guard and Williams / high-guard configurations show up more often when there is no jacket to hang from.

    Pants and hip control. In the gi, a pants grip helps manage the top player’s near hip when you open briefly or when they try to stand. Without pants, hip control lives in the thighs, the overhook, and how hard you clamp when they post.

    Passing. In both formats, the top player generally needs to open the legs before completing a guard pass. Standing and kneeling openings are both possible. Fabric grips can change the available controls, but neither format guarantees a faster or slower exchange.

    Gi-only offense. Lapel stuffing, worm-adjacent entries, and loop chokes need a jacket. They are real closed-guard and guard-adjacent weapons in IBJJF gi—and irrelevant in no-gi.

    Rubber guard. Rubber guard exists in both, but the 10th Planet system is no-gi native: the idea is to replace missing collar grips with extreme hip flexibility and overhooks. Gi athletes can borrow pieces; the full system is built for rashguards.

    Triangle and armbar mechanics. Same skeletal motion, different handles. Sleeve grips make it easier to isolate an arm for the triangle or keep the wrist for the armbar. Without sleeves, you pin the wrist with bare hands, use head position, or climb with an overhook already in place.

    Learning progression. Learn how closed guard connects to open guard rather than treating one as a graduation from the other. Your coach can choose a sequence that fits the class and your current movement skills.

    What Does Closed Guard Look Like in Practice?

    Two athletes are rolling in a sport BJJ class. After a scramble, Alex ends on their back with Jordan kneeling between their legs. Alex crosses their ankles behind Jordan’s lower back—closed guard. Jordan posts both hands on Alex’s biceps and tries to sit upright.

    Gi version. Alex establishes collar-and-sleeve, pulls Jordan’s posture down with a collar grip plus a knee-pull, then hip-escapes slightly to the left. When Jordan reaches to defend the head, Alex opens the guard, throws their right leg over Jordan’s head, and extends for an armbar on Jordan’s left arm. Jordan defends by stacking. Alex abandons the armbar, re-closes the guard, and switches to a scissor sweep: sleeve and collar, knee shield across Jordan’s chest, and a scissoring motion of the legs that puts Alex on top in mount.

    No-gi version. Same scramble, no fabric. Alex cannot hang on a collar. Instead they overhook Jordan’s right arm, control the left wrist, and knee-pull to break posture. The armbar still exists, but the entry often comes from the overhook side or a high-elbow climb rather than a sleeve drag. If Jordan stands quickly, Alex may open to a hip frame or half guard instead of muscling a closed lock that is already compromised.

    Both sequences show the same definition: closed guard controlled distance, broke posture, threatened a submission, then converted a failed finish into a better position—or a smarter transition when the jacket was not there to help.

    Types / Variations

    Closed guard itself is a specific configuration—legs around the waist—but athletes often name the offensive systems they run from it.

    Armbar from closed guard

    Isolate an arm, open the legs, pivot the hips, and bring a leg over the opponent’s head while controlling the wrist and extending the hips. In the gi, sleeve control makes the isolation cleaner. In no-gi, wrist control and an overhook often replace the sleeve. Legal in IBJJF sport competition across typical adult divisions when applied as a standard joint lock.

    Triangle choke from closed guard

    The bottom player forms a figure-four with their legs around the opponent’s neck and one arm, cutting blood flow through the carotids. Entries often start by pulling posture down and controlling a sleeve (gi) or wrist (no-gi) so one arm is trapped inside. Climbing high on the shoulders matters more than “crossing the ankles harder.”

    Kimura from closed guard

    A shoulder lock that isolates an arm with a figure-four grip, often after the opponent posts a hand on the mat. The kimura can finish or force a defensive reaction that opens a sweep. Works in both gi and no-gi; the gi sometimes adds a sleeve assist before the figure-four closes.

    Scissor sweep and pendulum sweep

    The scissor sweep uses a knee across the chest and a scissoring leg action to reverse the top player. The pendulum (or flower) sweep swings the legs and hips in a pendulum arc, commonly finishing in mount. Both are standard closed-guard sweeps in gi training and remain relevant in no-gi with adjusted grips—collar/sleeve become head/wrist or underhook variants.

    High guard, Williams, rubber guard

    High-elbow closed guard and Williams guard climb the shoulders and isolate an arm for triangles and armbars—especially useful in no-gi when collar grips are absent. Rubber guard uses extreme hip flexibility and an overhook to pin the posture; it appears in both, but the 10th Planet curriculum is built for no-gi. Treat these as branches of closed guard, not separate parent concepts.

    Entries, Counters, and Passes

    Entries. Closed guard often appears after a guard pull, a failed pass that leaves the top player between the legs, or a scramble where the bottom player wins the race to lock the ankles. In the gi, pulling closed guard is a deliberate first move for many competitors. In no-gi, the same lock more often appears as a recovery when an open configuration collapses.

    Counters from the bottom. When the passer postures, answer with the knee-pull plus a grip (collar-sleeve or overhook/wrist). When they stand, decide quickly: open to a hip frame, attack a trip/sweep, or transition to half / open rather than clinging. When they stack, shrimp out the side instead of fighting the stack head-on.

    Common passes against closed guard. Standing double-unders or stack passes; knee-cut after forcing an opening; back-step / long-step once one knee splits the thighs; in the gi, sleeve-and-collar control that freezes your hips before the pass. Knowing these patterns tells you when closed guard has already lost and open or half is the honest next step.

    Closed Guard vs Open Guard

    Closed and open guard provide different ways to manage distance. Athletes often move between them during one exchange.

    Factor Closed Guard Open Guard
    Connection Legs locked around the waist; high connection, low mobility Feet free on hips, biceps, or floor; lower lock, higher mobility
    Best when You need a home base, posture break, or recovery after a scramble You need range, hooks, and modern passing resistance
    Gi bias Collar-sleeve and pants make closed guard sticky and beginner-friendly Sleeve and lapel grips enable spider, lasso, and other open-guard controls
    No-gi bias Uses body grips, wrist control, and arm ties instead of fabric Seated, butterfly, and leg-entanglement guards offer other connections
    Failure mode Static shell → stack or standing pass Loose frames → smash pass or leg entanglement fights

    Opening the ankles deliberately is part of many attacks. The useful question is whether you have another connection or frame ready when you open, not whether one guard is always better.

    Special Considerations

    IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

    Closed guard is a standard, fully legal position in IBJJF Brazilian jiu-jitsu. Submissions commonly launched from it—armbar, triangle, kimura—are part of the normal adult sport toolkit. Pulling closed guard and working from it remains culturally normal in IBJJF gi.

    Guard-pull and passivity rules differ between events and divisions. Consult the current IBJJF rules resources or ADCC rules for the event you enter. Do not infer legality or scoring from the name of a position alone.

    Safety

    Train under qualified instruction. When a joint lock or choke is fully locked during drilling or sparring, tap early. Do not crank the neck to “force” a finish if an armbar or triangle is incomplete. Partners on top should avoid reckless stacking that drives the bottom player’s neck into awkward flexion.

    Common mistakes

    • Crossing the ankles but never breaking posture—turning guard into a rest position for the passer.
    • Opening the legs too early without frames, inviting a smash pass.
    • Chasing submissions without a sweep threat, making defense predictable.
    • In no-gi: hunting collar habits that do not exist, instead of building overhook and wrist control.
    • In the gi: ignoring lapel and sleeve tools, then wondering why the game feels like slippery no-gi.

    FAQ

    Common questions about identifying and using the position.

    Should beginners even bother with closed guard, or skip to open guard?

    Closed guard is a useful position to learn, but there is no universal first-guard sequence. Learn to identify the position, manage posture, and transition when the ankles open. Follow the progression taught in your beginner class.

    Does closed guard even work in no-gi?

    Yes. The crossed-leg connection remains available in no-gi. Athletes use wrist control, arm ties, overhooks, underhooks, and head control instead of collar and sleeve grips. Its usefulness depends on the exchange, not only the clothing.

    Why do people say close the guard in gi but not in ADCC?

    Closed guard exists in both formats. Guard-pull and passivity rules differ between competitions and divisions, so check the event’s current rules. A ruleset does not establish that every athlete should use or avoid a position.

    Why does my closed guard get passed the second they sit up?

    Sitting upright can help the top player begin an opening, but posture alone is not a completed pass. Notice whether your leg connection, grips, and hip angle have changed. Ask your coach to identify the first control you lost.

    How do I break posture if they’re stronger than me?

    Coordinate your leg connection, grips, and hip angle rather than pulling only with your arms. Rehearse the movement cooperatively with a coach before adding resistance; strength and posture affect which option is available.

    They stood up — do I keep closed guard or let it go?

    A standing opponent changes your balance and available connections. Learn a coached transition to an open guard or another position instead of assuming you must keep the ankles crossed at any cost.

    Is lapel stuff / worm guard / loop choke available from closed guard?

    Fabric-based collar and lapel attacks require a gi. The exact setup varies; collar grips and body grips are not interchangeable. Use the entry for the specific technique to understand its starting position.

    What should my hips be doing in closed guard?

    Your hips help manage distance and create an angle. The movement needed for an armbar differs from the movement needed for a sweep. Identify the next position you want rather than moving without a specific task.

    The Bottom Line

    Closed guard is the classic full-guard position in Brazilian jiu-jitsu: bottom player on their back, legs around the opponent’s waist, ankles crossed. Used well, it controls distance, breaks posture, and launches armbars, triangles, kimuras, and sweeps—in both gi and no-gi, with different grips and incentives. Used poorly, it becomes a static shell that invites a pass toward side control or mount. For the next submission-focused entry, see the rear naked choke.
