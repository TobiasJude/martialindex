Danaher Death Squad

The Danaher Death Squad (DDS) was an elite no-gi training group around coach John Danaher at Renzo Gracie Academy (and later Puerto Rico) that popularized systematic leg-lock grappling before splitting in 2021 into lineages such as New Wave and B-Team.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Informal elite group (~2011–2021), not a formal franchise—name coined in community culture.
  • Technical footprint: control-first ashi systems, heel hooks, and later passing/wrestling integration.
  • Competition spotlight via EBI, ADCC trials, and major no-gi titles.
  • 2021 split produced New Wave and B-Team as separate Austin projects.
  • Document influence; avoid cult narratives or invented lineage claims.

What Was the Danaher Death Squad?

DDS was a tightly focused no-gi training cohort that formed around John Danaher's morning classes at Renzo Gracie Academy in New York. Community naming (often credited to Ryan Quinn's online usage) stuck even though Danaher reportedly disliked the moniker. Members across the era included athletes such as Eddie Cummings, Garry Tonon, Gordon Ryan, Craig Jones, Nicky Ryan, Nick Rodriguez, and others—rosters shifted over time.

On Martial Index, DDS is a historical concept: a coaching culture and technical project that accelerated the leg-lock revolution, not a living franchise you "join" today. Descendants include New Wave Jiu-Jitsu and B-Team.

How DDS Training Culture Worked

Public accounts describe high-volume drilling, positional sparring, and interconnected lower-body systems: enter ashi / 50/50 / 411 / saddle, control the knee line, then finish (often with heel hooks). Later years added more explicit wrestling and passing curricula—continuity that New Wave still markets.

Pillar links: entanglement entries often start from open guard or half guard structures; top answers live under guard passing.

Gi vs No-Gi

No-gi. DDS public identity is no-gi / ADCC-adjacent. Rotational leg locks and reaping-heavy entanglements define highlight culture.

Gi. Many DDS athletes also held gi ranks; competition menus shrink under IBJJF heel-hook and reap limits. Do not paste no-gi DDS footage onto gi match planning.

What Did DDS Look Like in Practice?

In early EBI-era matches, a DDS athlete might pull to a single-leg X or 50/50, isolate an outside heel hook, and end the match before a traditional pass-and-mount sequence ever appeared—forcing the wider sport to learn entanglement defense.

Gi. Same athletes often played more orthodox passing and upper-body submissions under IBJJF constraints.

No-gi. Expect saddle / inside-ashi entries and heel-hook races as default finishing trees.

Phases / Variations

Blue-basement formation

Early Renzo Gracie Academy years: small cohort, heavy experimentation with leg systems after Dean Lister's famous "50% of the body" challenge to Danaher.

Leg-lock peak years

Mid-2010s EBI and ADCC visibility—Cummings, Tonon, Ryan, and others made heel hooks a mainstream topic.

Split and aftermath

2020–2021 relocation stresses and interpersonal conflict ended the unified room; New Wave and B-Team carried forward separate cultures.

Influence and Counters

Influence. Forced gyms worldwide to teach heel-hook defense, ashi entries, and no-gi passing that accounts for lower-body attacks—see modern passing.

Counters. Body-lock pressure, careful knee-line management, and early extraction from 50/50 became standard passer answers.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

DDS highlight culture tracks ADCC / submission formats more than IBJJF gi. Always verify legality before drilling competition-illegal finishes.

Safety

Heel hooks and reaps demand early taps and mature partners. Historical footage is not a safety syllabus.

Common mistakes

  • Romanticizing interpersonal conflict as "necessary greatness."
  • Assuming every DDS athlete shared one identical game.
  • Confusing DDS, New Wave, and B-Team as one continuous gym.
  • Copying elite training volume without recovery or coaching oversight.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Does DDS still exist?

Not as a unified team. The 2021 split ended the single room; successor projects continue separately.

Who invented the name Danaher Death Squad?

Community lore credits former teammate Ryan Quinn's online usage; it was not an official corporate brand.

Was DDS only about leg locks?

Leg locks defined public fame, but members also passed, wrestled, and finished upper-body submissions—see leg lock revolution.

How is DDS different from Renzo Gracie Academy?

DDS trained inside Renzo's New York academy for years but was a specific cohort/coach circle, not the whole school.

What came after the split?

New Wave (Danaher/Gordon Ryan line) and B-Team (Craig Jones and others) both landed in Austin—see New Wave.

Should white belts copy DDS training?

Study fundamentals and safety first. Elite room volume and heel-hook menus are advanced contexts.

Is this page endorsing any side of the split?

No. Martial Index records history and systems influence without recruiting or scoring personal disputes.

The Bottom Line

The Danaher Death Squad was a historically specific elite no-gi cohort that mainstreamed systematic leg locks and then fractured into successor cultures such as New Wave. Learn the technical lessons; skip the myth-making.
