Oi-Zuki

Oi-zuki is the lunge punch: you step into zenkutsu-dachi and punch with the same-side hand, finishing with hikite and kime.

Key Takeaways

  • Stepping same-side punch.
  • Foot–fist timing.
  • Needs zenkutsu integrity.
  • Contrast with gyaku-zuki.

What Is Oi-Zuki?

Oi-zuki (追い突き), also called the lunge or stepping punch, pairs the advancing lead with the same-side fist. It is central to Kihon and appears throughout Heian Shodan.

Compare with Gyaku-Zuki (opposite hand/lead) and Choku-Zuki (straight punch without the full step emphasis).

In Karate, oi-zuki trains timing: fist and front foot arrive together without collapsing the stance.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Oi-Zuki behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Oi-Zuki Works

From Zenkutsu-Dachi or a ready position, drive off the rear leg, step, and punch as the front foot plants. Hikite pulls; hips contribute; Kime finishes.

Hanmi often appears mid-step; many styles finish more shomen on impact—follow your coach.

Common prep: Gedan-Barai then stepping oi-zuki down the line.

Mechanically, successful oi-zuki work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, oi-zuki lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how oi-zuki appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill oi-zuki daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Sport scoring prefers other tools; oi-zuki remains a grading staple.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Line kihon: gedan-barai, step oi-zuki, repeat—head height steady.

Kata: consecutive oi-zuki sequences in Heian Shodan.

Dojo picture: oi-zuki appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply oi-zuki after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Fist arrives before the foot (or long after).
  • Overreaching with the shoulder.
  • Rear heel up and dead.
  • No {hikite}.
  • Rising up during the step.
  • Treating oi-zuki as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill oi-zuki inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so oi-zuki connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether oi-zuki appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Slow-motion steps with a metronome: plant and punch on the same beat.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe oi-zuki as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put oi-zuki behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Karate Stance, Kihon, and Kata into siblings, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for kicks, joint stress in deep stances, and impact training.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep oi-zuki scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on oi-zuki, one situational kumite theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Oi-zuki vs gyaku-zuki?

Same-side stepping punch vs reverse punch—see Gyaku-Zuki.

Which foot moves?

You step so the punching side becomes the lead in standard oi-zuki.

Is it used in sparring?

Less than Kizami-Zuki/Gyaku-Zuki, but the mechanics transfer.

Why synchronize foot and fist?

Stability and power; staggered timing is an advanced accent.

Common kata home?

Heian Shodan and many beginner forms.

The Bottom Line

Drill Oi-Zuki until the step and fist share one landing—then Zenkutsu-Dachi and Hikite stop fighting each other.
