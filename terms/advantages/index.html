Advantages (IBJJF)

Advantages in IBJJF-style competition are a secondary scoreboard: they recognize near-completed scoring actions and submission threats that put the opponent in real danger without awarding full points.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Advantages never outrank points—one point beats many advantages.
  • They reward almost-passes, almost-sweeps, and near submissions (verify live criteria).
  • Do not “play for advantages” as a primary strategy.
  • Penalties can feed advantages into the scoreboard—see penalties.
  • Always confirm referee interpretations in the current IBJJF book.

What Is Advantages (IBJJF)?

Advantages exist because jiu-jitsu is full of almosts: a pass that nearly clears the legs, a sweep that puts weight on the opponent for a moment, a choke that forces a desperate defense. The federation uses advantages to credit initiative when full stabilization for points does not occur.

Treat advantages as a tie-break layer beneath points scoring. If points differ, advantages usually do not matter. If points are equal, advantages can decide—until penalties or referee criteria intervene per the live book.

Conceptually they connect to every pillar: almost passing relates to guard passing; almost finishing from mount or back control still teaches attack chains. See the hub IBJJF Rules.

How Advantages (IBJJF) Works

Attack to finish or to stabilize—let advantages be a byproduct, not the goal.

When you nearly pass, keep chains alive into knee slice / long step / body lock families rather than resetting to farm another almost.

From bottom, sweep attempts that force the opponent to post and scramble build both skill and potential advantages—but prioritize completing the sweep.

Film study: watch referee hand signals at your local federation events; vocabulary varies less than gym folklore claims.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Collar threats and sleeve drags create many “almost submission” looks referees may credit—still verify.

No-gi. Front headlocks and body-lock near-passes play the same conceptual role without fabric.

ADCC culture does not use IBJJF advantages the same way—see ADCC rules.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex’s knee-cut nearly clears but the opponent recovers half guard—scorekeepers may note an advantage while Alex continues the pass chain.

Jordan hits a triangle that forces a frantic stack escape; even without the tap, the near finish can matter if the match ties on points.

Sam leads only on advantages and stalls; a penalty cycle can erase that soft lead—see stalling culture on the penalties page.

Types / Variations

Near positional scores

Almost takedown, sweep, pass, mount, or back that lacked full control time.

Near submissions

Attacks that put the opponent in genuine finishing danger without the tap.

Penalty-fed advantages

Certain penalty progressions award advantages to the opponent—official sequence only.

Strategic residual

End-of-match soft edges when points are tied—never your A-game plan.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: any committed scoring attempt or sincere submission chain.

Counters: don’t give “free almosts”—frame early, keep elbows in, and clear submission posture before the danger becomes referee-visible.

Escapes from advantage deficit: score real points or finish; hunting decorative almosts rarely flips a committed passer.

Advantages vs Points

Factor Advantages Points
Weight Secondary / tie-break Primary scoreboard

Risk of farming High—leads to stalling Lower if you keep improving

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Advantage definitions, examples, and hand signals live only in the current IBJJF rulebook. Martial Index explains the concept so you know what to look up—not so you can skip the PDF.

Safety

Cranking harder “for an advantage” is abuse. Release on taps; near-submission training still needs controlled finishing pressure.

Common mistakes

  • Believing five advantages beat one point.
  • Diving on loose submissions only to pad almosts.
  • Celebrating advantages while the opponent climbs real points.
  • Ignoring stalling rules because you “have the advantages.”

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Do advantages beat points?

No. Points are primary; advantages mainly matter when points are equal (verify live procedures).

What earns an advantage?

Near-completed scoring actions and submission attempts that create real danger—exact language is in the official book.

Should I play for advantages?

No. Play for points and finishes; let advantages be residual.

How do penalties interact?

Penalty progressions can award advantages or points to the opponent—see penalties.

Does ADCC use advantages?

ADCC uses a different sorting philosophy—see ADCC rules.

Gi vs no-gi differences?

Same concept; legal attacks and grip tools differ by book.

How do I practice?

Specific sparring where almost-passes must be chained into completed passes within a short window.

The Bottom Line

Advantages credit courage and near-misses, but they are not a strategy. Climb for points, finish when it’s there, and verify every criterion in the current IBJJF rulebook.
