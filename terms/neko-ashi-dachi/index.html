What Is Neko-Ashi-Dachi?

Neko-ashi-dachi (猫足立ち) is the cat stance under Karate Stance. Weight sits mostly on the rear leg; the front foot touches lightly.

Compare with Kokutsu-Dachi (also rear-biased but different shape) and Kiba-Dachi (wide root).

Useful for Mae-Geri and some crescent-kick drills (Mikazuki-Geri).

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Neko-Ashi-Dachi behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Neko-Ashi-Dachi Works

Rear knee bent, rear heel down, torso upright, front foot on the ball with heel raised as style requires. Keep hips settled—do not tip forward.

The light front foot can chamber quickly into kicks or step into another dachi.

Stability test: if a light push topples you, the rear knee/hip is not truly loaded.

Mechanically, successful neko-ashi-dachi work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, neko-ashi-dachi lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how neko-ashi-dachi appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill neko-ashi-dachi daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Exact front-foot contact (ball vs toe) is style-specific.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Kata transitions that pause in cat stance before a front kick.

Drill: neko-ashi → Mae-Geri → recover neko-ashi without hopping the rear foot.

Dojo picture: neko-ashi-dachi appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply neko-ashi-dachi after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Front foot flat and heavy.
  • Rear heel up—no root.
  • Leaning the chest over the front knee.
  • Treating it as a pose with no kick option.
  • Sliding the rear foot on every kick (telegraph).
  • Treating neko-ashi-dachi as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill neko-ashi-dachi inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so neko-ashi-dachi connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether neko-ashi-dachi appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Slow-motion kick from neko-ashi with a partner lightly holding your belt for balance feedback.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe neko-ashi-dachi as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put neko-ashi-dachi behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Karate Stance, Kihon, and Kata into siblings, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for kicks, joint stress in deep stances, and impact training.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep neko-ashi-dachi scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on neko-ashi-dachi, one situational kumite theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for neko-ashi-dachi under your next card (WKF vs full-contact, target levels, protective gear) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is neko-ashi-dachi?

Cat stance—rear-weighted with a light front foot.

Neko vs kokutsu?

Both rear-biased; shapes, foot positions, and uses differ—learn both under Karate Stance.

Why the raised front heel?

Keeps the front foot free to move or kick.

Is it only traditional?

Common in syllabus kata; sparring may use lighter cousins.

Good for beginners?

Yes, once basic balance exists—coach the load.

The Bottom Line

Use Neko-Ashi-Dachi when you need a light front foot—own the rear load so Mae-Geri fires without a hop.
