Trap and Roll

The trap and roll (upa) escapes mount by trapping an arm and same-side leg, then bridging diagonally to reverse. Chain with the knee-elbow escape when they post. Fundamentals live next to shrimp and frames.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Trap arm + leg on one side.
  • Bridge diagonally—not straight up.
  • Ends in their closed guard often—posture up.
  • If they post, switch to knee-elbow.
  • Classic white-belt essential.

What Is Trap and Roll?

Also called the upa or bridge-and-roll: remove their posts on one side and launch them over.

Best vs low mount when you can reach an arm; worse vs perfect high mount with grapevines—then prefer elbow escape.

Cross-link bridge as the engine.

How Trap and Roll Works

Trap their arm (tricep/wrist) to your chest and hook their foot with yours on the same side.

Plant the other foot, bridge explosively toward the trapped side (10 o’clock / 2 o’clock), and roll them over.

Land with elbows in and posture in their closed guard—or pass if they open.

Drill the fake: threaten the roll to force a post, then knee-elbow the posted side.

Competition note: after a successful roll you often land in their closed guard—so the escape is not finished until you posture or pass. Pair every upa drill with an immediate posture-and-open sequence so the reversal does not become a new problem.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve grips make trapping easier; watch collar chokes while you set up.

No-gi. Trap the tricep/upper arm; expect slipperier bridges.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex traps the right arm and foot, bridges, and reverses to top in closed guard.

Jordan’s upa is stuffed by a post; they immediately elbow-escape that side.

Types / Variations

Classic trap and roll

Arm + foot trap, diagonal bridge.

Fake upa to elbow escape

Force the post, then knee-elbow.

One-leg help

Knee in the back to start their hands to the mat.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

From bottom mount primarily. Counters: post the free hand/foot, shift to technical mount, grapevine. Chain escapes rather than spamming one.

Trap and Roll vs Knee-Elbow Escape

Factor Trap and roll Knee-elbow escape
Outcome Often reverse to top Recover guard / half
Needs Reachable arm + bridge room Frames + shrimp space
Fails when They post well Ultra-tight with no seam—need power shrimp
Chain Fake into elbow Fake into upa

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal escape; mount points already awarded may stay on the board depending on rules—verify current scoring language.

Safety

Bridge with control—don’t neck-crank yourself; clear space if grapevined before exploding.

Common mistakes

  • Bridging straight up.
  • Forgetting to trap the leg.
  • Landing without posture in their guard.
  • Never chaining to knee-elbow.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is trap and roll?

The upa mount escape: trap arm and leg, bridge diagonally, reverse.

Also called?

Upa / bridge-and-roll.

When does it fail?

When they post the free limbs or sit too high.

What then?

Knee-elbow escape the posted side.

Where do I land?

Often in their closed guard—posture immediately.

Gi tip?

Sleeve trap helps.

Related movement?

Bridge as the engine.

The Bottom Line

Trap and roll is the diagonal bridge reverse—trap, launch, posture—and chain to knee-elbow when posts appear.
