Sukui Nage

Sukui nage is the scooping throw: hands scoop and lift uke’s body to dump them—Kodokan Te-Waza with important modern gripping caveats. Compare Kata Guruma.

What is Sukui Nage?

Sukui nage (掬投 / scooping throw) is a Kodokan hand technique in which tori scoops under uke—classically often involving lower-body contact with the hands/arms—and throws them by lifting and dumping. It belongs with te-waza literacy beside Kata Guruma and Obi Otoshi.

Modern IJF bans on direct hand/arm attacks below the belt sharply limit classical scoop entries that palm the legs. Martial Index therefore teaches sukui-nage as history + legal adaptation awareness—not as “go pick legs in World Tour shiai.”

Parent: Nage-Waza. Family hub: Te-Waza. On the judo hub, keep scooping throws visible so athletes understand pre-ban competitive judo and kata curricula.

How it works

Classical picture: kuzushi, scoop under, lift, dump. The scoop may historically include leg contact that is illegal as a direct attack in modern IJF senior contests. Legal study focuses on upper-body control patterns and educational/historical modules labelled clearly.

If a scoop stalls, do not escalate into spine-threatening lifts. Disengage or change to another throw such as Tai Otoshi or seoi family attacks under the Seoi Nage (Judo) hub.

Amplitude still decides scores when the form is legal. Partial dumps become ne-waza.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

Gi jackets change scoop angles and available controls. No-gi scooping resembles wrestling lifts—different rule world. Transfers: lifting geometry. Does not transfer: IJF legality assumptions.

What it looks like in practice

Kata and classical classes preserve sukui-nage. IJF-rules Randori should not casually revive banned direct leg scoops. Whiteboard the difference.

Related historical leg attacks—Morote Gari, Kuchiki Taoshi, Kibisu Gaeshi—explain the wider ban context.

IJF notes & safety

Direct hand/arm attacks below the belt are banned in modern IJF senior shiai. Classical sukui-nage forms that rely on such grips are not contest tools on the World Tour. Teach history honestly; do not advise illegal scoops in IJF contests. See Judo Rules.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Drilling banned leg scoops as weekly shiai prep.
  • Confusing sukui with kata-guruma on film.
  • Unsafe lifting without ukemi culture.
  • No labelling of historical vs legal modules.

Training notes

Put sukui-nage in a classical te-waza + history block with explicit IJF notes. Pair reading of leg-grab history pages.

Protect partners’ spines—scoop lifts are not joke throws.

Study notes

Scooping lifts demand more than courage—they demand spine-aware coaching. Historical nage-komi should use experienced uke and clear intensity caps. Treat sukui-nage like a kettlebell: respectable, not a toy.

Post-ban teaching scripts that ignore sukui entirely create athletes who cannot read pre-2010 film. A single labelled historical module fixes that without poisoning contest habits.

If someone asks “is this just a double-leg,” answer carefully: related politics via Morote Gari, different classical identity and hand paths—open both pages.

Deep notes

Historical coaches sometimes paired sukui-nage with other lower-control throws in the same lesson. Modern coaches should pair it with a rules seminar instead—otherwise the body learns a forbidden path while the mouth says “history only.”

Biomechanics note: scooping lifts shift load into tori’s lumbar spine and uke’s torso. Progressive loading, not maximal first reps. Treat it like strength work with a human barbell—spotters, mats, ego checks.

Catalogue honesty: sukui remains in Kodokan educational materials even when contest frequency is near zero. Rarity ≠ deletion. Martial Index keeps rare techniques visible for that reason.

When athletes ask for “legal sukui,” answer with upper-body throws that scratch the same itch—seoi family, kata-guruma adaptations—and open those live pages rather than inventing a loophole.

Educational assessment idea: after a historical sukui module, ask athletes to write three legal throws they will use instead under IJF rules. Collect the papers. That writing assignment does more for contest hygiene than another forbidden scoop rep.

Finally, keep Te-Waza open while you study sukui so the scoop sits in a family rather than as a freak isolate. Family context is how encyclopedias beat move-of-the-week culture.
