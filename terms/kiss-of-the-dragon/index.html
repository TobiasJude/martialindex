Kiss of the Dragon

Kiss of the dragon is a reverse De La Riva back-take/sweep that inverts or slides underneath a passer to emerge behind them—sibling energy to the berimbolo, ending in a back take.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Primary habitat: reverse De La Riva (not classic DLR).
  • Off-balance first—make them step/light—then invert where they are.
  • Far-leg chop / hip control prevents them free-turning to face you.
  • Gi grips help; no-gi needs wrist/ankle discipline.
  • Troubleshoot with berimbolo lessons: grips > heroic rolling.

What Is Kiss of the Dragon?

Where berimbolo often starts from outside DLR hooks, kiss of the dragon typically starts from reverse De La Riva: you are inverted relative to their lead leg, then you travel under to their back. The poetic name stuck in English-speaking academies; the job is still “expose the spine and seat the seatbelt.”

Martial Index groups it with berimbolo under open-guard back-hunting so athletes learn both doors into the same house.

How Kiss of the Dragon Works

Win RDLR with a strong ankle/pants grip and frame. Lighten their lead leg (elevate, kick, or push-pull), invert or roll under the near hip, and keep connection so you do not slide past into a pass.

Chop the far side or hook as you emerge, climb to seatbelt, insert hooks. If they pressure fold you, abandon the vanity roll and reclaim RDLR or knee-shield frames.

Common fix from coaches: invert in place while making them step over you—use their motion—rather than trying to orbit a planted tree.

Troubleshooting mindset: if the orbit fails, reset reverse De La Riva frames and recreate the light leg—kiss of the dragon rewards patience more than desperation rolls.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Pants and sleeve grips stabilize the under-travel.

No-gi. Possible with ankle sleeves/wrist control; expect more athleticism and scrambles.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Brandon Mccaghren-style RDLR kiss demos emphasize reverse De La Riva entry and the spin to the back.

A competitor fails the orbit, resets RDLR, then hits kiss when the passer re-commits forward.

Types / Variations

Standard RDLR kiss

Invert under to back.

SLX-flavored variants

Some schools blend single-leg X ideas into the name.

Pass-counter kiss

Used specifically vs knee-cut pressure.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: reverse De La Riva, some knee-shield to RDLR transitions. Counters: free ankle, smash knee cut before invert, force 50/50. Escapes: peel emerging hooks, turn in early, reclaim passer posture.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal when performed without illegal pressure. Always verify the current IBJJF and ADCC (or event) rulebooks for division, belt, age, and gi vs no-gi. Martial Index gives orientation—not invented illegal-move lists.

Safety

Inversion neck safety identical to berimbolo—progressive drilling only.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to orbit a fully weighted, square passer.
  • Losing the ankle grip mid-invert.
  • Emerging without seatbelt.
  • Ignoring berimbolo as the twin tool.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Why the name?

Colorful English nickname for the RDLR-to-back move—focus on mechanics.

Same as berimbolo?

Sibling, not identical—different preferred starting guards.

Works no-gi?

Yes with adaptations; gi is friendlier.

Main failure point?

No off-balance before inverting.

Points?

Often treated as sweep/back sequences—verify scoring.

Prereqs?

Comfortable RDLR and basic inversion skill.

Next?

Berimbolo + back-take pages.

The Bottom Line

Kiss of the dragon is reverse De La Riva’s invert-to-back door. Off-balance, travel under safely, seat the back—and keep berimbolo as the DLR twin.
