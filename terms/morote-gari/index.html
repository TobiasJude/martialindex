What is Morote Gari?

Morote gari (双手刈) is the Kodokan two-hand reap: both hands attack the legs to take uke down—judo’s classical double-leg cousin. It was a recognised technique and a contest weapon before the IJF banned direct hand/arm attacks below the belt (era beginning 2010). Martial Index keeps the page for history, self-defense literacy, and cross-training transfer—not as IJF contest advice.

Parent: none on Martial Index (standalone historical cluster with siblings). Related: Morote Gari, Kuchiki Taoshi, Kibisu Gaeshi. Also read Kata Guruma for no-leg adaptations after the ban. We do not link a missing “leg-grab-ban” slug—the ban is explained here in prose.

Searchers want legality clarity first. Answer up front: do not attempt direct Morote Gari in IJF senior contests. Then study why it existed and where it still lives.

On the judo hub, historical techniques keep the art’s timeline honest.

How it works

Classical mechanics: level change, both hands on the legs, drive through for the takedown. Modern IJF senior contest: do not do this as a direct attack. Alternatives that remain legal include upper-body throws in Nage-Waza and adapted no-leg kata-guruma stories on Kata Guruma.

For self-defense or non-IJF rulesets, coaches may still teach the mechanics with crash-mat culture and clear consent. For IJF athletes, replace the slot in your game with legal attacks—seoi family, uchi-mata, harai, ashi-waza—rather than hoping referees “miss” a grab.

Ukemi for historical nage-komi still matters in demo contexts. Never surprise partners with banned entries in rules-randori.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

Gi or no-gi, the ban is about hands/arms attacking below the belt in IJF competition—not about whether cloth is present. Wrestling double-legs and heel picks remain other sports’ tools.

Transfers to BJJ/MMA: timing and level change. Does not transfer: IJF scoring incentives.

What it looks like in practice

Historical film before 2010 shows these techniques as ordinary contest weapons. After the ban, World Tour athletes who lived on leg grabs had to reinvent themselves—often raising seoi and uchi-mata volumes.

In modern IJF Randori, treat a direct grab as a rules violation in practice too, so habits match contests.

Educational modules can demonstrate the classical form once, then immediately drill a legal replacement combination.

IJF notes & safety

IJF rules from 2010 forward prohibit direct attacks or blocks with hand(s)/arm(s) below the belt in senior competition (details and later tweaks live in the Sport and Organisation Rules). Direct morote-gari, kuchiki-taoshi, and kibisu-gaeshi contest entries fall under that policy. This encyclopedia explains them as history and transfer—not as current World Tour techniques. See Judo Rules. Do not use them in IJF contests.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Using direct leg grabs in IJF shiai or IJF-rules randori.
  • Assuming “old judo” videos equal current legality.
  • Skipping legal replacement attacks in your tokui-waza.
  • Surprising partners with banned entries.
  • Confusing the three historical techniques with each other on film.

Study notes

Pre-2010 Olympic and World Championship film is full of double-leg judo. Watching it without a rules caption confuses new athletes—always narrate the ban when you press play in a modern IJF room.

Replacement curriculum ideas: when you would have shot morote-gari, chain kouchi into seoi, or threaten uchi-mata after a posture break. The athletic level change can remain; the hand target must change for IJF nights.

Self-defense modules can retain the classical shot with clear consent and crash mats—label the module so contest habits stay clean.
