Rear Hand

The rear hand is your power-side hand in Boxing Stance—home of the Cross, Overhand Right, Rear Hook, and Rear Uppercut.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Power-side concept in stance.
  • Offense + chin cover.
  • Pairs with Lead Hand.
  • Short and timed beats wild and late.

What Is Rear Hand?

Concept page: not a single punch. Orthodox rear = right; southpaw rear = left.

Pairs with Lead Hand: lead measures and disrupts; rear cashes out.

Also a defensive door: rear hand covers the chin when the lead is busy.

Martial Index files Rear Hand under Boxing so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Boxing style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Rear Hand behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Rear Hand Works

Load through the rear hip and Power Line. Keep the rear hand near the face until it fires.

Do not let “power side” mean “always wind up.” Short rear punches beat wild ones.

Train rear-hand counters off Slip and Parry so the hand is not only an offense button.

Mechanically, successful rear hand work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: straights need Slip/Parry awareness; hooks need Bob and Weave literacy; power shots need a clean Power Line.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, rear hand lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill rear hand daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

One-two: lead jab, rear straight.

Defensive: catch on the rear glove, return the rear uppercut.

Fight picture: rear hand appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply rear hand after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Rear hand low while jabbing.
  • Telegraphing every rear shot.
  • Crossing feet to “get more” on the rear.
  • Ignoring rear-hand defense duties.
  • Treating rear hand as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox rear hand inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so rear hand connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether rear hand appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shell defenses, check hooks, and pull counters.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe rear hand as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Boxing Range, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put rear hand behind heavy boxing pressure; others behind movement and counters. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Boxing Stance, Jab, and Slip into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for head movement and power punching where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “pressure,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Boxing entries keep rear hand scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on rear hand, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for rear hand under your next card (glove size, round length, amateur vs pro judging) so training intensity matches the bout.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Which punches are rear-hand?

Cross/straight rear, overhand, rear hook, rear uppercut, and some body digs.

Is rear always stronger?

Usually more mass available; technique still decides.

Orthodox vs southpaw?

Rear hand switches sides with stance.

Lead vs rear emphasis?

Great fighters use both; rear without lead is readable.

Power training?

See punching-power and power-line—not just heavier gloves.

The Bottom Line

Train the rear hand as a complete side—straight, loop, hook, uppercut, and guard—not as a single haymaker button.
