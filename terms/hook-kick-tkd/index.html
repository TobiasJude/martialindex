Hook Kick (Gwidolligi)

Gwidolligi is Taekwondo’s hook kick: extend past the target and hook the heel home—compare with Spinning Hook Kick so you do not add a spin you did not earn.

What Is Hook Kick (Gwidolligi)?

Hook kick (gwidolligi / 후려차기 family) chambers, extends past the target, then hooks the heel or sole back.

It is a common Head Kick (Olgul Chagi) and guard-stripping tool. The path is not a Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick) whip and not a Naeryeo Chagi (Axe Kick) chop.

Compare with Spinning Hook Kick: the spinning version adds a turn. This page is the non-spin hook.

Some schools teach it from a side-kick chamber; others from a turning-kick chamber. The hook is the identity.

Read this beside the Taekwondo style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Hook Kick (Gwidolligi) behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Hook Kick (Gwidolligi) Works

Chamber high, extend so the heel passes the target, then bend the knee to hook through.

The Pivot Foot (Taekwondo) and hip turn create the line; the lower leg supplies the hook.

Retract to Chamber (Bal Deung) and drop into Taekwondo Stance. A hooked kick that stays extended is a gift.

Hands stay up. Head-line hooks get countered by people who step in.

Mechanically, successful hook kick (gwidolligi) work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, hook kick (gwidolligi) is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill hook kick (gwidolligi) daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Lead-leg hook after a Feint Kick roundhouse chamber—opponent covers the body, you hook the head.

Pad: coach holds a side-of-head target; you pass it and hook without spinning.

Fight picture: hook kick (gwidolligi) appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply hook kick (gwidolligi) after a Feint Kick and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to Kick Combination work instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Common Mistakes

  • Hooking with the calf instead of the heel/sole.
  • No extension—just a floppy crescent.
  • Dropping the hands on every head hook.
  • Adding an accidental spin and calling it {spinning-hook-kick}.
  • Throwing hooks when the opponent is already inside knee range.
  • Treating hook kick (gwidolligi) as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowkick hook kick (gwidolligi) inside full rounds; return to Taekwondo Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so hook kick (gwidolligi) connects to a Kick Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so Counter Kick timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether hook kick (gwidolligi) appears in the right range band for Kyorugi (Sparring) or forms context.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for spinning kicks, aerials, and PSS-targeted Palding (Trunk Scoring) or Head Kick (Olgul Chagi) work.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe hook kick (gwidolligi) as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor distance, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put hook kick (gwidolligi) behind heavy Olympic pressure kicking; others behind traditional Poomsae precision. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Taekwondo Stance, Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick), and Kyorugi (Sparring) into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for spinning kicks and head-height targets where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “roundhouse,” “cut kick,” “hook,” and “feint.” Martial Index’s Taekwondo entries keep hook kick (gwidolligi) scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Muay Thai or Boxing articles that share casual English labels.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on hook kick (gwidolligi), one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for hook kick (gwidolligi) under your next card (WT vs ITF, PSS vs paper scoring, headgear) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Gwidolligi vs spinning hook?

This page is the non-spin hook. Spinning Hook Kick adds rotation; Bandae Dollyo Chagi (Reverse Turning) is the ITF reverse-turn cousin.

Good for PSS head?

If the heel/sock meets the head sensor with enough force under Electronic Scoring (PSS).

Hook vs crescent?

Bakkat Chagi (Outside Crescent) / An Chagi (Inside Crescent) slash across; the hook recoils the heel.

Lead or rear first?

Lead for speed; rear for the power feel.

Self-defense?

A controlled hook can clear a guard. It is not a first-day street technique.

The Bottom Line

Learn the hook as a precise heel path—then add spin only when Spinning Hook Kick is actually the job.
