What Is Dwi Chagi (Back Kick)?

Dwi chagi (뒤차기) is the back kick. Eyes glance over the shoulder; heel drives on a straight line.

It is a classic counter in Kyorugi (Sparring) when opponents rush turning kicks or step onto your centerline.

Compare with Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick): dollyo arcs; dwi drives straight after the turn.

Different from spinning hook kicks (later batches)—back kick stays linear through the heel.

How Dwi Chagi (Back Kick) Works

From fighting stance, turn over the lead shoulder (or as coached), sight the target, chamber briefly, drive heel into trunk, land without falling.

Hands protect as you turn—do not invent a full spin pirouette unless the variation demands it.

Timing beats raw speed: leave early and they wait; leave late and you eat the kick.

PSS athletes must still hit legal Hogu zones with enough force.

Mechanically, successful dwi chagi (back kick) work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, dwi chagi (back kick) is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill dwi chagi (back kick) daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Counter: opponent chambers a big rear turning kick; you spin dwi chagi into their exposed trunk.

Drill: partner throws slow dollyo on loop; you answer only with back kick and recovery.

Fight picture: dwi chagi (back kick) appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply dwi chagi (back kick) after a feint and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Turning blind without sighting—kicking air or illegal areas.
  • Falling forward after impact (gam-jeom risk).
  • Telegraphing with a huge step every time.
  • Dropping both hands during the turn.
  • Using it as a lead-off spam tool without setups.
  • Treating dwi chagi (back kick) as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Back kick vs spinning hook?

Back kick is heel-linear; hook variants whip the heel on a hook path.

Is it high percentage in Olympics?

Yes as a counter for many athletes when timed well.

How do I not get dizzy?

Short turns first; build spin volume gradually.

Body or head?

Trunk is the percentage target; head back kicks are highlight-risky.

Relation to dollyo?

Train both: Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick) offense and dwi counters.

The Bottom Line

Keep dwi chagi sharp as your emergency brake when opponents overswing—practice the landing as hard as the impact.
