Collar Tie

A collar tie grips the back of the head or neck to break posture, set dirty boxing, and open shots—single or doubled depending on the art.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Elbow wedge and fingertip pressure beat loose grabs.
  • Single collar frees a punching hand.
  • Double collar overlaps Muay Thai plum—cross-link, don’t duplicate.
  • Hand fighting clears and reclaims ties.
  • Feeds dirty boxing and level changes.

What Is Collar Tie?

The collar tie is wrestling’s head-control staple translated into MMA dirty boxing. Martial Index’s Collar Tie page covers the MMA/wrestling usage; Single Collar Tie details the one-hand fighting grip; Muay Thai’s Double Collar Tie / Thai Clinch (Plum) cover plum.

This entry sits in the Dirty Boxing cluster—read the pillar early so grip and fence details stay connected to the bigger MMA map.

Read this beside Collar Tie’s siblings when you need the full cluster; keep the focus on how Collar Tie behaves under gloves, elbows (where legal), and referee stoppages.

How Collar Tie Works

Place the hand high on the head, elbow down the centerline or wedged on the collarbone, and pull posture forward/down while you punch, snap, or drop to a Level Change.

Mechanically, successful collar tie work is usually a chain: establish position or grip → remove a base or frame → apply the scoring action → recover posture with hands alive.

If distance or fence angle is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close without a pin and you collide into frames, whizzers, or submission threats.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: clinch tools need pummel awareness; GNP tools need escape recognition; get-ups need strike covers.

MMA Rules / Cage Context

Under Unified Rules MMA, collar tie lives inside a full striking-and-grappling rule set—illegal targets, grounded-opponent knees, and fence-grab fouls change how you invest in this tool.

Amateur cards and some commissions restrict elbows or ground strikes. Gym culture may still drill collar tie daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit.

Confirm the bout agreement before importing gym frequency into restricted rules or into pure grappling contexts where strikes disappear.

Judging rewards damage, aggression, and control differently by panel. Let the card’s eyes shape whether you emphasize volume, rides, or finishes.

Pulling hair or eye gouging illegal; legal collar ties are on the head/neck permissibly.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Single collar, three short hooks, step to fence.

Collar tie snap into double-leg entry.

Fight picture: collar tie appears when cage geometry and grip economy say so, then yields to the next tool in the MMA chain—clinch, takedown, GNP, or get-up.

Gym test: can you apply collar tie after a realistic feint or scramble and still recover your stance or pin? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Grabbing low on the neck without elbow position.
  • Both hands tying while eating knees.
  • No angle.
  • Treating collar tie as a highlight instead of a position-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down or hips light.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe collar tie as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor fence awareness, or grips that collapse when strikes enter the round.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put collar tie behind heavy wrestling; others behind dirty boxing; submission-heavy rooms may leave GNP light. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Ground and Pound, Cage Wrestling, Dirty Boxing, and Get-Up into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for elbows, mat returns, and live striking on the ground.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “clinch,” “control,” and “get-up.” Martial Index’s MMA entries keep collar tie scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated pure-BJJ articles that share casual language—though we cross-link those pages when mechanics overlap.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on collar tie, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where cage angle or grip fighting failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for collar tie under your next card (elbows allowed? fence warnings? judging bias?) so training intensity matches the bout, not a generic Instagram MMA montage.

Film and Progress Notes

In live rounds, collar tie rarely appears in isolation: it is set by footwork, a prior grip fight, or a failed shot. Train the entry that creates the chance, not only the highlight finish.

Coaches tracking progress often use a simple metric: did collar tie appear under fatigue in the final minute? If it only works fresh, the skill is incomplete.

When reading fight film, pause every time collar tie shows up and ask what grip or fence angle made it free—and what the opponent failed to do. That reverse-engineering habit beats collecting more techniques.

Finally, keep Martial Index cross-links honest: if a BJJ or Muay Thai page already owns a pure-art stem, use it. This MMA entry exists because strikes, gloves, and the cage change incentives around collar tie.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Collar tie vs Thai plum?

Shared head-control family; plum usually seeks double collar and knees under Muay Thai scoring—link Thai Clinch (Plum).

One hand or two?

One for punching; two for heavier posture control.

How does Collar Tie fit beginner MMA training?

Learn it after stance and basic clinch/takedown literacy, then add it to positional rounds with coach oversight.
