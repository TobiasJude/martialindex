Kimura from Guard

The kimura from guard is the bottom-side kimura from closed guard (and nearby open/half variants): break posture, sit up on an angle, lock the figure-four on their posted arm, then finish the shoulder, sweep, or climb to the back.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Sit-up mechanics overlap the hip-bump sweep—same punch, different target.
  • Pinch the elbow to their hip; a loose figure-four is not a kimura.
  • Parent: kimura. Top cousin: kimura from side control.
  • If they post to stop the sweep, the kimura is often the punish.
  • Gi sleeves help; no-gi lives on wrist control and a tight closed guard.

What Is Kimura from Guard?

From closed guard the kimura is both a tap and a steering wheel. You break their posture, sit toward one hip, and catch the arm they posted to keep from being swept. The same figure-four that ends matches also creates sweeps and back takes when they defend the shoulder.

It is a core closed-guard cluster attack alongside the armbar from guard, triangle from guard, and hip-bump family. Beginners learn it as a submission; intermediates keep the grip even when the tap disappears.

Martial Index separates the guard path from the side-control path so each search intent has a home, with heavy links back to the parent kimura.

How Kimura from Guard Works

Break posture with legs and grips so at least one of their hands plants on the mat. Control that wrist at the wrist bone—not the meat of the forearm—and sit up as if hitting a hip bump.

Shoot your overhooking arm deep over their tricep, catch your own wrist, and close guard high on their back. Elbows pinch; their hand should travel toward their own hip / behind the back.

To finish, rotate your torso toward the trapped arm and extend your legs to stretch them out. To sweep, keep the kimura and roll them toward the trapped shoulder. To take the back, sit through as they post the free hand.

If they stack, angle off or switch to an omoplata / armbar rather than fighting a vertical pile-driver.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve-and-collar posture breaks make the sit-up cleaner; you can also start from a failed scissor or flower sweep when they post.

No-gi. Wrist rides and high closed guard replace fabric. Expect them to hide the elbow—open a hip and sit up on a diagonal, not straight into their chest.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex hip-bumps, they post a hand, and the kimura grip is already waiting—tap or sweep from the same sit-up.

Jordan cannot finish the shoulder, keeps the figure-four, and climbs to the back as the opponent turns away from the pain.

Types / Variations

Classic closed-guard kimura

Sit-up, figure-four, rotate. The white-belt staple that still works at brown.

Hip-bump / sweep kimura

Same sit-up as the hip bump; they post, you punish the post.

Open / half-guard kimura

Same grip from open or half when they post a hand during a pass or wrestle-up.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: posture break + posted hand, failed hip-bump, or they reach too far to pass. Counters: extract the elbow to their hip, sprawl their weight onto you, or stack. Escapes: turn the thumb, hide the elbow, or roll toward the lock early—do not wait for a fully extended shoulder.

Kimura from Guard vs Kimura from Side Control

Factor From guard (bottom) From side control (top)
Job Tap, sweep, or back take Tap or steer from top control
Angle Sit-up diagonal from closed guard Walk to north-south
Common chain Hip bump ↔ kimura ↔ omoplata Kimura ↔ side triangle ↔ back
Beginner risk Getting stacked / passed Losing side while hunting the arm

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Adult kimuras are standard legal shoulder locks. Always verify the current IBJJF and ADCC (or event) rulebooks for division, belt, age, and gi vs no-gi. Martial Index gives orientation—not invented illegal-move lists.

Safety

Bottom kimuras invite stacks. Protect your neck, tap early on your own shoulder, and do not crank a partner who is already rolling to relieve the joint.

Common mistakes

  • Sitting up square into their chest instead of angling to a hip.
  • Grabbing the forearm instead of the wrist.
  • Open, loose guard that lets them stand out of the lock.
  • Abandoning the grip the moment the tap fails—keep the system.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is this just a hip-bump with extra steps?

They share a sit-up. The hip bump wants the far post; the kimura punishes the near posted arm.

Can I hit this from open guard?

Yes—any time they plant a hand near your hip. Closed guard is the cleanest classroom version.

What if they stack me?

Angle off, switch to omoplata/armbar, or release and recover posture. Do not fight a vertical stack.

Gi or no-gi?

Both. Sleeves help isolation; no-gi teaches cleaner wrist control.

Same lock as the top kimura?

Same figure-four family; different angle and job. Read kimura from side control next.

Should beginners learn this early?

Yes—it teaches posture breaking, sit-ups, and a real finish from closed guard.

Does it score points?

The sweep can. The tap ends the match. Verify scoring in the current rulebook.

The Bottom Line

Kimura from guard is a closed-guard sit-up system: break posture, catch the post, pinch the figure-four, then tap, sweep, or take the back. Pair it with the parent kimura and the top-side kimura from side control.
