What Is Jitte?

Jitte (十手) is commonly glossed as “ten hands,” suggesting multi-directional defensive capacity. Some application traditions discuss staff (bo) defense themes—treat those as study lenses, not mandatory folklore.

Grouped with Jion and Jiin for mid-level study, Jitte often feels more spacious in embusen and posture changes.

Rising block families and rooted horse-stance moments appear in many Shotokan performances—verify against your association text.

Martial Index files Jitte under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Jitte behaves as a Kata pattern, not as a punch/kick drill alone.

How Jitte Works

Own the spacious embusen; do not shrink traveling steps to “look neat.”

Rising shapes need the same elbow discipline as Age-Uke kihon.

Keep Kiba-Dachi rooted when the form drops into horse-stance power.

Mechanically, strong Jitte work is usually a chain: stance integrity → Embusen honesty → timing → clear Kime → recovery with Kamae alive.

If orientation drifts, even perfect arms fail—walk the floor line slowly before chasing speed.

Pair form quality with Kihon: the same blocks, punches, and kicks that appear in Jitte should survive line practice and light partner timing.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, Jitte lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, kata competition scoring rewards clarity, timing, and athleticism; exact embusen and counts follow association texts.

Gym culture may drill Jitte weekly; grading and competition may emphasize different tempo and presentation.

Confirm your association’s syllabus text before treating any public video’s count as law.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Temple-group week: Jion, Jiin, Jitte once each with one shared cue (stance depth).

Slow Jitte focusing only on rising-arm trajectories.

Dojo picture: Jitte appears when Kata class cycles the syllabus, then yields to Kihon corrections on the hardest section.

Gym test: can you perform Jitte with honest Embusen after fatigue, still finishing with Kime? If not, slow the map before adding polish.

Common Mistakes

  • Shrinking the spacious map.
  • Mythology first, mechanics never.
  • Soft horse-stance landings.
  • Ignoring age-uke kihon transfer.
  • Skipping temple-group contrast.
  • Treating Jitte as a highlight reel instead of a stance-and-embusen skill.
  • Skipping recovery—finishing a sequence then posing with empty kamae.
  • Ignoring sibling kata that share themes you have not stabilized yet.

Training Notes

Section-drill Jitte: isolate the hardest turn or one-leg moment, then reconnect to the full Embusen.

Slow training days: hold stances and Hikite longer so Kime has somewhere to land.

Partner timing (light application study) only after the skeleton is stable—do not rewrite the official pattern casually.

Film from the same corner weekly to spot embusen drift and height changes.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially jumps, one-leg balances, and deep rooted stances.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe Jitte as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under fatigue, poor Embusen, or a stance that collapses when speed arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put Jitte behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport presentation. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Kata, Embusen, and Karate Stance into sibling kata, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for jumps, deep stances, and one-leg balance work.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “form,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep Jitte scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical runs of Jitte, one slow section focus, and one film review asking where timing or height failed.

If you compete in kata, write a one-line association note for Jitte (count, embusen, optional tokui status) so training matches the card.
