Drop Seoi Nage

Drop seoi nage is competition shorthand for a knee-drop seoi entry: change level, fit the shoulder, pull uke over—cousin to Kodokan Seoi Otoshi, not a separate classical gokyo name.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Modern competition language under Seoi Nage (Judo).
  • Compare carefully with Kodokan Seoi Otoshi.
  • Needs forward Kuzushi before the knees hit.
  • Related nickname: Korean Seoi for deep drop styles.
  • Follow messy landings into Ne-Waza.
  • Coach knees, necks, and Ukemi—amplitude is not free.

What is Drop Seoi Nage?

Drop seoi nage is not a separate Kodokan catalogue title the way seoi-otoshi or ippon-seoi-nage are. It is the worldwide competition and gym label for seoi attacks that deliberately drop one or both knees to finish—or to get under a defensive uke. Martial Index gives the phrase its own page because search volume is real, and because coaches need a place to separate slang from Kodokan names without pretending the film does not exist.

Parent navigation is the Seoi Nage (Judo) hub inside Nage-Waza. The closest classical neighbour is Seoi Otoshi: when the concept is pull-down with knees grounded at kake, Kodokan language prefers otoshi. When athletes still say “I dropped the seoi,” they often mean this competition pattern—from Morote Seoi Nage grips or Ippon Seoi Nage traps.

BJJ-labelled seoi-nage covers cousin shoulder throws in gi jiu-jitsu; keep that art label honest. This entry stays on judo incentives: IJF scores, judogi grips, and World Tour film study on the judo style hub.

Historically, knee-drop seoi volume exploded as athletes hunted ways under taller postures and as lightweights used tempo seoi as a primary scoring engine. The nickname cluster also overlaps Korean Seoi—a style/origin label, not a second gokyo row.

How it works

Create forward Kuzushi first. Athletes who plant both knees against a flat, heavy partner only invent a new shoulder injury. After the break, turn in as for seoi, then let the knees claim the mat while the hands continue loading uke over the back or trapped arm. Elbows stay close; the head tracks the throw rather than spearing the tatami.

Decision-making is part of the technique. Standing seoi that is already winning should not be abandoned for a dramatic drop. Drop when height, posture, or a posting hand kills the upright finish—or when the tactical plan is to force a sprawl and chain into pin wrestling. If uke sprawls hard, abandon the dead pull: switch to Kouchi Gari, recover posture, or glue into Osaekomi.

Referees still score landings by amplitude and control under Ippon and Waza-Ari criteria. A knee drop that parks uke on the buttocks without rotation is not automatically a highlight. Finish the turn.

Distinguish teaching cues aloud: “drop for seoi-otoshi mechanics” versus “drop as a competition seoi variant.” Both may look similar on broadcast film; vocabulary for your athletes should not.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

In the Judogi, sleeve-and-lapel or trapped-arm grips make the downward finish possible. Slick jackets and belt-line grips steal leverage—standard mid-sleeve and workable lapel height remain the baseline for teaching.

No-gi drop seoi becomes a level-change shoulder turn with wrist or arm control. It appears in MMA and submission grappling, but success rates and neck risk differ without cloth. Transfers: timing the level change on forward weight. Does not transfer: assuming grip-stalling habits from one rule set will be ignored in another.

Prefer coached progressions. Martial Index is reference material—not a license to copy highlight dives without Ukemi culture.

What it looks like in practice

World Tour lightweights popularised high-volume drop seoi as a tempo weapon: threaten standing seoi, force a posture rise, then drop under. In club Randori, the same pattern appears against taller partners who defend upright. Film study should mute commentary and count how often successful drops began with a step uke did not want to take.

After incomplete throws, hunt pins—especially Kesa Gatame and Yoko Shiho Gatame. See Seoi to Osaekomi. Arm-extended landings may open Juji Gatame conversations via Ippon Seoi to Juji Gatame.

Dojo programming unique to drops: alternate weeks of standing-only seoi with drop permission so athletes do not lose upright tsukuri forever. Knee health and landing mechanics are training constraints, not afterthoughts.

Types / variations

Single-knee drop seoi

One knee claims the mat while the other leg still drives—common bridge between standing seoi and full drop, and often the safest teaching step.

Double-knee drop

Both knees down; high-commitment finish that overlaps Kodokan seoi-otoshi descriptions. Demand excellent ukemi before volume.

Deep “Korean-style” drop

Deeper angle and knee drive associated with Korean athletes—see Korean Seoi for nickname history rather than inventing a fake Kodokan title.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter on forward weight or after a failed standing load that already broke posture. Counters include heavy sprawls, peeling the sleeve, posting on the back, and circling behind the loading shoulder. Combinations: Kouchi Gari into drop seoi; drop seoi feint into Tai Otoshi; partial drop into pin.

Moving Uchi-Komi should include the stand-or-drop decision so Randori is not the first laboratory. Against opposite stances, rebuild the entry footwork instead of forcing a mirrored fantasy.

Compare

Compare Seoi Otoshi: classical te-waza name and pull-down concept versus competition umbrella slang. Compare Morote Seoi Nage and Ippon Seoi Nage for grip bases. Compare Korean Seoi when the conversation is style and origin. Compare Reverse Seoi Nage when the entry turns the opposite direction rather than simply dropping.

IJF notes & safety

Controlled drop seoi is widely used in modern IJF judo when uke can take ukemi and tori keeps a clear throwing direction. Dangerous uncontrolled head-first dives and neck-loading crashes are coached out—and may draw penalties depending on the exact action under live Judo Rules. This page does not teach illegal diving.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Knees first, kuzushi never.
  • Spearing the head into the mat.
  • Only training drops—losing standing seoi.
  • Conflating drop seoi with every te-waza otoshi.
  • Ignoring knee conditioning and landing mechanics.
  • Giving up chest contact and losing the pin.

Training notes

Build standing Morote Seoi Nage and Ippon Seoi Nage before high-volume drops. Film whether the sleeve or trapped arm still pulls after the knees hit.

Ukemi quality sets the ceiling. When landings get sloppy, end the throwing block—even if ego wants more highlight drops. Revisit Te-Waza literacy so athletes can name what they are doing.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is drop seoi a Kodokan name?

No—competition/gym language; see Seoi Otoshi.

Drop seoi vs seoi-otoshi?

Otoshi is classical te-waza; drop seoi is the modern umbrella phrase.

Is Korean seoi the same thing?

Related—see Korean Seoi.

Legal in IJF contests?

Controlled seoi drops are common; follow Judo Rules and coaches on unsafe diving.

Best setup?

Forward Kuzushi via sleeve snap or Kouchi Gari.

Pin after a drop?

Kesa Gatame or Yoko Shiho Gatame.

Parent page?

Seoi Nage (Judo).

The Bottom Line

Use drop seoi as competition language with eyes open: build standing seoi first, study Seoi Otoshi for Kodokan literacy, and protect knees and necks while you chase scores.
