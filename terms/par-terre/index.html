Par Terre

Par terre is freestyle/Greco mat wrestling—top turns for Exposure, not NCAA Riding Time.

What Is Par Terre?

Par terre (French for “on the ground”) is the mat phase in Freestyle Wrestling and Greco-Roman Wrestling.

Athletes may arrive there after takedowns or via passivity/activity calls that put one wrestler top and one bottom in a set position.

Scoring emphasizes turns and Exposure—the Gut Wrench is the classroom example—rather than folkstyle riding-time clocks.

In Wrestling, par terre literacy is the bridge from American folkstyle rooms to international success.

Martial Index files Par Terre under Wrestling so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Wrestling style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Par Terre behaves across freestyle, folkstyle, and Greco-Roman contexts.

How Par Terre Works

Top: lock quickly (gut wrench, lifts, lace turns) and score before the bottom wrestler stands or forces a reset.

Bottom: fight hands, raise hips, and stand or turn into the wrench to kill the lock.

Do not treat par terre like referee’s position folkstyle—the incentives differ.

Coaches script first-three-seconds actions for both top and bottom.

Mechanically, successful par terre work is usually a chain: Wrestling Stance integrity → entry or control → finish, score, or recovery.

If posture collapses or hips float, even textbook grips fail—top riders meet Sit-Out and Switch Escape pressure; shooters meet a Sprawl (Wrestling).

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: rides need Escape literacy; turns need Near Fall awareness; trips need base recovery.

Rules / Style Notes

Under United World Wrestling freestyle rules, par terre is judged through takedowns, exposure, and activity—not riding time.

Under NCAA/NFHS folkstyle, control, escapes, reversals, and near falls reshape how par terre is valued once wrestlers hit the mat.

Greco-Roman bans holds below the waist, so leg-coupled cousins of par terre either disappear or migrate into upper-body variants.

Confirm the rule set before importing gym habits into contest intensity—folkstyle riding does not equal freestyle Par Terre.

UWW passivity and scoring emphases evolve—verify before competition trips.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Passivity call → par terre → gut wrench exposure chain.

Takedown finishes directly into par terre turns without a folkstyle ride pause.

Match picture: par terre appears when stance, ties, and timing say so, then yields to a finish, a Go-Behind, or a reset to neutral.

Room test: can you apply par terre after a Collar Tie or Russian Tie exchange and still recover posture? If not, chain it instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Riding soft like folkstyle without turning.
  • Bottom freezing on all fours.
  • Not knowing current passivity rules.
  • Confusing near-fall counts with UWW exposure.
  • Treating par terre as a highlight instead of a stance-and-timing skill.
  • Shooting or turning without a recovery plan when the opponent bridges or scrambles.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadow-wrestle par terre inside full rounds; return to Wrestling Stance after every attempt.

Partner drills: technical → moving → forced follow-up so par terre connects to a chain (shot → finish, or ride → turn).

Live situational wrestling beats drill-only fluency; feed realistic hand fights so timing is part of the skill.

Film practice weekly and ask whether par terre appears with clean hip position and a real Level Change when shots are involved.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for throws, neck ties, and pinning combinations.

Two-minute par terre rounds top and bottom.

Film Olympic freestyle/Greco mat sequences weekly.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe par terre as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under hand-fighting stress, a collapsed Wrestling Stance, or hips that never get under the opponent.

When you compare rooms, you will see stylistic accents: freestyle rooms push Exposure chains; folkstyle rooms push ride-and-turn; Greco rooms push upper-body locks. The encyclopedia definition of par terre stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Freestyle Wrestling, Folkstyle Wrestling, and Wrestling Stance into siblings, then return here after live rounds to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for throws, neck ties, and pinning combinations where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “ride,” “tilt,” and “headlock.” Martial Index’s Wrestling entries keep par terre scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language—compare pages are labeled when useful.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on par terre, one situational live theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing or hip position failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for par terre under your next event (UWW freestyle, NCAA folkstyle, or Greco) so training intensity matches the bout.
