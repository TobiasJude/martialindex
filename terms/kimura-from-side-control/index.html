Kimura from Side Control

The kimura from side control is the top-side kimura (ude-garami): from side control you isolate the near arm, lock a figure-four, and rotate the shoulder by walking toward north-south—or keep the grip as a steering wheel into back takes and armbars.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Finish from a north-west / north-south angle, not while square in side control.
  • Chest on the tricep and a knee wedge beat raw arm strength.
  • Parent lock: kimura; opposite rotation: americana from side control.
  • The grip is a system—sweeps, back takes, and side triangles live here.
  • Gi sleeve assists; no-gi needs sticky wrist control and tight elbows.

What Is Kimura from Side Control?

In side control, the kimura appears when their near arm is away from the hip—framing your neck, posting, or reaching. You thread under the upper arm, grab your own wrist, and own a figure-four that can tap the shoulder or just control the entire exchange.

Trying to finish while still chest-to-chest and square is the common beginner stall. The high-percentage geometry walks you halfway to north-south, steps a knee over or beside the head, and uses body rotation rather than a biceps curl.

Martial Index files this under both the side-control cluster and the parent kimura so positional search and submission search both land. Compare the bottom-side cousin kimura from guard.

How Kimura from Side Control Works

Pin the near wrist (hand or head clamp) and swim your far arm under their tricep to catch your own wrist. Elbows tight; their wrist, elbow, and shoulder should stack in a small triangle.

Shift your hips toward north-south. Wedge a knee against their head/shoulder so they cannot turn belly-down into the lock. Keep chest weight on the trapped tricep until the grip is fully set.

Finish by rotating their hand toward their hip / behind the back—motorcycle throttle, not a yank toward the ceiling. If they lock hands, threaten the side triangle or peel a thumb rather than muscling a stalemate.

If the tap is not there, keep the kimura as control: sit through to the back, switch to an armbar, or reverse to the far-side americana if the arm rotates the other way.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve grips and collar frames make the initial isolation easier; you can also pin their wrist to the mat with a fist in the sleeve.

No-gi. Monkey grips and head-on-wrist clamps replace cloth. Expect more hand fighting—hide your thumbs.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Sam isolates the near arm in side control, walks to a north-west angle, wedges a knee, and finishes the kimura without ever leaving the mat with their hips.

Riley cannot break the hands, so they lock a side triangle dilemma instead of stalling on a dead figure-four.

Types / Variations

North-south / north-west kimura

The finishing angle: step over or beside the head and rotate with your whole torso.

Kimura as a trap / system

Keep the grip to steer back takes, armbars, and tarikoplata-style branches rather than forcing the tap.

Near-arm vs switch

Classic attack is the near arm; if they rotate the other way, switch to an americana on the same figure-four family.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: they frame your neck, post the near hand, or over-reach during a side-control escape. Also available as you pass into side control if the near elbow is loose.

Counters: elbow to hip, turn into the lock early, clasp hands, or shrimp out before the knee wedge lands. Escapes late: hitchhike / roll toward the trapped shoulder only if space remains—tap if the rotation is already on.

Kimura vs Americana from Side Control

Factor Kimura from side control Americana from side control
Rotation Hand toward hip / behind the back Hand toward the head (paintbrush)
Best when Arm is away from the hip Arm is pinned near the head, 90°
Finish angle Walk to north-south Stay heavier, pin wrist to mat
System value Huge (back takes, triangles) More of a direct tap / switch

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Adult kimuras are standard legal shoulder locks. Always verify the current IBJJF and ADCC (or event) rulebooks for division, belt, age, and gi vs no-gi. Martial Index gives orientation—not invented illegal-move lists.

Safety

Shoulders can pop with little warning. Apply rotation slowly in training and release immediately on the tap. Do not wrench a clasped-hands stalemate.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to finish square in side control with no north-south walk.
  • Leaving space under the chest so they turn into the lock.
  • Bent, loose figure-four that becomes a sloppy straight armlock.
  • Abandoning the kimura the instant they clasp hands instead of switching threats.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Why can't I finish the kimura from regular side control?

You usually need a north-west angle and a head/shoulder wedge so they cannot rotate away.

Is the kimura only a tap?

No—treat it as a control system that also scores back takes and armbars.

Kimura or americana first from side?

If the arm is away from the hip, kimura; if it is pinned by the head, americana. Drill the switch.

Gi sleeve needed?

Helpful, not required. No-gi kimuras are everyday sport tools.

What if they lock hands?

Peel, threaten a side triangle, or walk the grip to the back rather than muscling.

Does this work on bigger partners?

Yes if your chest and hips do the rotation. Strength-only finishes fail first against size.

Related bottom kimura?

See kimura from guard—same figure-four, opposite job (sweep + tap from bottom).

The Bottom Line

Kimura from side control is figure-four plus angle: isolate the near arm, walk toward north-south, rotate with your body. Link the parent kimura and the americana from side control so both rotations stay in one system.
