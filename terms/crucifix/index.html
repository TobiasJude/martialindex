Crucifix

The crucifix is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu back-family position where your legs trap one of the opponent's arms while your arms control the other—stretching them like a cross for rear-naked variations, collar chokes, and armbars.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Legs own one arm; arms own the other—both shoulders compromised.
  • Classic entries: turtle, failed single-legs, and back scrambles.
  • Gi collar chokes are vicious; no-gi leans on RNC variants and armbars.
  • Loose leg triangle on the trapped arm lets them reclaim a frame—pinch.
  • Parent family: back control; cousin: turtle.

What Is the Crucifix?

The crucifix is a back-oriented control where one of the opponent's arms is trapped between your legs (often with a figure-four or tight triangle on the arm) while your arms control their other arm and head. The name comes from the stretched, cross-like posture of the bottom player.

It is a high-percentage finishing position from turtle and a specialized branch of back control offense—famous in both gi (collar chokes) and no-gi (RNC / armbar dilemmas).

How the Crucifix Works

Trapping one arm with the legs removes their near-side defense. Your upper body then attacks the free side: rear-naked variations, collar chokes, or armbars. Because they cannot easily clear both arms, every defense on one side opens the other.

Bottom player's job. Prevent the arm from being trapped between their legs early. If caught, clam the trapped arm, hand-fight the choking arm, and roll to clear the leg triangle before the second attack lands.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Collar chokes from crucifix are among the most reliable finishes in sport BJJ; sleeve control also freezes the free arm.

No-gi. Emphasize RNC mechanics, armbars on the free arm, and wrist control. Expect more scrambles if the leg triangle on the trapped arm is soft.

MMA context. Crucifix creates one-armed opponents for ground-and-pound while keeping choke and armbar threats live.

What Does the Crucifix Look Like in Practice?

Jordan turtles under pass pressure. Alex rides the near shoulder, steps a leg over Jordan's near arm, triangulates the arm with the legs, and attacks the far arm/neck for the finish.

Gi. Alex feeds the far collar for a crucifix collar choke.

No-gi. Alex threads a rear-naked grip and finishes while the legs keep the near arm pinned.

Types / Variations

Back crucifix

Chest toward their back, legs trapping one arm—classic finishing crucifix.

Side crucifix

More lateral orientation; useful transitional control and certain armbar angles.

Choke finishes

Gi collar chokes; no-gi RNC and short-choke mechanics with the free arm trapped.

Armbar finishes

Attack the free arm or transition to juji-style finishes when they defend the neck.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries. Turtle rides, clock chokes that fail into arm traps, scramble back takes, and some guard-pass residuals when an arm is left behind.

Top threats. Collar chokes, RNC, armbars, shoulder pressure / GNPs in MMA.

Bottom counters. Elbows glued in turtle, clear the near arm before the triangle locks, roll through to clear legs, and hand-fight constantly.

Crucifix vs Standard Back Control

Factor Crucifix Back Control
Arm control One arm trapped by legs Both arms free to hand-fight
Hooks Often no traditional hooks Two hooks typical
Finish bias Choke/armbar dilemma on free side RNC + harness attacks
Entry bias Turtle / arm-trap rides Seatbelt + hooks

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal. May score as back control depending on chest-to-back and control criteria—know your rule set. Submissions finish regardless.

Safety

Do not hyperextend shoulders while triangulating the arm. Tap early to chokes; release immediately on tap.

Common mistakes

  • Loose leg triangle—trapped arm escapes and frames return.
  • Attacking the neck without controlling the free arm.
  • Abandoning the crucifix too early for a soft back take.
  • In the gi: forgetting collar feeds that finish the position.
  • In no-gi: no wrist control on the free arm.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is the crucifix in BJJ?

A back-family position where your legs trap one arm and your arms control the other, creating choke and armbar dilemmas.

How do you enter the crucifix?

Commonly from turtle: trap the near arm with your legs while controlling the far arm and head, then finish.

Is crucifix better in gi or no-gi?

Both work. Gi collar chokes are especially high-percentage; no-gi relies on RNC and armbars.

Does crucifix score points?

It can score as back control if rule-set criteria for chest-to-back and control are met—check IBJJF/ADCC specifics.

How do you escape the crucifix?

Clear the trapped arm from between their legs first, then hand-fight the choking arm and roll to recover guard or turtle.

Crucifix vs regular back control?

Crucifix trades traditional hooks for an arm trapped by the legs—stronger arm isolation, different escape problems.

What submissions work from crucifix?

Collar chokes (gi), rear-naked variations, armbars on the free arm, and some shoulder locks.

The Bottom Line

The crucifix traps one arm with the legs and attacks the other side with chokes and armbars—especially from turtle. Gi athletes feed collars; no-gi athletes finish RNCs and armbars. See back control and turtle.
