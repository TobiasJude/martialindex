Sumi Otoshi

Sumi otoshi is the corner drop: hand-driven throw that sends uke down toward the corner line—te-waza timing sibling of Uki Otoshi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

What is Sumi Otoshi?

Sumi otoshi (隅落 / corner drop) is a Kodokan hand technique that throws uke toward a corner direction using arm levers and timing. Like uki-otoshi, it lives in te-waza and kata literacy; unlike a big koshi throw, it succeeds when uke’s balance is already compromised along the correct line.

Parent navigation: Nage-Waza. Family: Te-Waza. Primary compare: Uki Otoshi. Also compare Tai Otoshi for hand throws that insert a leg block.

Search intent is often kata-driven or “why does this throw exist if seoi scores more?”—because timing throws punish specific mistakes and complete the educational map of judo.

On the judo hub, sumi-otoshi reminds athletes that directionality is a skill: the same arms throw differently when the corner line changes.

How it works

Create kuzushi that opens the corner—not only straight forward. Hands guide uke’s upper body while footwork opens the falling lane. The throw is “small” in appearance and unforgiving of mistimed pulls.

If uke braces straight back, sumi-otoshi may die; change to another attack such as Ouchi Gari or reset grips. Do not escalate into neck torque.

Finish with continuous hand action through the landing. Partial throws become pin scrambles under Osaekomi rules.

Kata practice exaggerates clarity; randori practice tests whether you can still find the corner under noise.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

In the Judogi, cloth grips shape how Sumi Otoshi is entered and finished. Competition gripping rules still apply—attacks must be positive under Judo Rules.

No-gi approximations keep timing and direction where possible but lose classic cloth levers. Transfers: kuzushi timing. Does not transfer: assuming every classical grip remains legal or available without a jacket.

Prefer qualified coaching; Martial Index is a map, not unsupervised curriculum.

What it looks like in practice

In Randori, Sumi Otoshi appears when the specific kuzushi direction and grips line up—often rarer than high-percentage seoi or uchi-mata patterns, which is why encyclopedia literacy matters.

Kata and formal Uchi-Komi preserve timing details that competition film may skip. After landings, hunt Osaekomi holds such as Kesa Gatame or Yoko Shiho Gatame when chest contact remains.

Study beside siblings on Te-Waza and the pillar Nage-Waza so the throw is not an orphan highlight.

Types / variations

Kata sumi-otoshi

Slow, clear corner line—best diagnostic for directional kuzushi.

Randori opportunistic sumi

When uke’s weight already leaks to the corner after a failed attack.

Hand-throw medley

Alternating uki and sumi in uchi-komi to feel forward vs corner.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter only when balance and grips already tell the truth—Sumi Otoshi punishes forced entries. Counters include posture correction, grip breaks, and circling off the throwing line.

Combinations should connect to live neighbours (see Compare). Moving uchi-komi before hard nage-komi protects partners and builds timing.

Compare

Versus Uki Otoshi: corner versus floating forward drop. Versus Tai Otoshi: pure hand timing versus body-drop with leg. Versus Seoi Otoshi: no seoi back-load.

IJF notes & safety

Legal classical judo. Ordinary safety: ukemi, no neck cranking, positive gripping under Judo Rules.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Wrong direction (forward only).
  • Arms-only yank without footwork.
  • Skipping kata literacy then expecting randori magic.
  • Confusing with sumi-gaeshi (different throw family).
  • No pin follow-up.

Training notes

Pair sumi-otoshi with Uki Otoshi in the same session. Name the direction aloud before each rep.

If contest tokui-waza culture ignores te-waza, keep a weekly kata-speed block anyway—it improves kuzushi for everything else.

Study notes

Corner directional awareness transfers to almost every throw: ouchi, kouchi, and even seoi finishes improve when athletes can name which compass point uke is leaking toward. Sumi-otoshi is a laboratory for that compass.

Kata-speed sumi reps also humble strong athletes—the throw refuses raw muscle. That humility is useful before they return to high-amplitude seoi nage-komi.

If your syllabus never shows sumi-otoshi, add a quarterly literacy day beside Uki Otoshi rather than waiting for a guest seminar.

Deep notes

Directional vocabulary drill: point to the tatami corner you intend before each sumi-otoshi rep. If athletes cannot point, they cannot throw. The drill sounds childish; it fixes adult timing problems.

Link to ashi-waza: corner leakage also sets Deashi Harai and Ouchi Gari when the corner opens the inner thigh line. Sumi hands and ashi feet should talk to each other across a week’s plan.

Kata judges and coaches notice floppy uki/sumi more than spectators do. If you grade through kata, te-waza timing is not optional decoration.

No—different technique family.
