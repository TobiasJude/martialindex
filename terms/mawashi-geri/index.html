Mawashi-Geri

Mawashi-geri is the roundhouse kick: chamber, rotate the hip, whip or drive on an arc—karate’s primary curved kick.

Key Takeaways

  • Curved kick with hip rotation.
  • Pivot the base foot.
  • Sport kumite staple.
  • Contrast with mae-geri.

What Is Mawashi-Geri?

Mawashi-geri (回し蹴り) is the roundhouse kick. In modern Kumite it is a high-percentage scoring kick.

Compare with Mae-Geri (straight) and Mikazuki-Geri (crescent path).

Impact surface may be ball of foot or instep depending on style and pads/rules.

Martial Index files Mawashi-Geri under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Mawashi-Geri behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Mawashi-Geri Works

Chamber the knee, pivot on the supporting foot, rotate the hip, extend into the target, retract. Keep Kamae hands up.

Supporting foot pivot is essential—stuck feet wreck knees.

Train mid-level before chasing head kicks; control matters.

Mechanically, successful mawashi-geri work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, mawashi-geri lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how mawashi-geri appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill mawashi-geri daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

WKF and knockdown rules treat head kicks differently—know your format.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Pad round: mid mawashi after a Kizami-Zuki feint.

Kihon: slow mawashi focusing on pivot and chamber height.

Dojo picture: mawashi-geri appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply mawashi-geri after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • No pivot on the base foot.
  • Leaning away so hard the kick loses reach.
  • Chambering too low (telegraph).
  • Dropping hands.
  • Hyperextending the knee on pads.
  • Treating mawashi-geri as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill mawashi-geri inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so mawashi-geri connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether mawashi-geri appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Base-foot-only drills: pivot without kicking to groove the turn.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe mawashi-geri as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put mawashi-geri behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Instep or ball?

Both appear; pads and style decide—ask your coach.

Mawashi vs mikazuki?

Roundhouse vs crescent—see Mikazuki-Geri.

Why pivot?

Hip rotation and knee safety.

Good for beginners?

Yes at mid-level with supervision.

Common combo?

Kizami → mawashi or mawashi → Gyaku-Zuki.

The Bottom Line

Pivot, chamber, arc—make Mawashi-Geri a hip kick, not a shin flail, then recover Kamae like a fighter.
