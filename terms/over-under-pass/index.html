Over-Under Pass

The over-under pass is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu pressure pass in which the top player threads one arm under one of the bottom player’s legs and the other arm over the opposite leg, then drives shoulder and hip pressure to flatten and clear the guard—especially famous in gi, fully viable in no-gi with adjusted grips.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Over-under = one leg under your armpit/shoulder, the other controlled over your opposite arm—then relentless pressure.
  • Famous as a Bernardo Faria-style gi pressure pass; the structure also works no-gi without pants grips.
  • Tripod pressure into the diaphragm forces the bottom player to give a side—that side is your pass.
  • Compare with the body lock pass: over-under is under-the-legs pressure; body lock is around-the-waist low passing.
  • Common mistake: holding the shape without driving the shoulder—stalling in their half guard.

What Is the Over-Under Pass?

The over-under is a pressure-oriented guard pass. One of the bottom player’s legs is trapped under your shoulder (the “under”), the other is controlled over your opposite arm (the “over”). From that sandwich you drive forward—often with a tripod base—until the guard opens and you settle into side control, north-south, or mount. It sits inside the broader guard passing family.

It is a hallmark of modern gi pressure passing and remains relevant in no-gi whenever you can win under-the-legs position without giving up elevation sweeps.

Related but distinct from the stack pass (both legs stacked) and the body lock (arms around the waist, not under a leg).

How the Over-Under Works

Trap the legs. Secure the underhook under one leg and pin the other leg with the opposite shoulder/arm so the bottom player cannot recompose butterfly hooks freely.

Build the tripod. Hips high, weight through the shoulder into the diaphragm or sternum. The discomfort is the mechanism: they must move, and movement creates the pass lane.

Choose a side and clear. When they hip out or turn, follow to side control. If they stay flat and strong, improve shoulder angle and hip pressure before you rush the exit.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Pants grips inside the underhooked leg and collar/lapel assists make the cage sticky. Classic sport-gi over-under thrives here.

No-gi. Same sandwich without fabric: underhook the leg deep, control the far hip with your arm and head, and accept that elevation sweeps come faster—so sprawl and shoulder pressure must be tighter.

Half guard. Gi passers often enter over-under from half with lapel or pants help. No-gi half-to-over-under relies on clearing the knee shield first.

Meta. Gi rewards patient diaphragm pressure. No-gi often mixes over-under with body locks and knee cuts when the underhook on the leg becomes risky.

What Does Over-Under Look Like in Practice?

From open guard, Jordan shoots an underhook under Alex’s right leg, posts the left shoulder over Alex’s left shin, and walks hips up into a tripod. Pressure hits Alex’s diaphragm. Alex turns to the right to breathe; Jordan follows, clearing the left leg and landing in side control with the underhook already converted to chest pressure.

In no-gi, Jordan cannot hang on pants—so the underhook is deeper and the head drives into the sternum sooner to deny the elevator.

Types / Variations

Classic over-under to side control

Textbook sandwich into a side-control exit when they give a hip.

Tripod / diaphragm pressure

Emphasis on high hips and shoulder drive—the Bernardo-style pressure layer that makes the pass work against strong frames.

Over-under from half guard

Free or isolate the half-guard knee, then build the same over-under cage before finishing.

Entries, Counters, and Retention

Entries. From standing after clearing feet; from double-unders that become uneven; from half guard; as a counter when toreando fails and you dive under a leg.

Counters. Bottom: elevate before the shoulder settles, underhook the over-arm, shrimp to recover butterfly, or attack the exposed neck/arm. Top: if elevated, sprawl and reconnect; if neck is threatened, prioritize posture over the pass.

Breathing and pacing. Over-under is a pressure pass, not a sprint. Once the sandwich is locked, improve shoulder angle and hip height before you chase the exit. Bottom players who panic-hip early gift the pass; top players who rush early gift the elevator.

Submission threats while passing. From a deep over-under you sometimes see neck exposures or arm isolation for kimura-style attacks, but treat those as bonuses. The identity of the technique is still the pass to a dominant pin—especially in IBJJF where points for the pass matter.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal in IBJJF and ADCC. Gi competition historically showcased over-under pressure; no-gi athletes still use it but often prefer body-lock chains under submission-only incentives.

Safety

Do not dump all weight into the neck. Diaphragm pressure is the tool; neck cranks are not. Tap if breathing or a joint feels wrong during drilling.

Common mistakes

  • Holding the shape without tripod pressure.
  • Underhooking too shallow—losing the leg on the first shrimp.
  • Ignoring the far hip so they turn cleanly into you.
  • In no-gi: leaving space for butterfly elevation.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is over-under only a gi pass?

No. It is most famous in gi, but the sandwich structure works in no-gi with deeper underhooks and tighter sprawls.

Over-under vs double-under—what’s the difference?

Double-under puts both arms under both legs (often into a stack). Over-under splits the jobs: one under, one over.

Why do I get swept from over-under?

Your shoulder pressure is late and your hips are between their legs with weight back. Sprawl, lower your base, and drive the shoulder before you walk your knees up.

Can I use over-under from half guard?

Yes—after you free or neutralize the trapped knee enough to build the sandwich. Many pressure passers live there.

What if they frame on my face?

Clear or redirect the frame with head position and shoulder angle; do not abandon the underhooked leg while fixing the frame.

How does this relate to the stack pass?

Both are under-the-legs pressure families. Stack often loads both legs; over-under asymmetrically cages them. See stack pass.

Is Bernardo Faria’s over-under the only version?

It is the most influential modern teaching template, not the only mechanical variation. Concepts transfer across schools.

The Bottom Line

The over-under pass cages the legs—one under, one over—then uses shoulder and tripod pressure to force a side and clear to a pin. Gi pants help; no-gi still works with deeper connections. Compare it with the body lock when you want waist-level instead of under-the-legs pressure.
