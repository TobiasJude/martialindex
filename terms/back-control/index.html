Back Control

Back control is the Brazilian jiu-jitsu position where you ride behind the opponent with hooks (or a body triangle) and a seatbelt grip, hiding your attacks while you hunt the rear naked choke in no-gi or collar chokes in the gi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Hooks + seatbelt + chest connection define sport back control.
  • Primary finish family: rear naked choke (no-gi) and collar chokes (gi).
  • Fall to the underhook side and trap an arm before forcing the choke.
  • IBJJF awards 4 points for stabilized back control with hooks.
  • Turtle is the most common entry highway—and the most common give-up.

What Is Back Control?

Back control is the position where you are behind the opponent with both heels hooked inside their thighs (or a body triangle), chest glued to their back, and usually a seatbelt grip (one arm over the shoulder, one under the armpit) controlling the upper body. In Brazilian jiu-jitsu it is widely treated as the highest-percentage finishing seat because the opponent cannot see your attacks and the rear naked choke sits right there.

IBJJF awards four points for stabilized back control with hooks; ADCC also scores the back highly. The strategic job is to keep the hooks, trap an arm, expose the neck, and finish—or reset control when they hand-fight.

How Back Control Works

Hooks prevent them from turning into you or sliding their hips out; the seatbelt prevents them from peeling your chest off. Falling to the underhook side (the arm under their armpit) uses the mat as a second hook and makes the neck easier to attack. Modern systems (Gordon Ryan / Danaher lineage instructionals) emphasize arm trapping: once one of their wrists is pinned, the rear naked choke becomes far harder to defend.

Chest connection. If your chest separates, they elbow-escape out. Stay glued; move your hips with theirs.

Hand fighting. Before the choke, clear their defensive hands. Two-on-one grips, chin wedges, and trapping an arm with your leg are the usual tools.

Body triangle. Crossing your legs around their waist (figure-four) can replace traditional hooks for long-term control, especially in no-gi, with awareness of IBJJF restrictions on certain compressions and heel positions.

Gi vs No-Gi

Chokes. No-gi lives on the rear naked choke (hadaka jime). Gi adds collar chokes: bow and arrow, clock choke from turtle transitions, cross-collar from the back, and lapel feeds. The seatbelt still matters in both.

Control grips. Gi athletes can fist a collar across the chest or grab pants to freeze hips. No-gi athletes use body locks, wrist control, and the body triangle more often.

Entries. Same families—seatbelt from turtle, arm drag, failed guard passes where they turn away, truck and crucifix variants—but gi lapel drag and collar pulls create extra back-take paths.

Escapes. Bottom players still hand-fight the choking arm and slide hips to the floor. Gi grips on your sleeve can slow the choke; no-gi escapes rely more on raw hand fighting and turning into the underhook side correctly.

What Does Back Control Look Like in Practice?

From turtle, Sam wins a seatbelt, digs the first hook, then the second. Sam falls to the underhook side, traps Jordan's top wrist with a two-on-one, and slides the choking forearm under the chin for a rear naked choke.

Gi. Same back take, but Sam feeds the far collar for a bow-and-arrow when Jordan tucks the chin hard against the RNC.

No-gi. Sam cannot collar. They keep the body triangle, peel the chin, and finish the RNC—or transition to an armbar if Jordan rolls to defend the neck.

Types / Variations

Double hooks + seatbelt

The reference configuration for sport BJJ scoring and most instruction.

Body triangle

Figure-four around the waist for long control; common in no-gi. Watch competition legality details and partner safety on the diaphragm.

Technical back / one hook

When one hook is lost but chest and seatbelt remain—often a moment to reinsert the hook or take mount.

Crucifix

One arm trapped with your legs; neck and far arm exposed. High-percentage choke seat.

Truck / whale back

Entangled leg configurations that can lead to twisters or back exposure—more no-gi / submission-only flavored.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries. Turtle attacks, arm drags from guard, failed passes where they turn away, mount when they roll to their knees, scramble seatbelts from standing clinches.

Bottom escapes. Protect the neck first, clear hooks by sliding hips to the mat toward the underhook side carefully, turn belly-down to turtle only as a calculated trade, hand-fight constantly. Turning the wrong direction into the choking arm is how people get strangled.

Top maintenance. Follow their hips, reinsert hooks early, trap arms, and do not cross your feet in front of their waist where they can footlock you.

Back Control vs Mount

Factor Back Control Mount
Visibility They cannot see your attacks They face you and can frame more easily
IBJJF points 4 for hooks + control 4 for mount
Primary finish Rear naked choke / collar chokes Cross choke, arm triangle, armbar
Escape feel Hand fight + hip slide Bridge + elbow escape
Striking (MMA) Less punching power from back High ground-and-pound threat

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Back control with heels between the thighs scores 4 points in IBJJF after three seconds. Crossing feet in certain ways or trapping both arms can affect scoring. Body triangles are widely used; follow current IBJJF language on illegal compressions. ADCC values the back heavily and finishes often decide matches there.

Safety

Train under qualified instruction. The rear naked choke is a blood choke—tap early. Do not crank the neck. Body triangles can stress the ribs and spine; apply gradually with partners.

Common mistakes

  • Crossing feet in front of their body and getting footlocked.
  • Chasing the choke before trapping an arm, then losing the back.
  • Chest separating when they hip-escape.
  • In the gi: forgetting collar attacks when the chin is tucked.
  • In no-gi: soft seatbelt without wrist control.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Why do I lose the back every time they hand-fight?

You are probably chasing the choke without trapping an arm or keeping chest connection. Secure a wrist, fall to the underhook side, and only then peel the chin.

Hooks or body triangle?

Hooks are the scoring default and easier to teach. Body triangles shine for long control in no-gi once you understand partner safety and competition rules.

Is back control better than mount?

For submissions in sport BJJ and no-gi, often yes—the RNC is that strong. In MMA, mount can be better for striking. Both score four in IBJJF.

Which side should I fall to?

Usually the underhook side (the arm under their armpit), so the mat helps trap that arm and the neck opens. Falling to the overhook side is a common beginner mistake.

Do collar chokes replace the RNC in the gi?

They complement it. When the chin is tucked against bare arms, a bow-and-arrow or other collar attack may finish faster. Keep both toolkits.

How do I take the back from turtle?

Win the seatbelt first, insert hooks one at a time, and expect hand fighting. See the turtle entry for the top and bottom games.

Can I get footlocked from back control?

Yes—if you cross your feet in front of their body. Keep heels inside the thighs or use a controlled body triangle instead.

The Bottom Line

Back control is the behind-the-opponent pin with hooks and a seatbelt: hide your attacks, trap an arm, finish the rear naked choke or a gi collar choke. For the choke itself see rear naked choke; for the common entry fight see turtle.
