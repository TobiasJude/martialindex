Yoko-Geri

Yoko-geri is the side kick: chamber, open the hip, drive sideways with the foot edge or heel—karate’s lateral power kick.

What Is Yoko-Geri?

Yoko-geri (横蹴り) is the side kick. Variants include keage (snap) and kekomi (thrust).

Mechanically related to rooted work in Kiba-Dachi; compare with Ushiro-Geri (back kick) and Mae-Geri.

In Karate kihon it teaches hip orientation and supporting-leg stability.

Martial Index files Yoko-Geri under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Yoko-Geri behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Yoko-Geri Works

Chamber the knee across, turn the hip, extend sideways, strike with sokuto (edge) or heel as taught, retract to chamber, then down.

Supporting foot pivots; eyes look toward the target; hands protect.

Thrust versions emphasize penetration; snap versions emphasize speed and retract.

Mechanically, successful yoko-geri work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, yoko-geri lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how yoko-geri appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill yoko-geri daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Some sport contexts favor mawashi over slow side kicks—train accordingly.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Hold Kiba-Dachi, chamber into yoko-geri slowly, return—root training.

Pad: side thrust kick at body height with a partner bracing the pad.

Dojo picture: yoko-geri appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply yoko-geri after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Common Mistakes

  • Leaning the torso down to “get height.”
  • No chamber.
  • Supporting foot stuck.
  • Looking away from the target.
  • Landing with a soft, collapsed stance.
  • Treating yoko-geri as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill yoko-geri inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so yoko-geri connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether yoko-geri appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Side-on wall drills: keep the shoulder from collapsing toward the wall as you extend.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe yoko-geri as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put yoko-geri behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Karate Stance, Kihon, and Kata into siblings, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for kicks, joint stress in deep stances, and impact training.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep yoko-geri scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on yoko-geri, one situational kumite theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Edge or heel?

Style and variant dependent—sokuto common for many side kicks.

Keage vs kekomi?

Snap vs thrust within yoko-geri.

Why train kiba-dachi?

Root and hip lessons transfer—see Kiba-Dachi.

Is it for sparring?

Yes, carefully; telegraphs if chamber is slow.

Sibling kicks?

Ushiro-Geri, Mae-Geri.

The Bottom Line

Open the hip with honesty—Yoko-Geri rewards chamber discipline and a supporting leg that actually pivots.
