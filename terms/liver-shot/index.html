Liver Shot

A liver shot targets the liver under the opponent’s right ribs—usually via Body Hook or Shovel Hook—and can end exchanges without a head KO.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • High-leverage Body Shot to the right ribs.
  • Setup > raw power.
  • Shovel / tight hook paths preferred.
  • Mix head attacks so elbows rise.

What Is Liver Shot?

Inventory lists no parent; conceptually it lives with Body Shot / Body Hook.

Orthodox attackers usually throw the left hand to the opponent’s right side.

Precision and timing beat macho power; a clean dig beats a swinging miss.

Martial Index files Liver Shot under Boxing so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Boxing style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Liver Shot behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Liver Shot Works

Lower level, drive a compact rising or horizontal hook under their right elbow. Palm position often thumb-up / shovel.

Best windows: after they throw a right hand (elbow lifts), after high guard from head combos, or off a Slip.

Eyes stay on their chest/head—do not stare at the liver.

Mechanically, successful liver shot work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: straights need Slip/Parry awareness; hooks need Bob and Weave literacy; power shots need a clean Power Line.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, liver shot lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill liver shot daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Jab-cross to lift hands, then shovel to liver.

Bernard Hopkins–style jab then liver dig is the textbook patience lesson.

Fight picture: liver shot appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply liver shot after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Telegraphing by looking down.
  • Wide swings that get caught on the elbow.
  • Throwing only liver shots with no head threat.
  • Staying in the pocket after without defense.
  • Treating liver shot as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox liver shot inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so liver shot connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether liver shot appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shell defenses, check hooks, and pull counters.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe liver shot as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Boxing Range, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put liver shot behind heavy boxing pressure; others behind movement and counters. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Boxing Stance, Jab, and Slip into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for head movement and power punching where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “pressure,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Boxing entries keep liver shot scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on liver shot, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for liver shot under your next card (glove size, round length, amateur vs pro judging) so training intensity matches the bout.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Why does it hurt so much?

Liver trauma triggers autonomic shutdown—pain, breath loss, delayed crumple.

Best punch shape?

Often shovel hook or tight body hook.

Southpaw liver?

Southpaw’s rear right can also dig that side depending on angle—train both.

Legal?

Yes if above the belt; referee calls low blows if you stray.

Training safely?

Pads and bag first; controlled sparring—partners need time to recover from clean digs.

The Bottom Line

Hunt the liver with patience and setups—one clean dig can rewrite a round that head hunting could not.
