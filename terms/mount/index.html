Mount

Mount is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu pin in which the top player sits on the opponent's torso facing the head, knees (or one knee and one foot) on the mat, using weight and base to flatten escapes—then isolates arms or the neck for finishes that change between gi and no-gi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Mount puts your weight on the torso with knees posting beside the hips—IBJJF's classic 4-point pin.
  • Primary jobs: flatten the bridge/shrimp, isolate an arm or neck, finish or improve to the back.
  • Gi leans on collar chokes and pants; no-gi leans on underhooks, chest wraps, arm triangles, and mounted triangles.
  • Technical mount and S-mount are the usual finishing geometries once they turn or frame.
  • Compared with side control: more points and different submission angles; less mobility after the pass.

What Is Mount?

Mount is a dominant top position in Brazilian jiu-jitsu in which you sit on the opponent's torso, facing their head, with your knees (or one knee and one foot) on the mat beside their hips and your weight distributed so their hips and shoulders stay pinned. In gym language it is often called full mount when both knees are down and clear of the legs.

In the positional hierarchy, mount sits above side control and knee on belly, and just below or alongside back control depending on rule set and striking context. IBJJF awards four points for a stabilized mount; ADCC scores it lower than back control but still treats it as a major pin.

The strategic job of mount is not "sit and wait." It is to flatten the bottom player, kill the bridge and elbow-escape paths, then isolate an arm or the neck for finishes: armbars, cross chokes (gi), arm triangles, and mounted triangles among them.

How Mount Works

Mechanically, mount works by putting your center of mass over the opponent's hips and chest while your knees act as posts that block hip escape. When they bridge (upa), you ride the bridge by posting a hand and shifting weight rather than being thrown over. When they shrimp for an elbow escape, you keep them flat with head control, a crossface, or a collar grip that turns their face away from the escape side.

Base and posture. Low hips, chest heavy, knees wide enough to stop the shrimp but not so wide that they insert a knee. High, floaty mount invites the upa. Grapevining the legs (wrapping your feet under theirs) can freeze their hips in both gi and no-gi when you need to stall their escape engine.

Arm isolation. Once they are flat, the next layer is separating an elbow from the ribs so you can climb to S-mount, technical mount, or a chest wrap for kata-gatame / arm triangle. Modern no-gi systems emphasize underhook-to-ratchet control; gi systems often start with a deep collar.

Angle. Square, static mount without an angle is easy to survive. Sliding toward technical mount (one knee high by the head, hip heavy on the far side) opens armbars and back-take paths when they turn away.

Gi vs No-Gi

The skeleton of mount is shared; the handles and finishes diverge hard.

Grips. Gi mount thrives on collar grips: a deep cross-collar keeps them flat and sets cross chokes; pants grips help when they try to shrimp. No-gi replaces fabric with underhooks, chest wraps, head position, and figure-four arm wraps. Without a collar, you cannot hang them; you must smother with chest and shoulder.

Submissions. Gi: cross choke, Ezekiel (with sleeve), bow-and-arrow transitions if they turn, classic armbar from S-mount. No-gi: arm triangle (kata-gatame), mounted triangle, armbar, occasional Americana or kimura when an arm is isolated. Collar strangulations disappear; shoulder pressure and head position do more of the work.

Escapes from bottom. Same bridges and elbow escapes, but gi bottom players can use lapel frames and sleeve grips to create space; no-gi bottom players rely on frames, handfighting, and timing the bridge when the top player reaches high for a choke.

Sport incentives. IBJJF gi rewards the four-point mount heavily, so holding and improving mount is often correct. ADCC and MMA change the calculation: back control may be preferred when available, and stalling without progress can be policed.

What Does Mount Look Like in Practice?

After a knee-cut pass, Sam settles in side control, clears the near frame, then steps the far knee over the belly into mount. Jordan bridges hard. Sam posts on the mat, rides the bridge, and drops the hips again: mount retained.

Gi version. Sam feeds a thumb-in collar, redirects Jordan's head away from the escape side, and walks the free hand to the far collar for a cross choke. When Jordan frames the choking arm, Sam switches to S-mount and swings for an armbar.

No-gi version. No collar. Sam gets an underhook, walks the elbow past the shoulder line, settles a single chest wrap, and drives the shoulder into the face to force a panic frame, then transitions to an arm triangle or climbs for a mounted triangle when Jordan pushes.

Types / Variations

Mount is one pin with several useful geometries.

Low mount / grapevine

Hips heavy, knees tight or feet grapevined. Best for shutting down escapes before you hunt submissions. Common control-first home base.

High mount

Knees closer to the armpits. Increases armbar and choke access but raises the risk of being bridged if base is lazy.

Technical mount

One knee up by the head, hip heavy on the far side, often when they turn to their side. Gateway to back takes, armbars, and seatbelt entries.

S-mount

Both knees high, sitting toward the head to isolate an arm for armbars. Classic finishing platform in gi and no-gi.

Mounted triangle / arm triangle

Leg or shoulder configurations that finish from mount without giving up the pin entirely, especially valuable in no-gi.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries. From side control by stepping over; from knee on belly by dropping the posted leg; from half-guard top after clearing the knee; from failed guard passes that land you on the torso.

Bottom counters. Trap an arm and bridge (upa) toward the trapped side; elbow-escape / knee-elbow shrimp once a frame exists; frame the hips and recover half or full guard. Do not push straight up into their chest without trapping an arm; you gift the Americana.

Top maintenance. Kill the bridge by wide base and riding; kill the shrimp by keeping them flat and blocking the near hip; when they turn, take technical mount or the back rather than fighting for square mount forever.

Mount vs Side Control

Both are pins; they solve different problems.

Factor Mount Side Control
Scoring (IBJJF) 4 points when stabilized No points for the pin itself; points come from the pass
Submission access High for chokes and armbars once flat High for kimura, americana, baseball bat (gi), north-south choke
Escape difficulty Hard once flat and grapevined Hard under crossface + underhook; easier if frames return
Mobility Lower; you are committed on the torso Higher; easier to transition to KOB, north-south, mount
Best when You can keep them flat and isolate an arm You need pressure while still mobile after a pass

Many athletes pass to side control first, then climb to mount when frames die. In IBJJF, rushing mount for the four points is often correct; in training, side control can be the better classroom for pressure.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Mount is fully legal and scores 4 points in IBJJF when you are clear of half guard, facing the head, with two knees or one knee and one foot down, stabilized for three seconds. Trapping both arms under your legs can void the score. ADCC also scores mount but values back control more highly. Stalling without submission attempts can draw referee attention in both rule families.

Safety

Train under qualified instruction. From mount, do not crank neck-crank variations of chokes. When drilling armbars, control the fall and tap early. Bottom players should bridge with control; wild bridging into a posted arm risks shoulder injury.

Common mistakes

  • Sitting high without base and getting bridged immediately.
  • Hunting submissions before flattening the hips.
  • In no-gi: reaching for collars that do not exist instead of underhooks and chest wraps.
  • In the gi: ignoring collar control that would freeze their escape.
  • Giving up mount to chase a bad armbar and landing back in their guard.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is mount always better than side control?

Not always. Mount scores more in IBJJF and opens classic finishes, but side control can be heavier, more mobile, and safer while you kill frames. Choose mount when you can keep them flat; stay in side control when they still have strong underhooks and frames.

Why do I keep getting bridged off mount?

Your hips are too high or your knees too narrow. Drop your weight, widen your base, and ride the bridge by posting instead of staying rigid. Grapevining can also freeze their hips while you reset.

Does mount work the same in no-gi?

The pin works; the finishes change. Without collars you build underhooks, chest wraps, and head position for arm triangles and mounted triangles instead of cross chokes.

How long do I need to hold mount to score in IBJJF?

Three seconds of stabilization once you meet the criteria (clear of half guard, facing the head, proper knee/foot placement). Refs will not score a flashy jump-through that never settles.

Should beginners learn mount before back control?

Learn both. Mount teaches weight distribution and escape defense; back control teaches hooks and the rear naked choke. Many curricula introduce mount early because it appears naturally after passes.

What is technical mount for?

It is the geometry you take when they turn to their side—one knee high by the head—so you can attack the arm, take the back, or prevent them from recovering guard.

Can I score mount if I trap both of their arms?

In IBJJF, trapping both arms under your legs typically voids mount points. Keep at most one arm trapped under a leg if you want the score.

The Bottom Line

Mount is the classic torso pin in Brazilian jiu-jitsu: sit on the hips, face the head, flatten the escapes, then finish or take the back. Gi athletes hang on collars; no-gi athletes smother with underhooks and chest wraps. For the adjacent pin after a pass, see side control; for the four-point back alternative, see back control.
