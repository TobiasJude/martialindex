Bridge and Roll

The bridge and roll (upa) escapes mount by trapping an arm and same-side leg, then bridging diagonally to reverse. It shares DNA with the trap and roll and chains into the knee-elbow escape when posts appear.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Trap arm + same-side leg before you explode.
  • Bridge diagonally (10/2 o’clock)—not straight up.
  • Often lands in their closed guard—posture immediately.
  • If they post, switch to knee-elbow / frame-and-shrimp.
  • Engine is the bridge; timing beats raw power.

What Is Bridge and Roll?

Also called the upa: remove posts on one side and launch them over your shoulder line.

Best versus low or mid mount when you can reach an arm; less reliable versus high mount with grapevines—then prefer elbow-knee routes.

Lives in the same family as trap and roll; Martial Index treats bridge-and-roll as the movement label and trap-and-roll as the named sequence.

How Bridge and Roll Works

Protect the neck, bring heels close, and create an initial off-balance so they post.

Trap the posted arm to your chest and hook the same-side foot; plant the free foot.

Bridge explosively toward the trapped side and roll through to top—elbows in, posture up in their closed guard.

Drill the fake: threaten the roll to force a post, then run knee-elbow / frame-and-shrimp on that side.

Competition note: points for mount may already be on the board—your job is survival and reversal, then posture or pass.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve grips make the arm trap sticky; watch collar threats while you set the bridge.

No-gi. Trap the tricep/upper arm; expect slipperier bridges—emphasize foot hook and hip height.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex traps the right arm and foot, bridges to 10 o’clock, and reverses into closed guard top.

Jordan’s bridge is stuffed by a free-hand post; they immediately elbow-escape the posted side and recover half guard.

Types / Variations

Classic bridge and roll

Arm + foot trap, diagonal bridge, reverse to top.

Fake bridge to elbow escape

Force the post, then knee-elbow / shrimp the seam.

One-leg assist

Knee into the back to start hands to the mat before the trap.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Primary entry: bottom mount. Counters: free-limb posts, technical mount, grapevines. Chain with shrimp, frames, and escape hierarchy thinking rather than spamming one move.

Bridge and Roll vs Knee-Elbow Escape

Factor Bridge and roll Knee-elbow escape
Outcome Often reverse to top Recover guard / half
Needs Reachable arm + bridge room Frames + shrimp space
Fails when They post well / sit too high No seam—need power shrimp or bridge first
Chain Fake into elbow escape Fake into upa

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal escape under IBJJF and ADCC-style events; verify current scoring language for mount points already awarded.

Safety

Bridge with control—clear grapevines before exploding; don’t neck-crank yourself under heavy mount.

Common mistakes

  • Bridging straight up.
  • Forgetting to trap the leg.
  • Landing without posture in their guard.
  • Never chaining to knee-elbow when posts appear.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is bridge and roll?

The upa mount escape: trap arm and leg, bridge diagonally, reverse to top.

Same as trap and roll?

Closely related—Martial Index cross-links both; trap-and-roll names the sequence, bridge-and-roll stresses the engine.

When does it fail?

When they post free limbs or sit too high with grapevines.

What then?

Knee-elbow / frame-and-shrimp the posted side.

Where do I land?

Often in their closed guard—posture or pass immediately.

Gi tip?

Sleeve trap helps secure the arm.

Related movement?

Bridge as the engine; shrimp as the follow-up.

The Bottom Line

Bridge and roll is the diagonal reverse—trap, launch, posture—and it only stays reliable when you chain to frame-based escapes the moment posts appear.
