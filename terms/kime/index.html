Karate · Concept

Kime

Kime is the focused finish: a brief, whole-body commitment at the end of a strike, kick, or block so power and intent arrive together.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Kime is a timed finish.
  • Relax–accelerate–focus–recover.
  • Visible in kihon and kata grading.
  • Not the same as staying stiff.

What Is Kime?

Kime (決め) relates to “decide” / focus. In Karate, it is the crisp completion of a technique—not endless muscle clamping.

Searchers conflate kime with stiffness. Good kime is timed: relax → accelerate → focus → release into recovery and Kamae.

Kime shows in Kihon, Kata, and the finishing identity of punches like Gyaku-Zuki.

It pairs with Hikite: structure and pull set the shot; kime is the decision at the end.

Martial Index files Kime under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Kime behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Kime Works

Align feet, hips, torso, and striking limb so force has a path. Breath often ends with a short expiration at impact.

Tension is momentary. Holding a clenched pose after the hit is posing, not kime.

In kata, kime makes embusen techniques readable for graders and judges. In kumite, “kime” may mean decisive scoring intent under rules.

Train slow to feel alignment, then add speed without losing the finish.

Mechanically, successful kime work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, kime lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how kime appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill kime daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Judges reward decisive technique presentation; dojo culture may emphasize quiet focus over loud theatrics.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

A Choku-Zuki that stops with hips square, fist closed, and body quiet for a beat—then recovers.

A soft kata with drifting limbs: same moves, no kime—grades fail on “lack of focus.”

Dojo picture: kime appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply kime after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Constant full-body tension (fatigue, slow technique).
  • No finish—techniques fade out.
  • Kiai without body alignment.
  • Looking at the floor at impact.
  • Confusing kime with brutality.
  • Treating kime as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill kime inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so kime connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether kime appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Alternate three slow kime punches with three fast ones—finish quality must match.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe kime as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put kime behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Karate Stance, Kihon, and Kata into siblings, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for kicks, joint stress in deep stances, and impact training.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep kime scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on kime, one situational kumite theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for kime under your next card (WKF vs full-contact, target levels, protective gear) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is kime in karate?

Focused completion of a technique—alignment and intent at the end.

Is kime just tensing muscles?

Brief focus, not permanent rigidity.

How do I know I have kime?

Coach feedback, sound of gi, stillness at impact, and partner pad feel.

Does kime apply to blocks?

Yes—blocks finish too, then often flow to counters.

Can you have kime without power?

Alignment can be present at low intensity; power grows with speed and mass transfer.

The Bottom Line

Chase clean Kime on boring reps—Hikite, hips, and breath—so Kata and Kumite inherit a finish that reads as real.
