Roundhouse Kick

The roundhouse kick is Muay Thai’s rotational kick family—shin through the target with full hip turn, not a snapped foot tap.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Shin is the bat; hips are the engine.
  • Pivot the plant foot hard.
  • Thai arc follows through—see {thai-roundhouse}.
  • Targets: leg, body, head.
  • Checks and catches answer it.

What Is Roundhouse Kick?

Roundhouse kick is the umbrella for curved kicks that swing the shin into the opponent. In Muay Thai it usually means the Thai Roundhouse pattern: long arc, hip rotation, shin contact.

Child/sibling pages: Low Kick, Body Kick, High Kick, Switch Kick, and the dedicated Thai Roundhouse mechanics page.

Karate/TKD roundhouses often snap with the foot; Thai habit drives through with the shin like a bat.

Martial Index files Roundhouse Kick under Muay Thai so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Eight Limbs map when you need the full weapon system; keep the focus on how Roundhouse Kick behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Roundhouse Kick Works

Step/open the lead foot, pivot plant heel toward the target, rotate hips, swing shin, pull same-side arm, keep opposite hand high, follow through, recover.

Higher targets need more pivot and posture control; lower kicks allow a flatter plant and drop of weight.

Setups: behind Jab/Teep (Push Kick), after feints, or as counters when they plant.

Mechanically, successful roundhouse kick work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Muay Thai Stance with hands alive.

If Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into elbows or clinch threats you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Kick Check awareness; pocket strikes need Long Guard or tight covers; clinch tools need posture.

Stadium vs Gym / Rules Notes

Under traditional stadium Muay Thai, roundhouse kick lives inside a full Eight Limbs rule set—elbows, knees, and clinch time change how often you invest in this tool.

Amateur cards, kickboxing crossovers, and some promotions restrict elbows or clinch duration. Gym culture may still drill roundhouse kick daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit.

Confirm the bout agreement before importing stadium frequency into restricted rules or MMA, where wrestling reframes clinch incentives.

Scoring emphasis varies. Some judges still reward hard kicks and balance; others read more like kickboxing. Let the card’s eyes shape volume.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Jab, rear body roundhouse, reset.

Check their kick, return low roundhouse.

Feint low, go high.

Fight picture: roundhouse kick appears when Range and Timing say so, then yields to the next limb in the Eight Limbs chain.

Gym test: can you apply roundhouse kick after a Jab feint (or a teep feint) and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Kicking with the foot.
  • No plant pivot.
  • Hands down.
  • Snapping back with zero follow-through when you wanted Thai power.
  • Telegraphing with a huge wind-up.
  • Treating roundhouse kick as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox roundhouse kick inside full rounds; return to Muay Thai Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up (punch, kick, or clinch) so roundhouse kick connects to Eight Limbs.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so Timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether roundhouse kick appears in the right Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for elbows, flying knees, and catch-heavy games.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe roundhouse kick as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Range, or a stance that collapses when kicks and clinch enter the round.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put roundhouse kick behind heavy boxing; others behind teeps and body kicks; stadium-oriented rooms may emphasize clinch delivery. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Teep (Push Kick), Thai Clinch (Plum), and Muay Thai Stance into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for elbows, knees, and catch-kick dumps where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “clinch,” “guard,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Muay Thai entries keep roundhouse kick scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on roundhouse kick, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where Timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for roundhouse kick under your next card (elbows allowed? clinch time? scoring bias?) so training intensity matches the bout, not a generic Instagram Muay Thai.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Shin or foot?

Muay Thai prefers shin for power and durability.

Why pivot?

Without pivot, hips lock and power caps.

Same as kickboxing?

Overlap; Thai versions often emphasize shin-through and clinch follow-ups.

Condition shins?

Progressive bags/pads—do not bone-smash cold.

Best defense?

Kick Check, catch, evade, or jam with teep.

The Bottom Line

Treat roundhouse as a family: learn Thai rotation once, then aim it at legs, body, or head with the right risk.
