New Wave Jiu-Jitsu

New Wave Jiu-Jitsu is the Austin-based system and team identity associated with coach John Danaher and athletes such as Gordon Ryan after the 2021 split of the Danaher Death Squad—known for systematic no-gi instruction, wrestling-up priorities, and integrated passing and leg-lock games.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Post-2021 Austin system/brand descending from DDS coaching culture—not a synonym for all modern no-gi.
  • Emphasizes structured positional curricula, wrestling-up, and body-lock / connection passing.
  • Continues the leg-lock revolution lineage while expanding stand-up and passing systems.
  • Sibling concepts: DDS, modern passing, 10th Planet.
  • Encyclopedia entry: history and influence—not a join-this-gym CTA.

What Is New Wave Jiu-Jitsu?

New Wave Jiu-Jitsu names the coaching project and competitive identity that coalesced in Austin, Texas, after the 2021 dissolution of the Danaher Death Squad. In encyclopedia terms it is both a system label—Danaher's continuing curriculum of interconnected positions—and a team brand associated with athletes who stayed under that coaching umbrella (most prominently Gordon Ryan).

It is not a synonym for every modern no-gi gym, nor a franchise network like 10th Planet or Alliance. Treat "New Wave" as a historically specific coaching lineage that extends DDS-era systems into stand-up wrestling, body-lock / connection passing, and refined lower-body attacks.

How New Wave Systems Work

Instructional culture emphasizes long positional curricula: isolate a problem (pass a seated guard, wrestle up from turtle, finish from the saddle), then chain adjacent positions rather than collecting orphan techniques. Public-facing themes include wrestling-up preferences over prolonged guard pulling, body-lock and headquarters-style passing, and heel-hook / ashi-garami control before breaking.

Parent pillars on Martial Index: bottom structures still live under guard; top systems under guard passing. Cross-bridges to heel hook, 411, and 50/50 remain central.

Gi vs No-Gi

No-gi. Primary public arena—ADCC-style rulesets reward wrestling, body locks, and rotational leg locks. New Wave discourse is overwhelmingly no-gi.

Gi. Principles (connection, positional hierarchy, control before submit) transfer; collar-sleeve and lapel games diverge. Do not assume a New Wave no-gi playbook maps 1:1 onto IBJJF gi.

What Does New Wave Look Like in Practice?

Alex faces a seated guard. Instead of hovering in a toreando, Alex closes distance into a body lock, clears legs methodically, and pins—then only looks for upper-body submissions. From bottom, when smashed, Alex wrestles up to feet rather than fishing forever for a De La Riva recover.

Gi. Same connection idea with pants and lapels; finishing menus shrink under IBJJF heel-hook bans.

No-gi. Expect heel hooks from saddle / SLX / 50/50 as primary finishing trees.

Types / Variations

Standing & wrestling-up

Standing curricula and "wrestle up" from turtle / half as a deliberate meta—see also wrestling for BJJ and takedown.

Passing systems

Body-lock, J-point / headquarters, and chained pressure—overlaps modern passing, pressure passing, and connection passing.

Leg-entanglement game

Continued systematization of ashi families, saddle, and heel hooks—see leg lock revolution.

Influence, Counters, and Context

Influence. Instructional volume and ADCC performances made New Wave vocabulary (body lock passing, wrestling-up) common gym talk even outside Austin.

Counters. Guard players answer with K-guard, reverse De La Riva, and aggressive entanglements; passers outside the system still use speed and toreando games.

Context. Parallel Austin culture (B-Team) shares DDS DNA with different gym norms—compare historically, do not flatten into one brand.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Most New Wave highlight reels assume ADCC / submission-only legality. IBJJF gi restricts heel hooks and many reaps—check IBJJF rules and ADCC rules before copying competition footage.

Safety

Rotational leg locks injure knees quickly. Tap early; drill with experienced partners; follow coach policy on heel hooks.

Common mistakes

  • Treating New Wave as a personality cult rather than a positional curriculum.
  • Copying body-lock pressure without hip-switching details.
  • Importing ADCC finishes into IBJJF gi without reading the rulebook.
  • Ignoring stand-up until purple belt, then expecting overnight wrestling.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is New Wave the same as DDS?

No. New Wave is the post-2021 Austin system/team identity; DDS was the earlier NYC/PR training group that split.

Do I need to train at New Wave to learn the system?

No. Instructionals and seminar culture spread many ideas; local coaches teach similar positional frameworks without the brand.

Is New Wave only leg locks?

No. Public memory starts with leg locks, but modern curricula stress wrestling-up and systematic passing as much as finishes.

How does New Wave relate to B-Team?

Both descend from DDS culture after the 2021 split; they are separate Austin projects with different gym cultures—not one brand.

Does New Wave work in the gi?

Principles transfer; many signature finishes do not under IBJJF gi rules. Train the rule set you compete in.

What should beginners study first?

Positional hierarchy, frames, and safe tapping culture—not advanced heel-hook entries.

Is this a gym advertisement?

No. Martial Index documents systems and history; we do not recruit for any academy.

The Bottom Line

New Wave Jiu-Jitsu is the Austin-era continuation of Danaher-system coaching after the DDS split: wrestling-up, connection passing, and refined leg locks. Read it as history and systems literacy—then train under qualified coaches in the rule set you actually compete under.
