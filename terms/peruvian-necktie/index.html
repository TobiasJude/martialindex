Peruvian Necktie

The Peruvian necktie is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu front-headlock choke finished by throwing a leg over the opponent’s back while you pull the head—sibling to the guillotine and Japanese necktie.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Front headlock first; the leg is the finishing lever.
  • Works gi and no-gi when head-and-arm control is tight.
  • Cluster with guillotine, darce, and Japanese necktie.
  • If they posture hard, return to guillotine options.
  • Tap early—shin-on-neck pressure can spike fast.

What Is Peruvian Necktie?

The Peruvian necktie is a front-headlock strangle: control the head (often with an arm), create an angle, then throw the near leg over the upper back so the shin helps drive the head down while the arms pull.

It lives next to guillotine entries and the Japanese necktie. Without a real front headlock it is easy to reverse.

Pedagogically treat it as a finish inside front-headlock systems—not a standalone flying trick. In MMA-adjacent rooms it shows up after sprawls and failed shots.

How Peruvian Necktie Works

Secure a tight front headlock with posture broken. Sit or circle to the choking-arm side, throw the near leg high over the back so the shin crosses the neck/upper back, then connect legs and finish by extending hips while curling the arms.

Connection. Keep their forehead painting toward the mat; tall posture kills the lever.

Finish. Arms pull the chin/neck line while the leg drives down—three-point compression beats raw squeeze.

If the leg cannot clear, return to guillotine geometry instead of inventing a crank. Chain thoughtfully rather than muscling the neck.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Collar assists and sleeve grips can help set the front headlock; finish mechanics stay the same.

No-Gi. Primary home: wrist/head ties, arm-in guillotine structures, and sprawls into front headlock.

Both contexts reward the same order: control the head, then add the leg. Do not skip connection for a highlight-reel throw.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

After a failed shot, Alex sprawls into a front headlock. Jordan turtles; Alex sits to the hip, throws the near leg over Jordan’s back, locks the feet, and finishes as Jordan tries to stand.

If Jordan posts hard and the shin misses, Alex retightens the guillotine instead of forcing a crank—catalog discipline over ego finishes.

Types / Variations

Classic shin-across finish

Shin over the upper back/neck with locked feet—the standard teaching model for academies that include neckties.

Arm-in / guillotine link

Enter from arm-in guillotine structures when they refuse to release the neck fight and leave space for the leg.

Angle and hip-sit variants

Small hip sits change whether the shin clears cleanly versus sliding off the shoulder into a scramble.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: sprawls, front headlock from failed shots, guillotine scrambles, and turtle neck rides. Chain to guillotine if the leg cannot clear, or to Japanese necktie when the angle differs.

Counters: posture before the leg lands, roll through, extract the head, or circle out of the front headlock early.

Escapes mid-finish: hand-fight the choking arm, turn into the pressure, relieve the shin line—and tap if carotids load.

Peruvian Necktie vs Japanese Necktie

Factor Peruvian Necktie Japanese Necktie
Primary lever Leg over back + arm pull Different arm/shoulder geometry
Feel Shin-driven compression Angle-driven cousin
Common home Sprawl / turtle front headlock Front headlock systems
Sibling bridge Guillotine family Guillotine / darce bridges

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Front-headlock chokes including necktie finishes are generally treated as legal strangles when they are not illegal neck cranks as defined by the event. Always confirm your rule set; some promoters police cranky neckties more strictly than clean chokes.

Safety

Apply gradually. Shin-on-cervical pressure can injure. Emphasize the choke path and release immediately on tap. Do not chase flying entries without control.

Common mistakes

  • Throwing the leg without a real front headlock.
  • Sitting flat with no angle—easy to posture out.
  • Abandoning guillotine options when the leg fails.
  • Cranking the neck instead of compressing carotids.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is a Peruvian necktie?

A front-headlock choke finished by throwing a leg over the opponent’s back while pulling the head.

Is it the same as a Japanese necktie?

No—related front-headlock family, different finishing geometry.

Gi or no-gi?

Both. No-gi sprawls are classic; gi collars can help entries.

How do I defend it?

Posture early, prevent the leg from clearing, extract the head, or roll through.

What if they keep the guillotine after I pass?

Do not hang on forever—von Flue danger appears.

Is it a blood choke?

Often hybrid neck compression; tap early.

When should I learn it?

After basic guillotine and front-headlock control.

The Bottom Line

Peruvian necktie is the leg-over finish of the front-headlock game: lock the head, clear the shin, pull and drive. Keep it wired to guillotine and Japanese necktie.
