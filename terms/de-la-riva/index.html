De La Riva Guard

De La Riva is an open guard built around an outside hook on a standing opponent’s leg. Learn to recognize the hook, understand its supporting grips, and distinguish it from reverse De La Riva.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 21 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • An outside hook around the near leg identifies classic DLR.
  • The hook needs supporting grips and a free-leg connection.
  • Off-balancing is a reaction, not a completed sweep.
  • Gi and no-gi use different grips around the same leg relationship.
  • Inversion is optional; learn to retain and recover the position first.

What Is De La Riva Guard?

De La Riva (DLR) is an open-guard position in which the bottom player wraps a leg around the outside of the opponent’s near leg. An ankle or heel grip commonly supports that connection. The free leg and remaining hand help manage distance and upper-body movement. The hook identifies the position; the complete grip configuration determines which attacks are available.

How De La Riva Works

Think in terms of three connections: the outside hook, control of the near leg, and a second point of contact that manages distance. A hook by itself does not prevent the opponent from turning or stepping away. Watch how the passer changes their stance and how the bottom player adjusts their hips. An off-balance creates a reaction; it is not yet a completed sweep.

DLR and spider guard can appear in the same exchange. DLR describes the leg hook; spider describes sleeve-and-foot control on an arm. Recognizing those different controls is more useful than treating every guard name as a separate, fixed pose.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve, collar, and permitted trouser grips offer different ways to connect the upper body to the leg hook. Changing a grip changes the attack; a collar-and-sleeve entry is not identical to a double-sleeve entry.

No-gi. The outside hook remains possible, but fabric grips are unavailable. Leg and upper-body controls must be established differently. Do not assume that a gi sequence transfers unchanged when the jacket is removed.

What Does De La Riva Look Like in Practice?

In a cooperative drill, the passer stands with one leg forward. The bottom player identifies the outside hook and the grip controlling that leg. When the passer steps back or turns, pause and identify which connection was lost first. Restore the position before adding a sweep taught by the coach. This separates recognizing DLR from successfully attacking with it.

Types / Variations

Classic DLR hook

An outside hook supported by control of the near leg. Identify what the free hand and foot are doing before selecting an attack.

Collar-sleeve DLR (gi)

Combines the outside hook with collar and sleeve control. The grip pair adds upper-body influence; it does not remove the need to manage the leg.

Reverse De La Riva

A related but distinct hooking position, often encountered when a passer brings a knee between the legs. Compare the leg relationship in the reverse DLR entry.

Waiter / DLR hybrids

A separate under-the-leg control that can connect to DLR. Treat the transition and its grips as an additional lesson, not a synonym for the basic hook.

Entries, Counters, and Passes

Entry. Look for an accessible near leg while retaining an open-guard frame. The position may emerge as a passer stands; it does not require an inversion.

Top player. Clearing the hook, freeing the controlled leg, and moving around the remaining frames are separate tasks. Removing one hook is not automatically a completed pass.

Bottom player. If the hook is cleared, recover a usable frame or transition to another guard. Avoid forcing the same hook while your knee and hips are being turned out of alignment.

De La Riva vs Spider Guard

Factor De La Riva Spider Guard
Primary tool Outside hook on the lead leg Feet on biceps with sleeve grips
Best against Standing passers Standing or kneeling with sleeve access
Gi dependency Useful in both; richer in gi Heavily gi-dependent
Common next Berimbolo, RDLR, SLX Lasso, triangle, sweep to mount

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Check the current rules for your event, age, belt, and gi or no-gi division. A familiar guard name does not establish whether every connected grip or submission is permitted. The official rules resources are linked below.

Safety

Train under qualified instruction. Inversions need clear mats. Tap early if DLR entries become heel hooks in no-gi.

Common mistakes

  • Relying on the hook while letting the controlled leg move freely.
  • Keeping the hips fixed while the passer changes stance.
  • Starting an inversion before understanding the available controls.
  • Treating every step or post as a completed sweep.

Check your understanding

Can you point to the outside hook, identify the grip controlling the same leg, and explain what the free foot is doing? If one connection disappears, name the frame you would recover before considering an attack.

FAQ

Questions about identifying the position and reviewing it after class.

Is De La Riva only a gi guard?

No. The outside leg hook can be used in no-gi, but fabric-based grips and the sequences built around them need different controls.

How is DLR different from reverse DLR?

They use different relationships between the bottom player’s hooking leg and the passer’s leg. Reverse DLR is commonly encountered around a knee-cut position; it is not simply the same hook with a new name.

Do I need to invert to learn DLR?

No. Learn to identify and maintain the outside hook first. An inversion or berimbolo is a separate skill and is not part of the definition.

Why does the passer keep stepping out?

Check the near-leg grip, your hip position, and your free-leg connection. A coach can identify which connection fails first; pulling harder on the hook alone may not solve it.

Does an off-balance count as a sweep?

No. Making a partner step or post demonstrates a balance reaction. Completing and scoring a sweep requires more; consult the rules used by your event.

What should I study next?

Read open guard for the parent concept, then compare spider and collar-sleeve control. Study one coached sweep from your chosen grip configuration before adding several transitions.

The Bottom Line

De La Riva is an open guard built around an outside hook on a standing opponent’s leg. Learn to recognize the hook, understand its supporting grips, and distinguish it from reverse De La Riva.
