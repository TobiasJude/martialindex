Collar-Sleeve Guard

Collar-sleeve guard pairs a collar grip with sleeve control in gi open guard. The grips describe the upper-body connection; your feet and hip angle determine the rest of the position.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 21 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • One collar grip and one sleeve grip define the hand configuration.
  • The leg frames or hooks must be described separately.
  • The grips influence posture and an arm; the legs manage distance.
  • A De La Riva hook is optional, not part of the definition.
  • Learn one configuration before collecting unrelated attacks.

What Is Collar-Sleeve Guard?

Collar-sleeve guard is an open-guard configuration with one hand controlling the collar and the other a sleeve. It is not a single fixed arrangement of the legs. A foot may frame a hip or upper arm, or a leg may form a hook such as De La Riva. Specify both the grip pair and the leg configuration when describing what you see.

How Collar-Sleeve Guard Works

The collar connection helps influence upper-body posture, while sleeve control limits how freely one arm can withdraw or post. Your legs supply frames and hooks that manage the space between both bodies. The grips cannot do those jobs alone. If the passer clears a foot or changes their stance, you may need to adjust your hips or replace a frame even while both hands retain their grips.

Different collar-sleeve systems emphasize different leg positions and reactions. For example, Mikey Musumeci’s inside-control curriculum separates establishing control from responses to posture and submission entries. That is a useful distinction when organizing your own class notes.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. The position is named for its fabric grips. Cross-collar and same-side collar configurations are not interchangeable; follow the exact grip pairing being taught.

No-gi. There is no collar or sleeve to hold. Wrist, arm, and head controls can influence posture, but they form different positions with different escape routes.

What Does Collar-Sleeve Guard Look Like in Practice?

The bottom player retains a collar and sleeve grip while one foot frames the hip and the other contacts an upper arm. The passer changes posture. Before attempting an attack, identify whether the bottom player still controls distance and whether the sleeve-controlled arm can post. If a foot is cleared, practise recovering a frame rather than treating the remaining hand grips as complete control.

Types / Variations

Basic collar-sleeve control

Name the collar side, controlled sleeve, and both foot contacts. These details define the configuration more precisely than the grip pair alone.

To DLR / reverse DLR

The grip pair can combine with leg hooks when the opponent’s stance permits. Check the hook’s path rather than treating DLR and reverse DLR as interchangeable.

Sweeps and back takes

Attacks depend on posture, base, and available space. Learn the entry for the chosen sweep or back take as a separate sequence.

Passing collar-sleeve

Identify the frame or grip restricting the intended direction. Clearing a connection creates an opportunity; the passer still needs to move beyond the remaining legs.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entry. Connect the grip pair to an open-guard position with space for your legs to work. Do not assume that obtaining the collar alone establishes the guard.

Top player. Address the grip or frame that is restricting the movement you want. There is no universal rule that the collar must always be stripped first.

Bottom player. Replace a lost frame or adjust your angle before forcing an attack. Transitions to spider or DLR are options when the required contacts are available, not mandatory next steps.

Collar-Sleeve vs Spider

Factor Collar-Sleeve Spider
Primary grips Collar + sleeve Both sleeves + foot-on-biceps
Feel Collar-and-sleeve influence on the upper body Extension + sleeve tension
What to identify Collar side, controlled sleeve, and leg frames Sleeve control and foot-on-arm contact
Hook pairing Hip or arm frames; optional leg hooks Lasso / spider feet

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Check the current rules for your event, age, belt, and gi or no-gi division. A familiar guard name does not establish whether every connected grip or submission is permitted. The official rules resources are linked below.

Safety

Do not crank necks with collar grips in drilling. Tap early to gi chokes set from the same grips.

Common mistakes

  • Remembering the grips but not the foot placements taught with them.
  • Holding both hands fixed while the passer clears the leg frames.
  • Assuming the collar grip must always be broken first.
  • Moving to a named attack before identifying the opponent’s reaction.

Check your understanding

Can you name both hand grips and both leg contacts without using only the phrase “collar-sleeve”? Then explain which connection manages distance and which limits an arm’s ability to post.

FAQ

Questions about identifying the position and reviewing it after class.

Is collar-sleeve only for lightweight competitors?

No. The name describes grips, not a weight class. The useful distance and configuration depend on the partners and the lesson.

Is collar-sleeve the same as spider guard?

No. Collar-sleeve names the hand grips; spider names a sleeve-and-foot connection on an arm. A position can combine elements of both.

Can I use collar-sleeve in no-gi?

Not literally: both named grips require a jacket. Learn no-gi body controls as distinct connections rather than direct fabric-grip substitutes.

Which grip should the passer break first?

It depends on which grip or frame blocks the intended movement. Review the specific configuration instead of assuming one sequence fits every exchange.

Do I need a De La Riva hook?

No. Collar-sleeve can use other leg frames, including a foot on the hip or upper arm. A DLR hook is one possible combination.

What should I write down after class?

Record which hand held which grip, where both feet were, and the partner’s reaction. Those details distinguish one collar-sleeve lesson from another.
