Bullfighter Pass

The bullfighter pass is the classic English name for the toreando pass (torreando)—an outside guard passing method that redirects the legs like a matador’s cape. See toreando variations, slash, and speed passing.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Bullfighter = toreando / torreando naming.
  • Outside pass: redirect legs, step around, settle.
  • Sibling pages: toreando-pass, torreando-variations, slash.
  • Works gi and no-gi with grip changes.
  • Fails when you retreat instead of circling forward.

What Is Bullfighter Pass?

Bullfighter is gym English for toreando: control both legs (pants, ankles, or shins), push them aside, and circle to side control or north-south.

It is a foundational outside pass under guard passing, often taught before complex body-lock systems.

Martial Index keeps both toreando-pass and this bullfighter page so searchers using either name land in the cluster.

How Bullfighter Pass Works

Grip the pant legs near the knees or control ankles/shins no-gi. Posture tall with base wider than the hips.

Push the legs as a unit to one side while your lead foot steps the opposite way—hips face the new angle, chest stays over the clear path.

If they recompose, switch sides (the classic bullfighter dial) or fold into knee cut / stack.

Do not back straight up—retreating recreates their frames. Circle and pressure the far hip.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Pant grips are the textbook entry; sleeve grips help stop collar drags.

No-gi. Cup heels or pin shins with palms; expect more wrestling-style sprawls.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex grips both pants, bullfighters to the right as the bottom player reaches for spider, and lands in side control.

Jordan’s first bullfighter is stuffed; they immediately reverse direction into a knee-cut headquarters.

Types / Variations

Basic pant-grip bullfighter

Two pant grips, hip twist, step around.

Ankle / shin bullfighter

No-gi cups or low grips for mobile open guard.

Bullfighter into knee-cut / stack

When the cape fails, fold to knee cut or stack.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries after opening closed guard or vs seated open guard. Counters: De La Riva, lasso, inversion. Escapes: shrimp to recover hooks before the pass settles.

Bullfighter vs Pressure Pass

Factor Bullfighter / toreando Pressure / over-under
Path Outside circle Through or over the legs
Tempo Often faster Often slower, heavier
Key risk Recompose / collar drag Triangles / frames
See also Variations Over-under

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Pass points require clearing the legs and stabilizing per the current book—verify IBJJF / event criteria.

Safety

Control the dump—don’t drive necks into the mat when they invert under a bullfighter.

Common mistakes

  • Backing up instead of circling.
  • Leaving the second leg free.
  • Bent posture inviting sweeps.
  • No plan when the first side fails.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is bullfighter the same as toreando?

Yes—bullfighter is the common English nickname for toreando / torreando.

Gi or no-gi first?

Gi pant grips are easiest to learn; transfer the circle to no-gi shin control.

Why do I get swept?

Usually broken posture or backing up into hooks.

What if they invert?

Follow the hips, don’t chase the feet; switch to stack or disengage safely.

How does it relate to slash?

Slash is a related outside-cutting idea; toreando/bullfighter is the classic named family.

Next pass to learn?

Knee cut and body lock so you can chain when the outside path stalls.

Where is the pillar?

Guard passing—link early and often.

The Bottom Line

The bullfighter pass is toreando by another name—redirect the legs, circle with posture, and chain when the cape is caught.
