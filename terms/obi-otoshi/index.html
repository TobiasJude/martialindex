Obi Otoshi

Obi otoshi is the belt drop: a Kodokan Te-Waza that uses belt-side control to break and drop uke—gi-dependent, uncommon in modern highlight reels, essential for catalogue literacy. Compare Kata Guruma.

What is Obi Otoshi?

Obi otoshi (帯落 / belt drop) is a Kodokan hand technique that throws by controlling around the belt line and dropping uke—literally an obi story. It is less common as a modern World Tour tokui-waza than seoi or uchi-mata, which is exactly why encyclopedia pages exist: catalogue judo is wider than Instagram judo.

Martial Index places obi-otoshi under Nage-Waza and lists it on Te-Waza. Compare Kata Guruma for shoulder-wheel loading and Sukui Nage for scooping actions that also interact with lower grips historically.

Because the throw names the belt, it is inherently Judogi-shaped. Self-defense and classical curricula keep it alive; contest athletes may meet it mainly in kata, demonstrations, or rare opportunistic moments.

Searchers often ask whether belt grips are legal. Modern IJF gripping rules regulate where and how you hold—including restrictions and interpretations around belt and below-belt actions over the years—so coach this throw inside current Judo Rules, not folklore.

How it works

Create kuzushi that lets the belt-side control matter—often forward or slightly cornered balance loss. Hands and posture lift/redirect while the belt grip helps remove uke’s base, then the drop finishes the throw. Exact classical hand shapes vary by school; the shared idea is belt-involved drop rather than a pure koshi sweep.

Do not confuse a belt grab stall with an attack. Positive throwing purpose must appear quickly. If the grip is illegal in your current rule set, do not drill it as contest default—study it as classical literacy or adapt to legal controls.

When the throw partials, keep chest contact for Osaekomi. Scores follow ordinary Ippon / Waza-Ari criteria when the form is legal.

Safety: belt-line wrestling can torque lumbar spines—communicate with partners and avoid yanking from dead postures.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

Without a belt and jacket, “obi-otoshi” ceases to be itself. No-gi players may study related body locks and dumps under other names. Transfers: the idea of controlling the waistline. Does not transfer: the Japanese belt-drop name as a no-gi brand.

Gi training should include legal grip checks under your federation’s current interpretations before contest use.

What it looks like in practice

Expect obi-otoshi more in kata/demo contexts than in every club’s weekly tokui-waza rotation. That rarity is not unreality—it is distribution. On the judo hub, keep classical te-waza visible so athletes inherit the whole art.

In Randori under strict IJF senior rules, only attempt forms your coach clears as legal. Historical drill blocks can be labelled as such on the whiteboard.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter when belt-side control is already meaningful—do not dive for the obi from nothing. Counters: peel belt grips, posture up, circle. Combinations are uncommon; treat neighbouring te-waza as the practical chain.

Uchi-komi should be cooperative and slow until partners trust the lumbar load.

Compare

Versus Kata Guruma: belt drop versus shoulder wheel. Versus Sukui Nage: scoop versus belt-drop emphasis. Versus Uki Otoshi: floating hand drop without belt naming.

IJF notes & safety

Obi-otoshi as classical technique must be reconciled with current gripping regulations. Direct below-belt hand attacks have been heavily restricted in IJF senior competition since 2010; do not assume every classical belt action is World Tour legal. Verify with coaches and Judo Rules. This page is literacy—not a license to break gripping bans.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Treating any belt grab as automatic obi-otoshi.
  • Ignoring current gripping bans.
  • Yanking the lumbar spine from a flat start.
  • Skipping classical literacy because the throw is rare on TV.
  • No ukemi plan.

Training notes

Teach obi-otoshi in a classical te-waza block beside Sukui Nage and Uki Otoshi. Label contest-legal vs historical clearly.

If your club never shows classical te-waza, athletes inherit a thinner judo—use Martial Index to widen the map.

Study notes

Belt-line control appears in many arts; judo’s naming makes the obi explicit. That explicitness is educational even when contest frequency is low—athletes learn to think about waist connection as a balance tool, not only a jacket sleeve war.

When federations clarify belt-grip interpretations, update your lesson plan the same week. Classical names do not freeze refereeing gestures in amber—see live Judo Rules notes from your national body.

Pair obi-otoshi literacy with Judogi fitting and belt-tying culture so beginners understand why the belt is equipment, not decoration.
