Body Triangle

The body triangle is a rear back-control configuration that figure-fours your legs around the opponent’s torso—pinning hips and ribs so you can climb for the rear naked choke or isolate arms without relying only on foot hooks.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Body triangle replaces (or upgrades) hooks when you need torso immobilization.
  • Prefer the underhook-side orientation for choking angles; know how to switch when crushed.
  • Too-low triangles invite hip escapes; too-high triangles lose hip control—find the sweet spot.
  • Gi and no-gi both use it; gi may add belt/lapel assists while locking.
  • Link with seatbelt and hooks literacy—not a standalone trick.

What Is Body Triangle?

From the back, a body triangle threads one leg across the midsection and locks your figure-four behind their hip or lower ribs. Compared with two hooks, it trades some mobility for crushing control—harder for them to hip-escape, easier for you to wait out hand fighting.

It is a staple of modern no-gi back hunting and remains legal and common in gi when locked safely above illegal crushing zones. Martial Index nests it under the back control pillar beside seatbelt grips and hook mechanics.

How Body Triangle Works

Insert hooks first or slide a single hook deep, then throw the second leg across and lock heel-to-thigh (or equivalent figure-four) without crossing ankles in a loose “grapevine only” that they can peel.

Keep the lock high enough to control hips but not so high that you only squeeze air under the armpits. Use the seatbelt to choose strong-side vs weak-side and climb the choke.

If they crush toward the triangle lock, post, underhook, and rotate them back toward your preferred choking side—or unlock to hooks and retake the angle rather than stubbornly dying on a bad side.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Belt grips and lapel seatbelts can stabilize while you lock; be mindful of illegal crushing interpretations—verify current rules.

No-gi. Pure leg geometry and chest-to-back connection; expect longer hand-fighting cycles before the RNC seats.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Priya locks a body triangle on the underhook side, waits out two-on-one defense, then slides the RNC blade under the chin.

Chris gets crushed to the overhook side, unlocks to hooks, switches hips, and relocks on the better angle.

Types / Variations

Underhook-side body triangle

Preferred for many RNC entries—chest free to climb.

Overhook-side body triangle

More defensive for the attacker; often a transition to switch sides.

High / armpit body triangle

Extra shoulder pin for armbars from back—watch hip escape risk.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries: from traditional hooks, from technical mount rolling to back, or after a failed RNC when they expose midsection space. Counters: wedge inside the lock, peel the figure-four, or rotate to smash the locking hip. Escapes: inside wedge, continuous rolling escapes, and peeling the seatbelt first so you can turn in.

Body Triangle vs Double Hooks

Factor Body triangle Double hooks
Control type Torso figure-four crush Hip hooks + mobility
Escape difficulty Often harder to hip-escape Easier to clear one hook
Mobility for attacker Lower Higher
Best for Stalling hand fights, RNC climbs Dynamic back takes & transitions

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Body triangles are generally allowed; some crushing/neck-pressure behaviors can draw penalties depending on placement. Always verify the current IBJJF and ADCC (or event) rulebooks for division, belt, age, and gi vs no-gi. Martial Index gives orientation—not invented illegal-move lists.

Safety

Do not crank lumbar spines. If a partner reports lower-back pain, unlock immediately. Tap early when breathing is restricted.

Common mistakes

  • Locking too low across the belt line and calling it “control.”
  • Crossing ankles loosely instead of a true figure-four.
  • Refusing to unlock when crushed on the wrong side.
  • Abandoning the seatbelt while adjusting legs.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is body triangle better than hooks?

Different tools: triangle immobilizes; hooks allow freer hip movement. High-level players use both.

Does it count as back control points?

Scoring depends on hooks/position definitions in the current rulebook—verify; control quality still matters for riding time culture.

Can it injure the back?

Poorly placed triangles can stress the lumbar spine—prioritize safe height and partner feedback.

Gi legal?

Generally yes; verify any crushing/neck interaction language annually.

How do I switch sides?

Post, underhook, unlock or roll, then relock on the underhook side.

Good for beginners?

Learn hooks first, then add body triangle as an upgrade.

Link to armbar from back?

High triangles and climbing angles often open armbar from back when chokes are defended.

The Bottom Line

Body triangle is back-control immobilization: lock the torso, keep the seatbelt, and climb the RNC—or unlock and reset rather than dying on a crushed angle.
