What Is An Chagi (Inside Crescent)?

An chagi (안차기) is the inside crescent kick commonly drilled opposite outside crescents.

It sweeps inward across the centerline on a circular path.

Compare with Bakkat Chagi (Outside Crescent): naming marks direction; both differ from Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick).

Appears in basics and some pattern applications more than as a PSS workhorse.

How An Chagi (Inside Crescent) Works

Raise and arc the foot inward across the target, controlled hip turn, soft landing into stance.

Do not let the swinging leg yank you off the supporting foot.

Coordinate breath and hands so the kick is not a solo leg trick.

Match form heights when performing Poomsae.

Mechanically, successful an chagi (inside crescent) work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, an chagi (inside crescent) is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill an chagi (inside crescent) daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Mirror drill: partner holds a center mitt; you alternate inside and outside crescents.

Forms lab: isolate the crescent section of a pattern until facing stays true.

Fight picture: an chagi (inside crescent) appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply an chagi (inside crescent) after a feint and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Slapping with a floppy ankle.
  • Twisting the base knee inward unsafely.
  • Rushing past the target line.
  • Confusing names with bakkat.
  • Using crescents as random sparring spam.
  • Treating an chagi (inside crescent) as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe an chagi (inside crescent) as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor distance, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

An vs bakkat?

Inside vs outside crescent—train as a pair (Bakkat Chagi (Outside Crescent)).

Safe for beginners?

Yes at controlled heights with coaching.

Sport use?

Limited compared with Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick) and Cut Kick (Taekwondo).

Striking surface?

Often sole/instep edge—confirm school cue.

Relation to axe?

Crescents arc across; Naeryeo Chagi (Axe Kick) chops down.

The Bottom Line
