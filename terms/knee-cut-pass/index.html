Knee Cut Pass

The knee cut pass (also called the knee slice or cross-knee through) is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu guard pass in which the top player pins a leg, drives one knee across the bottom player’s near thigh, and clears to side control—with different grips, pressure, and failure modes in gi versus no-gi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • The knee cut drives one knee across the bottom player’s thigh to split the guard and settle into side control or north-south.
  • Success depends on pinning the far hip/leg, winning inside position, and finishing with chest-to-chest pressure—not just “sliding the knee.”
  • Gi games add collar, sleeve, and pants grips; no-gi leans on underhooks, head position, wrist control, and body locks.
  • Common failure: knee on the thigh without clearing the knee line, inviting underhooks, half guard, or a reverse de la Riva recovery.
  • Pairs naturally with the toreando, body lock, and over-under in modern passing chains.

What Is the Knee Cut Pass?

The knee cut pass is a foundational top-game skill in Brazilian jiu-jitsu. From a standing or kneeling approach to open or half guard, the passer pins one of the bottom player’s legs, places the near knee across that thigh (the “cut”), and drives hips and chest forward until the legs are cleared and side control is established. Gyms also call it the knee slice, knee through, or cross-knee through. It sits inside the broader guard passing family.

Unlike a pure smash pass that folds the guard under heavy chest pressure, the knee cut is a splitting pass: it uses the shin and knee as a wedge between the bottom player’s legs while upper-body control stops the reguard. It is legal and ubiquitous in IBJJF gi and no-gi, and it remains a high-percentage option in ADCC-style rulesets when chained with body locks and leg pins.

Pedagogically, the knee cut sits next to the toreando as one of the first “around or through the legs” passes white and blue belts learn—then reappears at black belt as a headquarters system with dozens of entries and finishes.

How the Knee Cut Works

Mechanically, the knee cut solves three problems in order: control the legs, gain upper-body connection, and finish past the knee line.

Leg control. If the bottom player’s knees stay glued to their chest, there is nothing to cut. The passer first separates the knees—by gripping pants or shins in the gi, or controlling ankles/shins and driving weight in no-gi—until one thigh becomes a platform for the cutting knee. Headquarters (one knee between the legs, the other outside) is the most common staging position.

Upper-body connection. A floating knee cut gets underhooked. Once the cutting knee is on the thigh, the passer must win a crossface, underhook, collar drive, or head-and-arm connection so the bottom player cannot sit up into the passer’s hip. Keeping the opponent flat (or at least denying the underhook) is often more important than how deep the knee is.

Finish. Clearing the knee line means the cutting knee and hip slide past the bottom player’s near hip until chest-to-chest side control (or a high underhook / north-south) replaces the half-guard entanglement. If the bottom player locks half guard mid-cut, the pass becomes a half-guard passing problem—not a failed identity of the move.

Gi vs No-Gi

The skeletal motion of the knee cut is shared. The handles, timing, and retention fights are not.

Grips. In the gi, pants grips on the far shin and a collar or sleeve on the upper body let you pin the far hip while you cut. Cross-collar pressure can replace a naked crossface. In no-gi, those fabric anchors disappear: you use shin rides, wrist control, underhooks, head position, and often a body lock or hip clamp to stop the shrimp.

Speed vs pressure. Gi knee cuts can be slower and more positional because fabric slows the scramble. No-gi knee cuts often arrive from headquarters after a toreando threat or a failed body lock, and the bottom player’s hip escape is faster—so finishing weight on the hip matters more.

Retention meta. Gi bottom players answer with lasso, spider, and collar-sleeve frames that stall the cut before it starts. No-gi answers lean on knee shields, reverse de la Riva, K-guard, and underhooks the moment the cutting knee floats. Your “same” knee cut needs different hand fights in each rule set.

Half-guard consequences. When the cut stalls into half guard, gi passers often use crossface-and-underhook smash passes or lapel weaves. No-gi passers more often switch to body-lock half-guard passing or back-step to attack the legs—especially under rules that allow heel hooks.

What Does the Knee Cut Look Like in Practice?

Two athletes are rolling after a failed guard pull. Jordan stands in front of Alex’s open guard. Jordan grips both shins, separates the knees, and steps into headquarters: right knee between Alex’s legs, left knee outside.

Gi version. Jordan takes a pants grip on Alex’s far shin and a cross-collar on the near side, pins Alex’s far hip to the mat, and slides the right knee across Alex’s left thigh. Alex frames on the biceps. Jordan clears the frame with a shoulder drive, hips follow the knee, and the pass finishes in side control with the crossface already installed.

No-gi version. Same headquarters, no pants. Jordan controls Alex’s near wrist, posts a forearm across the jawline, and cuts the knee while sprawling the opposite hip so Alex cannot underhook. If Alex locks half guard, Jordan switches to a body lock instead of forcing a floating cut—and either finishes the half-guard pass or back-steps into a safer top position.

Both versions show the definition: split the legs with the knee, connect the upper body, clear the knee line. The jacket only changes how you pin and how long you can stall mid-cut.

Types / Variations

Headquarters to knee cut

Headquarters is the classroom home of the knee cut: one knee inside, one outside, hips heavy. From here you can cut, long-step, or switch to a smash. Most “knee cut systems” are really headquarters systems with a preferred finish.

Long-step / back-step finish

When the bottom player’s knee shield or frame stops a straight cut, passers long-step or back-step the free leg around the head/hips to change the angle—often landing in north-south or a reverse half. The cut created the opening; the long-step finished it.

Vs knee shield and RDLR

Against a knee shield, the cut may start as a smash on the shield or a hip switch that clears the frame before the knee slides through. Against reverse de la Riva, clearing the hook and winning the near hip usually come before any knee cut attempt—otherwise you cut into an entanglement.

Entries, Counters, and Retention

Entries. From standing open guard after separating the knees; from a toreando that forces the bottom player flat; from half guard when you free the trapped knee and reset to headquarters; from a failed body lock when you still own inside position.

Bottom counters. Early: keep knees to chest and fight grips. Mid-cut: underhook the cutting side, shrimp to recover half or closed closed guard, or invert to RDLR/K-guard. Late: if the knee has cleared, fight for frames and recover before the crossface settles—or accept side control and escape from there.

Top contingencies. If they underhook, abandon the floating cut and flatten or switch passes. If they lock half, treat it as half-guard passing. If they invert, do not chase the knee cut into a leg entanglement you did not plan to enter.

Knee Cut vs Toreando

These are the two beginner passes that define modern open-guard top play—and they solve different geometry problems.

FactorKnee CutToreando
PathThrough / across the near thighAround the legs toward the head/hips
StagingHeadquarters and hip pinsShin/pants grips while circling
Best whenYou can pin a hip and win upper-body connectionYou can keep their knees away from their chest
Gi biasPants + collar make the pin stickyPants grips excel at circling pressure
No-gi biasNeeds underhooks/head control to finishNeeds shin rides and fast hip connection
Failure modeStalls in half guard or gets underhookedFrames return; you get pulled into closed or SLX

Strong passers do not pick one forever. A toreando that meets a stiff frame often becomes a knee cut; a knee cut that meets a flexible invert often becomes a toreando or leg drag. See the full toreando pass entry for the circling side of the pair.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

The knee cut is fully legal in IBJJF gi and no-gi. No special age or belt restrictions attach to the pass itself. In ADCC and submission-only formats it remains central; the surrounding chain often includes body locks and, where legal, lower-body entries if the pass stalls into an entanglement. Stalling rules matter more than legality: hovering in headquarters without progress can draw referee attention in some no-gi events.

Safety

Train under qualified coaches. Avoid driving the cutting knee into the bottom player’s face or throat while adjusting. When the pass becomes a stack, do not crank the neck to “force” the finish—change angle or reset. Partners on bottom should tap to positional pressure only if something is wrong with a joint or breath; this is a pass, not a submission.

Common mistakes

  • Cutting the knee with no far-hip pin—so the bottom player simply turns and recovers.
  • Floating the hips; inviting the underhook that kills the pass.
  • Stopping when half guard locks instead of switching to a half-guard plan.
  • In no-gi: reaching for collar habits that do not exist.
  • In the gi: ignoring pants grips and wondering why the cut feels slippery.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is the knee cut the same as the knee slice?

Yes in most gyms. Knee cut, knee slice, and cross-knee through describe the same family: drive a knee across the near thigh and clear to side control. Instructors may emphasize different grips or finishes, but the geometry matches.

Why does my knee cut always end in their half guard?

You cleared part of the thigh but not the knee line, and they locked the outside leg. Pin the far hip harder before you cut, keep chest pressure, and have a half-guard passing follow-up—body lock, smash, or back-step—ready when they catch you.

Does the knee cut work in no-gi?

Yes. Replace pants and collar with shin control, underhooks, head position, and often a body lock. The cut still works; the hand fight changes. Many modern no-gi passers build entire games around headquarters knee cuts chained with toreando threats.

Should beginners learn knee cut or toreando first?

Either works if your coach has a clear system. Knee cut teaches hip pressure and side-control finishes; toreando teaches movement and grip separation. Most programs introduce both within the first year because they bail each other out.

How do I stop getting underhooked mid-cut?

Do not float. Lower your hips, win the crossface or underhook first, and cut only when their near shoulder cannot sit up into your armpit. If they already have the underhook, abandon the floating cut and flatten or switch passes.

Is the knee cut good against closed guard?

Not directly. You must open closed guard first—standing, stack, or hip pressure—then establish headquarters. The knee cut is an open/half-guard pass, not a closed-guard opener by itself.

What do I do if they invert or throw RDLR during the cut?

Do not force the same line. Clear the hook, reclaim the near hip, or switch to a toreando/leg-drag path. Cutting blindly into an invert is how passers gift entanglements.

The Bottom Line

The knee cut pass is BJJ’s classic splitting pass: pin a hip, drive one knee across the near thigh, connect the upper body, and clear to side control. Gi grips make the pin sticky; no-gi demands underhooks and head control. Learn it next to the toreando, and keep a half-guard plan for when the cut stalls.
