Jab-Cross-Hook

Jab-cross-hook is the spoken form of the One-Two-Three: lead straight, rear straight, lead hook under the Combination umbrella.

What Is Jab-Cross-Hook?

Searchers asking for jab-cross-hook want the mechanics of linking three fundamentals without memorizing numbers yet.

It sits beside One-Two-Three and under Three-Punch Combo. In Boxing, coaches may switch labels mid-round.

Parent: Combination. Compare with One-Two-Three.

Martial Index files Jab-Cross-Hook under Boxing so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Boxing style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Jab-Cross-Hook behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Jab-Cross-Hook Works

Link Jab → Cross → Lead Hook with continuous hip rotation and a high rear hand on the hook.

Keep Boxing Range mid-length: too far and the hook claws air; too close and convert the third shot to a Short Hook or Uppercut.

Use a Feint jab sometimes so the cross-hook arrives against a committed Parry.

Mechanically, successful work with Jab-Cross-Hook is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: straights need Slip/Parry awareness; hooks need Bob and Weave literacy; power shots need a clean Power Line.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, jab-cross-hook lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill jab-cross-hook daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Bag work: step-jab, cross in place, hook with lead pivot, then step offline.

Sparring: throw it when their right hand drops after catching your jab.

Fight picture: jab-cross-hook appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply jab-cross-hook after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

All head

Default teaching version—three head shots, equal or accelerating rhythm.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives sparring pressure, not only pad stations.

Third shot body

Third punch digs Body Hook / liver line—see Jab-Cross-Body Hook.

After a slip exit

Common Mistakes

  • Telegraphing the hook with a big arm swing.
  • Stopping feet during the three punches.
  • Ignoring the rear-hand guard.
  • Treating jab-cross-hook as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox jab-cross-hook inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so jab-cross-hook connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether jab-cross-hook appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shell defenses, check hooks, and pull counters.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe jab-cross-hook as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Boxing Range, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put jab-cross-hook behind heavy boxing pressure; others behind movement and counters. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Boxing Stance, Jab, and Slip into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for head movement and power punching where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “pressure,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Boxing entries keep jab-cross-hook scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on jab-cross-hook, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for jab-cross-hook under your next card (glove size, round length, amateur vs pro judging) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Jab-cross-hook vs one-two-three?

Same combo; names vs numbers.

Can southpaws throw it?

Yes—mirror: lead jab, rear cross (left), lead (right) hook from Southpaw Stance.

Best finish?

Often another cross or an angle exit—see 1-2-3-2.

The Bottom Line

Call it jab-cross-hook or one-two-three—the skill is the same: three punches, one idea, clean exit.
