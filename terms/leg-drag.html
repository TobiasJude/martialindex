Leg Drag

The leg drag is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu guard pass that pulls one of the bottom player's legs across their body, pins the hip, and clears the path to side control or the back—blending speed-passing redirects with sticky control.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Drag the leg across + pin the hip—do not just shove and run.
  • Stops re-hooks better than a naked toreando when you connect.
  • Gi pants/sleeve grips help; no-gi uses wrist and shin control.
  • Loose drag gets inverted—keep their knee offline and your chest heavy.
  • Family: speed passing; cousin: toreando.

What Is Leg Drag?

The leg drag is a guard pass that moves one of the opponent's legs across their centerline and pins it so they cannot easily recover hooks. Your hips and chest then clear to the outside for side control or ride toward the back. It sits inside the broader guard passing family.

How Leg Drag Works

Control a sleeve or wrist and the near leg, pull the leg across, staple or pin the hip/knee, and step your near leg past. Keep their far hooks dead by staying heavy on the dragged leg. Finish by connecting to the torso.

Bottom player's job. Frame early, invert or hip-escape before the drag pins, and reclaim De La Riva or butterfly hooks.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Pants and sleeve grips make the drag sticky and slow.

No-gi. Use wrist control, underhooks, and shin staples; expect faster scrambles.

MMA context. Keep posture so you are not kicked while dragging.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex grips Jordan's sleeve and ankle, drags the near leg across Jordan's body, pins the hip, and settles into side control before hooks return.

Gi. Pants grip freezes the dragged ankle.

No-gi. Alex staples the shin with his own shin and chest.

Types / Variations

Classic leg drag

From open guard: drag, pin, clear.

From reverse De La Riva

Common modern entry when they invert or hook your lead leg.

Leg drag to back

When they turn away from the pin, insert hooks.

Leg drag to side control

Default finish—stabilize past the legs.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries. Standing vs seated open guard; from failed toreando; from reverse DLR battles.

Counters. Long frames, early inversion, recovering the dragged leg, forcing you upright.

Failure mode. Dragging without a hip pin—they re-hook instantly.

Leg Drag vs Toreando

Factor Leg Drag Toreando
Leg control Drag + pin one leg Circle both legs aside
Connection Often heavier on the hip More upright circling
Back exposure Common if they turn Less automatic
Gi bias Pants/sleeve sticky Sleeve toreando classic

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal. Pass scores when legs are cleared and position stabilized.

Safety

Do not torque the knee sideways while dragging. Control partners on the dump to side control.

Common mistakes

  • Shoving the leg without pinning the hip.
  • Soft chest—getting inverted.
  • No plan when they turn (miss the back).
  • In no-gi: empty hands while dragging.
  • Celebrating before side-control criteria.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is a leg drag pass?

A pass that pulls one leg across the body, pins the hip, and clears to side control or the back.

Leg drag vs toreando?

Leg drag pins a dragged leg more tightly; toreando circles both legs with more upright movement.

Does leg drag work in no-gi?

Yes—with wrist and shin control replacing pants grips.

How do you stop a leg drag?

Frame early, free the dragged leg, and re-hook before their chest pins your hip—see guard retention.

Can you take the back from leg drag?

Yes—when they turn away from the pin, follow with hooks and a seatbelt.

Common entries to leg drag?

From open guard standing, reverse De La Riva scrambles, and failed toreandos.

Is leg drag a speed or pressure pass?

It sits between: speed entry with sticky hip control—often taught inside speed-passing systems.

The Bottom Line

The leg drag clears open guard by dragging a leg across and pinning the hip—finish to side control or the back. See toreando and speed passing.
