Overhand Right

An overhand right is the orthodox Overhand: rear fist looping over the top—cousin to the Cross, enemy of the lazy Haymaker.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Orthodox rear Overhand.
  • Arc with structure, not panic.
  • Set it—do not spam it.
  • Miss recovery matters as much as the hit.

What Is Overhand Right?

Parent: Overhand. Handedness label for orthodox fighters’ rear loop; southpaws throw an overhand left.

Lands when opponents lean in, jab with a low right hand, or shell flat-footed.

Must stay tighter than a bar-fight swing.

Martial Index files Overhand Right under Boxing so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Boxing style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Overhand Right behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Overhand Right Works

From Boxing Stance, dip/load slightly, rotate, and arc the rear fist over the opponent’s lead. Chin tucked behind the shoulder.

Often set by Jab, Feint, or after slipping outside.

Recover immediately—overhands miss big when they miss.

Mechanically, successful overhand right work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: straights need Slip/Parry awareness; hooks need Bob and Weave literacy; power shots need a clean Power Line.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, overhand right lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill overhand right daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Jab to occupy, overhand over their jab elbow.

Against short fighters who walk in with a high peek-a-boo, time the overhand as they step.

Fight picture: overhand right appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply overhand right after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Full {haymaker} wind-up.
  • Eyes and chin up on the swing.
  • Throwing it every time you feel tired.
  • No plan if it misses.
  • Treating overhand right as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox overhand right inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so overhand right connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether overhand right appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shell defenses, check hooks, and pull counters.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe overhand right as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Boxing Range, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put overhand right behind heavy boxing pressure; others behind movement and counters. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Boxing Stance, Jab, and Slip into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for head movement and power punching where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “pressure,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Boxing entries keep overhand right scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on overhand right, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for overhand right under your next card (glove size, round length, amateur vs pro judging) so training intensity matches the bout.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Overhand right vs cross?

Cross is straight; overhand arcs. Different defensive reads.

Overhand vs haymaker?

Overhand is trained with stance and recovery; haymaker is the wild, telegraphed version.

Is it only a KO punch?

It can score and disrupt even when it does not finish.

Southpaw equivalent?

Overhand left from southpaw-stance.

Defense vs overhand?

Slip, step in, high guard angles, or pull-counter themes.

The Bottom Line

Throw the overhand right as a trained arc under the Overhand pillar—powerful when timed, reckless when it becomes a habit loop.
