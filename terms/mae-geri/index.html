Mae-Geri

Mae-geri is the front kick: chamber the knee, strike with the ball of the foot, retract fast—karate’s centerline kick.

What Is Mae-Geri?

Mae-geri (前蹴り) is the front kick. In Karate it is usually among the first kicks taught in Kihon.

Compare with Mawashi-Geri (roundhouse) and Yoko-Geri (side). Defense often meets it with Gedan-Barai.

Keage (snap) vs kekomi (thrust) variants matter for training emphasis.

Martial Index files Mae-Geri under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Mae-Geri behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Mae-Geri Works

From Zenkutsu-Dachi or Neko-Ashi-Dachi, lift the knee, keep the supporting leg stable, extend the kick, strike with koshi (ball of foot) as taught, then retract the chamber before stepping down.

Hips square or slightly adjusted by style; hands stay in Kamae—do not drop both arms to “help” the kick.

Retraction is part of the technique; leaving the leg out invites counters.

Mechanically, successful mae-geri work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, mae-geri lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how mae-geri appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill mae-geri daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Target legality varies by ruleset; groin kicks may be banned in sport.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Line kihon: step mae-geri down the floor with steady chin and hands.

Kumite: teep-like check with mae-geri to manage distance before Kizami-Zuki.

Dojo picture: mae-geri appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply mae-geri after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Keage (snap)

Snap emphasis—speed and retract.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives kumite pressure, not only line kihon.

Kekomi (thrust)

Thrust emphasis—penetration with a stronger push.

Common Mistakes

  • Kicking with the toes (injury).
  • Leaning back excessively.
  • No chamber—leg swings from the floor.
  • Hands down.
  • Planting without retract.
  • Treating mae-geri as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill mae-geri inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so mae-geri connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether mae-geri appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Slow chamber holds for 2 seconds before extending—kills lazy swing habits.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe mae-geri as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put mae-geri behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for kicks, joint stress in deep stances, and impact training.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep mae-geri scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on mae-geri, one situational kumite theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Ball of foot or heel?

Commonly ball (koshi) for mae-geri; follow your style.

Snap vs thrust?

Keage vs kekomi—both are mae-geri family.

How to stretch safely?

Dynamic warm-ups and progressive range—avoid ballistic cold splits.

Best stance to learn from?

Stable Zenkutsu-Dachi or Neko-Ashi-Dachi under coaching.

Common block?

Gedan-Barai and checks.

The Bottom Line

Chamber, drive, retract—own Mae-Geri so distance control in Kumite is a kick, not a hope.
