Narae Chagi

Narae chagi is an advanced twin/scissor kicking expression in Taekwondo—built on Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick) literacy and often seen in demos and highlight Kyorugi (Sparring) moments.

What Is Narae Chagi?

Narae chagi (나래차기) is commonly described as a twin or “wings” kicking action—both legs involved in a scissor/turn family display.

It showcases timing, air sense, and hip snap beyond basic single turning kicks.

Compare with Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick): narae is the flashy relative, not the first teach.

Related highlight culture overlaps with Tornado Kick in spectator appeal.

Martial Index files Narae Chagi under Taekwondo so searchers get definition, mechanics, WT/ITF context, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Taekwondo style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Narae Chagi behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Narae Chagi Works

From motion, chain a turning-kick idea with a second kick action so both feet participate in the attack phrase; exact school definitions vary—follow your instructor’s technical standard.

Eyes track the target; do not spin blind.

Land balanced; falling after a highlight can cost penalties in sport.

Build from clean single Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick) volume first.

Mechanically, successful narae chagi work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, narae chagi is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill narae chagi daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Demo team: narae chagi for crowd reaction between patterns.

Advanced class: slow-motion breakdown of the scissor timing before speed.

Fight picture: narae chagi appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply narae chagi after a feint and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Attempting it before basic turning kicks are reliable.
  • Ignoring landing.
  • Copying social media without spotting.
  • Using it as ego spam in club sparring.
  • Confusing naming across schools.
  • Treating narae chagi as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowkick narae chagi inside full rounds; return to Taekwondo Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so narae chagi connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether narae chagi appears in the right range band for Kyorugi (Sparring) or forms context.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for spinning kicks, axe kicks, and PSS-targeted Palding (Trunk Scoring) work.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe narae chagi as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor distance, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put narae chagi behind heavy Olympic pressure kicking; others behind traditional Poomsae precision. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Taekwondo Stance, Ap Chagi (Front Kick), and Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick) into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for spinning kicks and head-height targets where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “roundhouse,” “cut kick,” and “axe.” Martial Index’s Taekwondo entries keep narae chagi scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Muay Thai articles that share casual English labels.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on narae chagi, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for narae chagi under your next card (WT vs ITF, PSS vs paper scoring, headgear) so training intensity matches the bout.

What does narae literally suggest?

Often glossed with wing/twin imagery—schools differ on exact technical checklists.

Prereq kicks?

Same as tornado?

No—see Tornado Kick for the 360-style turning kick relative.

Safe for kids?

Only with age-appropriate progressions and mats.

Score value?
