Speed Passing

Speed passing is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu guard-passing style that clears the legs with movement, timing, and redirects—toreando, leg drag, and long-step families—rather than prolonged chest smash.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Move their legs, change sides, and connect before they re-hook.
  • Classic tools: toreando, leg drag, long step, agile knee cuts.
  • Gi grips fuel classic speed systems; no-gi uses wrist control and athleticism.
  • Speed without connection gets inverted—still pin the hips at the end.
  • Compare: pressure passing.

What Is Speed Passing?

Speed passing is the agile, often upright branch of guard passing that uses circling, grip breaks, and side changes to clear open-guard hooks. It is associated with toreando systems and modern long steps—not with slow smash pressure.

How Speed Passing Works

Control sleeves or wrists, move the legs offline, and step past before hooks return. Redirects matter: if one side dies, flip to the other. Finish by connecting to the hips so retention cannot reset.

Bottom player's job. Long frames, angled hips, and early re-hooks—see guard retention.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Sleeve grips make toreando and long step high-percentage.

No-gi. Harder without sleeves—use wrist rides, shin staples, and athletic toreando variants; body-lock hybrids appear more.

MMA context. Upright speed passing must respect upkicks—do not lean empty-handed into legs.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Alex grips Jordan's sleeves, bullfights the legs aside with a toreando, and drops to side control before hooks return.

Gi. Dual sleeve control throughout.

No-gi. Alex redirects off wrist control and a shin staple.

Types / Variations

Toreando speed

Bullfighter circling—see toreando pass.

Leg-drag speed

Drag a leg across and pin—see leg drag.

Long-step speed

Long outside step past hooks—see long step pass.

Chain passing / redirects

Switch sides and pass families when retention answers the first attempt.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries. Standing vs seated/supine open guard; after grip dominance; when pressure paths stall.

Counters. Frames, De La Riva recovers, inversion, and forcing the passer to plant.

Failure mode. Over-circling without ever connecting to the torso.

Speed vs Pressure Passing

Factor Speed Pressure
Posture More upright More chest-heavy
Key skill Redirects + grips Connection + smash
Best vs Loose open guards Stubborn frames / half
Risk Inversion if empty Gas / stall warnings

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal. Speed passes still need stabilization for IBJJF pass points.

Safety

Control partners when dumping them to the side; avoid wild knee torques on planted legs.

Common mistakes

  • Running around without grips.
  • No hip connection at the finish.
  • Predictable single-direction toreando.
  • In no-gi: copying gi sleeve patterns blindly.
  • Leaning into upkicks in MMA.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is speed passing?

An agile guard-passing style using movement and redirects—toreando, leg drag, long step—rather than prolonged smash.

Do I need to be athletic to speed pass?

Athleticism helps, but grip strategy and timing matter more than raw sprint speed.

Best speed passes for beginners?

A basic toreando and a simple leg drag teach the redirect idea safely.

Speed passing in no-gi?

Yes, with wrist control and shin staples replacing sleeves—expect more connection hybrids.

Why do I get inverted when I speed pass?

You circled empty without controlling legs or posture. Keep grips and do not dive chest-first into hooks.

How do open guards stop speed passing?

Long frames and early re-hooks—study guard retention against around-the-legs passes.

Should I only speed pass?

No. Mix with pressure and connection so retention specialists cannot specialize against one tempo.

The Bottom Line

Speed passing clears open guards with movement and redirects—then still must connect to the hips. See toreando, leg drag, and pressure passing.
