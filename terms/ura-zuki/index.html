Ura-Zuki

Ura-zuki is karate’s rising close-range punch—an uppercut-like tool when you are too near for a long straight.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Close-range rising punch.
  • Different path than choku-zuki.
  • Needs cover and leg drive.
  • Style details vary—ask your sensei.

What Is Ura-Zuki?

Ura-zuki (裏突き) refers to a punch with a rising/palm or knuckle orientation depending on style—commonly taught as an uppercut-like close-range hit.

Compare with Choku-Zuki (straight) and Gyaku-Zuki (reverse straight). In Karate curricula it fills the short-range pocket.

Searchers want palm-up vs vertical fist details—follow your ryu’s specification.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Ura-Zuki behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Ura-Zuki Works

Bend the elbow more than a long straight; drive upward into the target with legs and hips; keep Kamae cover with the other hand.

Works after slips, clinch-adjacent moments (where allowed), or when a partner’s elbows create a center lane for a rise.

Still finish with intent and recover—do not leave your head on the centerline admiring the shot.

Mechanically, successful ura-zuki work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, ura-zuki lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how ura-zuki appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill ura-zuki daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Sport legality depends on targets and contact rules—confirm before using head ura-zuki in sparring.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Pad drill: partner holds a pad at solar plexus height; you fire ura-zuki from close Kamae.

Kihon: alternate Choku-Zuki and ura-zuki to feel path differences.

Dojo picture: ura-zuki appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply ura-zuki after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Body ura-zuki

Body-level rising punch—common teaching target.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives kumite pressure, not only line kihon.

Head ura-zuki

Toward the chin—high risk/reward; control in the dojo.

Common Mistakes

  • Wide looping haymaker path.
  • No leg drive—arm-only scoop.
  • Exposing the head while watching the punch.
  • Using ura-zuki from too far away.
  • Ignoring {hikite}/cover on the other hand.
  • Treating ura-zuki as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill ura-zuki inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so ura-zuki connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether ura-zuki appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Three-range pad round: long choku, mid gyaku, close ura.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe ura-zuki as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put ura-zuki behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Karate Stance, Kihon, and Kata into siblings, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for kicks, joint stress in deep stances, and impact training.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep ura-zuki scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on ura-zuki, one situational kumite theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for ura-zuki under your next card (WKF vs full-contact, target levels, protective gear) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is ura-zuki an uppercut?

Often taught that way; exact fist orientation is style-specific.

When do I use it?

Close range when straights are smothered.

Kata appearances?

Varies by kata/style; more common in kihon sets and applications.

Safer training tip?

Pads and controlled partner work—chin targets need supervision.

Related straights?

Choku-Zuki, Gyaku-Zuki.

The Bottom Line

Keep Ura-Zuki for the pocket—when Choku-Zuki is too long, rise with structure and return to Kamae.
