Toreando Pass

The toreando pass (bullfighter pass) is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu guard pass in which the top player controls the bottom player’s legs—usually at the shins or pants—and circles around them toward the head or hips to clear the guard, with different anchors and tempo in gi versus no-gi.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Toreando means circling around the legs while controlling shins, ankles, or pants—not smashing through them.
  • Keep their knees away from their chest; once knees return to the torso, the circle stalls.
  • Gi pants grips make the classic toreando sticky; no-gi uses shin rides, hip posts, and faster hip connection.
  • Chains into leg drag, knee-on-belly, and knee cut when frames appear.
  • Failure mode: upright posture with loose grips, getting pulled into closed guard or seated entanglements.

What Is the Toreando Pass?

The toreando—Portuguese for a bullfighter’s cape work—is an around-the-legs guard pass. The top player grips the bottom player’s legs (pants in the gi; shins/ankles in no-gi), pushes the knees apart or aside, and circles toward the head or far hip until the legs are cleared and a pin (side control, knee-on-belly, or north-south) is established.

It is one of the first standing passes taught in sport BJJ because the geometry is readable: control the legs, move your body around them, land heavy. At higher levels it becomes a threat that forces the bottom player to frame—creating openings for the knee cut, stack, or leg drag rather than a single-path finish.

Fully legal in IBJJF and ADCC contexts; the pass itself carries no special restrictions.

How the Toreando Works

Separate and pin the legs. If both feet stay on your hips with knees tight to the chest, there is no circle. Push shins outward or pin one knee while you move.

Circle with posture. Low hips, elbows pinched, weight driving into the grips so the bottom player cannot re-center. Tall, loose toreando is how you get swept or pulled into closed guard.

Connect the hip. The pass is not finished when you are “past” the feet; it is finished when your hip and chest meet theirs and frames are collapsed. Many “failed” toreandos are actually incomplete hip connections.

Gi vs No-Gi

Grips. Gi toreando thrives on pants grips below the knee plus optional sleeve control. No-gi replaces fabric with shin clamps, ankle control, and hand posts on the hips.

Tempo. Fabric slows recovery, so gi circles can be methodical. No-gi bottom players recompose faster—so the circle must arrive with immediate hip pressure or transition to another pass.

Retention. Gi answers include lasso and spider that immobilize the circling arm. No-gi answers include seated guard snaps, SLX entries, and underhooks when you overcommit your hands low.

Chains. Both rule sets use toreando → knee cut and toreando → leg drag. No-gi more often adds body-lock entries when the circle stalls close to the hips.

What Does the Toreando Look Like in Practice?

Alex sits in open guard. Jordan grips both pants at mid-shin, pushes the knees outward, and circles to Alex’s right. Alex frames on Jordan’s shoulder. Jordan punches the near knee down, steps the far leg, and lands hip-to-hip in side control.

No-gi. Same circle with shin control instead of pants. When Alex’s frame stiffens, Jordan switches to a knee cut on the near thigh rather than forcing the cape-work finish—showing the pass as a system, not a single path.

Types / Variations

Classic shin/pants circle

Two hands on the legs, circle to side control or knee-on-belly. The textbook toreando.

Toreando to leg drag

When the bottom player frames, slide an ankle into the armpit, step across, and pin the leg drag. Common gi and no-gi contingency.

Toreando to knee cut

Circle forces a hip reaction; plant into headquarters and cut. See knee cut pass.

Entries, Counters, and Retention

Entries. Standing above open guard; after breaking closed guard and forcing feet on hips; as a reset when a smash pass fails and space appears.

Counters. Bottom: knees to chest, collar-sleeve or wrist frames, sit-up into the passer’s hands, enter SLX/RDLR when hands overcommit. Top: if frames win, switch to knee cut, stack, or body lock instead of muscling the circle.

Toreando vs Knee Cut

Around versus through—the core pairing of modern open-guard passing.

FactorToreandoKnee Cut
PathAround the legsAcross the near thigh
Primary gripsPants/shins while circlingHip pin + upper-body connection
Best whenKnees can be kept off the chestA hip can be pinned for a wedge
Gi biasPants grips shineCollar + pants pin
No-gi biasNeeds fast hip connectionNeeds underhooks/head control
Failure modePulled into closed/SLXStalls in half guard

Standing vs kneeling. A standing toreando uses mobility and pants/shin pushes to create the circle. A kneeling or low toreando looks more like a hip-switch pass: same grip idea, less cape-work footwork. Both count as toreando when the path goes around the legs rather than through a knee cut.

Connection to open-guard meta. Against spider and lasso, you often cannot toreando until you clear the sleeve entanglement. Against de la Riva, clearing the hook and winning the far pant or shin usually precedes the circle. The pass is simple; the grip fights before it are not—see the parent guard entry for how bottom configurations change your first job.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal everywhere the sport is played. In IBJJF gi, pants-based toreando remains a staple. In ADCC/no-gi, expect faster scrambling and more immediate transitions to body lock or knee cut when the circle stalls.

Safety

Do not yank ankles sideways under load. Circle with control; tap early if a toe or knee complains during grip fighting.

Common mistakes

  • Circling upright with straight arms—easy to pull into closed guard.
  • Gripping only the knees (easy to circle) instead of shins/pants.
  • Abandoning hip connection once “past” the feet.
  • Forcing the circle against a locked lasso instead of clearing the grip first.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Why is it called toreando?

From Portuguese bullfighting imagery: you “cape” around the legs instead of colliding with them. English gyms also say bullfighter pass.

Toreando or torreando—which spelling?

Both appear. Martial Index uses toreando, matching common Portuguese romanization in BJJ media; pronunciation is roughly tor-e-AHN-do.

Does toreando work without a gi?

Yes, with shin and hip control instead of pants. Tempo is higher and you must connect the hip sooner.

They keep putting feet on my hips—what now?

You have not separated the knees. Push shins outward, change levels, or threaten a knee cut so they cannot keep both feet comfortably on your hips.

Should I finish every toreando in side control?

No. Knee-on-belly, north-south, and transitions to knee cut or leg drag are valid finishes. The goal is clearing the legs with control, not a single pin.

How do I stop getting pulled into closed guard?

Lower your posture, pinch elbows, and move as you grip. Standing tall with both hands committed low is the classic gift into closed guard.

Is toreando good against butterfly?

It can be, if you clear both hooks and keep weight on the shins. Many athletes prefer body lock or over-under against strong butterfly elevators.

The Bottom Line

The toreando pass clears open guard by circling around controlled legs. Gi pants grips slow the game; no-gi demands faster hip connection. Pair it with the knee cut so frames become opportunities instead of dead ends.
