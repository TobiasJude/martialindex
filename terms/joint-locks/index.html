Joint Locks

Joint locks threaten structural damage to a limb until the opponent taps. This hub indexes upper-body locks and points to the leg locks branch.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 16 Sep 2026 · Updated 16 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Joint Locks = Submissions that threaten hyperextension, rotation, or compression of a joint—arms, shoulders, wrists, knees, or ankles—until the opponent taps.
  • Mechanics: Isolate a limb, remove escape circles, then apply leverage slowly enough to allow a tap.
  • Gi vs no-gi: Sleeve control aids upper-body entries; twisting leg locks are often more central in no-gi—verify IBJJF vs ADCC.
  • Submission-family hub beside chokes; leg locks is the lower-body branch.
  • Drill both sides; tap early on neck, shoulder, and knee pressure.

What Are Joint Locks?

Joint locks in Brazilian jiu-jitsu are submissions that threaten hyperextension, rotation, or compression of a joint—most commonly elbow, shoulder, wrist, knee, or ankle—until the opponent taps. This Martial Index hub sits beside chokes under Submission, with leg locks as the specialized lower-body branch.

How Joint Locks Work

Isolate a limb, remove the opponent's ability to circle the joint free, then apply leverage slowly. Upper-body classics: armbar (elbow hyperextension), kimura and americana (shoulder rotation), omoplata, tarikoplata, and wrist locks where legal. Lower body: see leg locks—heel hook, straight ankle, toe hold, kneebar, calf slicer.

Gi vs No-Gi

Mechanics transfer; grips change. Gi sleeve control helps enter kimuras and armbars; no-gi uses wrist control, underhooks, and figure-fours. Twisting leg locks are often more central in no-gi / ADCC-style rooms—always verify the rule set.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

From S-mount you climb for an S-mount armbar; from side control you chain americana to kimura to armbar; from saddle you finish an inside heel hook. Failed locks should feed the next option in the chain—not a reset to neutral.

Types / Variations

Upper-body locks

Armbar family (from side, from S-mount, from crucifix), kimura / kimura trap, americana, omoplata.

Leg locks

Hub page: Leg locks. Entries via ashi garami entries, K-guard to heel hook, saddle heel hook.

Specialty & spinal

Wrist locks, compression locks, and spinal/neck cranks appear in some rule sets. Treat cranks as high-risk and rule-dependent—prefer positional control and blood chokes for general training.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries follow pins and guards. Counters: early frames, hide the elbow/heel, turn to reverse the figure-four. Once a lock is fully set, escapes are dangerous—tap to mechanics, not only pain.

How It Compares

Joint locks threaten structure; chokes threaten consciousness. Both are submissions. A sweep only changes who is on top.

Special Considerations

Rules & sport context

Legal locks vary sharply by belt and organization—especially heel hooks, reaping, and spinal locks. Confirm IBJJF vs ADCC before competing or drilling restricted attacks.

Safety

Apply slowly; knee and shoulder ligaments can fail with little warning. Drill under qualified coaches only.

Common mistakes

Muscling without isolation; skipping the chain when the first lock fails; ignoring rule-set legality.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What are joint locks in BJJ?

Submissions that force a tap by threatening a joint through hyperextension, rotation, or compression.

Are leg locks joint locks?

Yes—leg locks are the lower-body subclass with their own hub.

Gi or no-gi differences?

Same skeletons; grips and legal twisting attacks differ by rule set.

What should you chain next?

Failed americana often feeds kimura or armbar; failed heel hook feeds a different entanglement.

Common mistake?

Cranking without isolating the joint or controlling the hips.

Is it beginner-friendly?

Armbar/kimura/americana fundamentals yes; twisting heel hooks need supervised progressive training.

Where does it fit on Martial Index?

Under Submission beside Chokes, with Leg Locks as the lower-body hub.

The Bottom Line

Use Joint Locks to organize finishes by structure: upper-body classics first, then the leg-locks branch when your rule set allows.
