Uki Otoshi

Uki otoshi is the floating drop: tori breaks uke forward with hand levers and timing, dropping them without a big hip load—classic Te-Waza and nage-no-kata material. Compare Tai Otoshi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

  • Compare Tai Otoshi.
  • Needs clear Kuzushi and timing.
  • See also Te-Waza when it is a hand technique.

What is Uki Otoshi?

Uki otoshi (浮落 / floating drop) is a Kodokan hand technique that throws primarily with arm action and exquisite timing rather than a large koshi platform. Tori creates forward kuzushi, steps and pulls so uke’s floating balance collapses to the mat. It is famous as a nage-no-kata te-waza and as a reminder that judo scoring is not only about power hip throws.

Martial Index files uki-otoshi under Nage-Waza and as a live member of Te-Waza. Primary compare is Tai Otoshi, which also uses hand levers but typically inserts a blocking leg. Sibling corner drop: Sumi Otoshi.

Searchers wanting “uki otoshi judo” usually need kata literacy plus a modern sense of when the throw still appears in randori. On the judo hub, it sits with other timing throws that punish over-commitment.

Because the throw looks “small,” hobbyists underestimate the ukemi demand—floating drops can be abrupt. Teach falling before amplitude.

How it works

Kuzushi is forward and slightly upward so uke rises onto the toes—the “floating” feeling. Hands pull and guide while the body opens a path; unlike seoi, you are not loading uke on the back. Unlike tai-otoshi, you may not plant a stiff blocking leg as the primary stopper.

Timing beats muscle. If uke is flat and heavy, uki-otoshi will not invent balance. Setups include sleeve snaps, failed hip-throw recoveries, and moments when uke pushes forward into your pull.

Finish with continued arm action until the back landing is clear enough for Ippon or Waza-Ari. On partial success, follow into pins rather than arguing with physics.

Common failure: pulling down early before the float appears—then it becomes a clumsy tug.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

In the Judogi, cloth grips shape how Uki Otoshi is entered and finished. Competition gripping rules still apply—attacks must be positive under Judo Rules.

No-gi approximations keep timing and direction where possible but lose classic cloth levers. Transfers: kuzushi timing. Does not transfer: assuming every classical grip remains legal or available without a jacket.

Prefer qualified coaching; Martial Index is a map, not unsupervised curriculum.

What it looks like in practice

In Randori, Uki Otoshi appears when the specific kuzushi direction and grips line up—often rarer than high-percentage seoi or uchi-mata patterns, which is why encyclopedia literacy matters.

Kata and formal Uchi-Komi preserve timing details that competition film may skip. After landings, hunt Osaekomi holds such as Kesa Gatame or Yoko Shiho Gatame when chest contact remains.

Study beside siblings on Te-Waza and the pillar Nage-Waza so the throw is not an orphan highlight.

Types / variations

Kata uki-otoshi

Formal nage-no-kata pacing—best for feeling true floating kuzushi.

Randori timing uki-otoshi

Faster, opportunistic pulls when uke over-commits forward.

Combination uki after failed koshi

When a hip throw is stuffed and uke surges forward into your hands.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter only when balance and grips already tell the truth—Uki Otoshi punishes forced entries. Counters include posture correction, grip breaks, and circling off the throwing line.

Combinations should connect to live neighbours (see Compare). Moving uchi-komi before hard nage-komi protects partners and builds timing.

Compare

Versus Tai Otoshi: floating hand drop versus body-drop with a blocking leg. Versus Sumi Otoshi: forward float versus corner direction. Versus Seoi Otoshi: seoi back-load pull-down versus no back-load float.

IJF notes & safety

Uki-otoshi is legal classical judo. Safety issues are ordinary nage-waza concerns—ukemi, controlled pulling, no neck cranking. Follow Judo Rules for gripping and positive attacking.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Pulling before floating kuzushi.
  • Muscle instead of timing.
  • Confusing uki-otoshi with tai-otoshi on film.
  • Neglecting ukemi for “small” throws.
  • No follow-up when the throw partials.

Training notes

Drill kata-speed uki-otoshi weekly if you care about te-waza literacy—even if it is not your contest tokui-waza. Pair with Sumi Otoshi to feel directional differences.

Film the moment uke’s heels lighten; that frame is the throw’s truth.

Study notes

In nage-no-kata, uki-otoshi teaches the feeling of floating balance more clearly than almost any contest sequence. Athletes who skip kata sometimes never feel true forward-up kuzushi except by accident—then wonder why their power throws fail against calm partners.

A practical drill: after each failed hip throw, freeze and ask whether uki-otoshi was available on the rebound. If yes, you are leaving free timing on the table. If never, your grip fighting may be too static to create float.

Compare film of Tai Otoshi and uki-otoshi side by side with the sound off. Watch for the blocking leg: present in tai, typically absent in uki’s pure hand story.

The Bottom Line

Train uki-otoshi as timing literacy: float the balance, finish with the hands, and compare constantly with Tai Otoshi and Sumi Otoshi.
