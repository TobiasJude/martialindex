Mikazuki-Geri

Mikazuki-geri is the crescent kick: a moon-shaped arc used to strike or sweep aside—different geometry from mawashi-geri.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Crescent-path kick.
  • Not just a misnamed mawashi.
  • Useful for clears and angles.
  • Control over wild swinging.

What Is Mikazuki-Geri?

Mikazuki-geri (三日月蹴り) is the crescent (“crescent moon”) kick. In Karate it appears in kihon and some self-defense/bunkai contexts.

Compare carefully with Mawashi-Geri: similar “curved” family in casual speech, different mechanics and purposes.

May be used to clear a guard or strike with the sole/edge depending on teaching.

Martial Index files Mikazuki-Geri under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Mikazuki-Geri behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Mikazuki-Geri Works

Chamber, then swing the leg on a crescent path across the target line, control the hip, and recover without spinning off-balance.

Supporting foot stability and Kamae hands matter—crescent paths can leave you open if you lean.

Train both directions if your syllabus includes uchi/soto mikazuki variants.

Mechanically, successful mikazuki-geri work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, mikazuki-geri lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how mikazuki-geri appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill mikazuki-geri daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Sport scoring may rarely reward crescent kicks versus faster tools.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Drill: crescent kick to a focus pad held to the side of a partner’s guard.

Kata/bunkai classes sometimes use mikazuki to clear an arm before a punch.

Dojo picture: mikazuki-geri appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply mikazuki-geri after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Inside crescent

Arc coming from inside line—common teaching starting point.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives kumite pressure, not only line kihon.

Outside crescent

Opposite arc direction.

Common Mistakes

  • Treating it as a lazy mawashi.
  • Leaning the torso into the arc.
  • Hitting with a floppy ankle.
  • No recovery to stance.
  • Using it at the wrong range.
  • Treating mikazuki-geri as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill mikazuki-geri inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so mikazuki-geri connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether mikazuki-geri appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Draw the crescent slowly with the foot while keeping shoulders quiet.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe mikazuki-geri as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put mikazuki-geri behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Mikazuki vs mawashi?

Crescent vs roundhouse—paths and hip use differ; see Mawashi-Geri.

Is it practical?

Situational—clearing and angled strikes; not a replacement for mae/mawashi fundamentals.

Which surface hits?

Sole/edge variants exist—follow your coach.

Beginner kick?

Usually after mae-geri basics.

Stance partners?

Often Neko-Ashi-Dachi or zenkutsu drills.

The Bottom Line

Learn Mikazuki-Geri as its own geometry—crescent with control—then decide when it beats a clean Mae-Geri or Mawashi-Geri.
