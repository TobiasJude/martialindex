Chicken Wing

A chicken wing bends and elevates the far/near arm behind the back—ride pressure that feeds tilts and Near Fall turns.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Chicken wing = bent-arm elevation ride.
  • Feeds tilts and near falls.
  • Needs hips and legs.
  • Sibling of bar arm.
  • Defend before the elbow climbs.

What Is Chicken Wing?

The chicken wing is a top ride that controls a bent arm, often wedged behind the opponent’s back, to flatten and rotate.

It sits beside the Bar Arm in the arm-control family and often pairs with legs (Turk, Grapevine).

Searchers want the folkstyle ride that “chicken wings” an arm to invent back points—not a food metaphor.

In Wrestling, chicken-wing rides are a scholastic staple for manufacturing Tilt scores.

Read this beside the Wrestling style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Chicken Wing behaves across freestyle, folkstyle, and Greco-Roman contexts.

How Chicken Wing Works

Isolate an arm, bend the elbow, and drive it up the back while your hips and legs keep the opponent from coming to base.

From there, step over, lace, or tilt to put a shoulder toward the mat.

Combine with a Crossface when the free arm posts.

Release and re-break if the arm is slipping—do not hold a soft wing that invites a Switch Escape.

Mechanically, successful chicken wing work is usually a chain: Wrestling Stance integrity → entry or control → finish, score, or recovery.

If posture collapses or hips float, even textbook grips fail—top riders meet Sit-Out and Switch Escape pressure; shooters meet a Sprawl (Wrestling).

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: rides need Escape literacy; turns need Near Fall awareness; trips need base recovery.

Rules / Style Notes

Under United World Wrestling freestyle rules, chicken wing is judged through takedowns, exposure, and activity—not riding time.

Under NCAA/NFHS folkstyle, control, escapes, reversals, and near falls reshape how chicken wing is valued once wrestlers hit the mat.

Greco-Roman bans holds below the waist, so leg-coupled cousins of chicken wing either disappear or migrate into upper-body variants.

Confirm the rule set before importing gym habits into contest intensity—folkstyle riding does not equal freestyle Par Terre.

Potentially dangerous arm pressure will be stopped.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Near arm chicken-winged, you tilt through for a long near-fall count.

Chicken wing plus turk lace flattens a tough bottom wrestler.

Match picture: chicken wing appears when stance, ties, and timing say so, then yields to a finish, a Go-Behind, or a reset to neutral.

Room test: can you apply chicken wing after a Collar Tie or Russian Tie exchange and still recover posture? If not, chain it instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Cranking the shoulder without hip control—injury risk and easy bridge out.
  • Leaving legs inactive.
  • No tilt plan—just holding the wing.
  • Getting stacked when you over-rotate.
  • Treating chicken wing as a highlight instead of a stance-and-timing skill.
  • Shooting or turning without a recovery plan when the opponent bridges or scrambles.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadow-wrestle chicken wing inside full rounds; return to Wrestling Stance after every attempt.

Partner drills: technical → moving → forced follow-up so chicken wing connects to a chain (shot → finish, or ride → turn).

Live situational wrestling beats drill-only fluency; feed realistic hand fights so timing is part of the skill.

Film practice weekly and ask whether chicken wing appears with clean hip position and a real Level Change when shots are involved.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for throws, neck ties, and pinning combinations.

Partner drills: wing to tilt with referee counts.

Live top: score back points only via arm rides.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe chicken wing as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under hand-fighting stress, a collapsed Wrestling Stance, or hips that never get under the opponent.

When you compare rooms, you will see stylistic accents: freestyle rooms push Exposure chains; folkstyle rooms push ride-and-turn; Greco rooms push upper-body locks. The encyclopedia definition of chicken wing stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Freestyle Wrestling, Folkstyle Wrestling, and Wrestling Stance into siblings, then return here after live rounds to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for throws, neck ties, and pinning combinations where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “ride,” “tilt,” and “headlock.” Martial Index’s Wrestling entries keep chicken wing scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language—compare pages are labeled when useful.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on chicken wing, one situational live theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing or hip position failed.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Chicken wing vs bar arm?

Closely related arm rides; naming varies by room—bar arm often emphasizes the forearm bar angle.

Is it a pin by itself?

Rarely—usually a ride that creates a turn.

Legal for youth?

Teach under coaches; shoulder safety first.

Freestyle use?

Less as a long folkstyle ride; turns still appear.

Best defense?

Don’t let the elbow elevate—elbow in, Escape early.

Common chain?

Chicken wing → Tilt → Near Fall.

The Bottom Line

Own the chicken wing as a scoring ride, not a stall—tilt or release.
