What Is Gankaku?

Gankaku (岩鶴) is Shotokan’s renaming of the Chinto family kata—commonly glossed as “crane on a rock,” matching one-leg balance postures.

The embusen is often described as a relatively linear advanced map that demands pivots and balance under Yoko-Geri-related pairings and manji-uke postures.

Balance is the curriculum: if the crane falls, the rest of the choreography does not matter.

Martial Index files Gankaku under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Gankaku behaves as a Kata pattern, not as a punch/kick drill alone.

How Gankaku Works

Build one-leg holds separately before full-speed kata.

Eyes stabilize balance—look at a fixed point before kicking.

Landings after kicks must re-root immediately into a trustworthy Karate Stance.

Mechanically, strong Gankaku work is usually a chain: stance integrity → Embusen honesty → timing → clear Kime → recovery with Kamae alive.

If orientation drifts, even perfect arms fail—walk the floor line slowly before chasing speed.

Pair form quality with Kihon: the same blocks, punches, and kicks that appear in Gankaku should survive line practice and light partner timing.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, Gankaku lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, kata competition scoring rewards clarity, timing, and athleticism; exact embusen and counts follow association texts.

Gym culture may drill Gankaku weekly; grading and competition may emphasize different tempo and presentation.

Confirm your association’s syllabus text before treating any public video’s count as law.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Balance prep: 30-second crane holds each side, then Gankaku sectionals.

Full run only after holds stop wobbling.

Dojo picture: Gankaku appears when Kata class cycles the syllabus, then yields to Kihon corrections on the hardest section.

Gym test: can you perform Gankaku with honest Embusen after fatigue, still finishing with Kime? If not, slow the map before adding polish.

Common Mistakes

  • Kicking before the standing leg is set.
  • Looking down and destroying balance.
  • Rushing spins that should be pivots.
  • Skipping isometric balance prep.
  • Treating Gankaku as only aesthetic.
  • Treating Gankaku as a highlight reel instead of a stance-and-embusen skill.
  • Skipping recovery—finishing a sequence then posing with empty kamae.
  • Ignoring sibling kata that share themes you have not stabilized yet.

Training Notes

Section-drill Gankaku: isolate the hardest turn or one-leg moment, then reconnect to the full Embusen.

Slow training days: hold stances and Hikite longer so Kime has somewhere to land.

Partner timing (light application study) only after the skeleton is stable—do not rewrite the official pattern casually.

Film from the same corner weekly to spot embusen drift and height changes.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially jumps, one-leg balances, and deep rooted stances.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe Gankaku as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under fatigue, poor Embusen, or a stance that collapses when speed arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put Gankaku behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport presentation. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Kata, Embusen, and Karate Stance into sibling kata, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for jumps, deep stances, and one-leg balance work.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “form,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep Gankaku scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical runs of Gankaku, one slow section focus, and one film review asking where timing or height failed.

If you compete in kata, write a one-line association note for Gankaku (count, embusen, optional tokui status) so training matches the card.
