Cut Angle (Kyorugi)

Cut angle is a Kyorugi (Sparring) idea: do not live on the railroad track. The kick that often starts it is Cut Kick (Taekwondo); the feet that finish it are Sliding Step and Switching Stance (TKD).

What Is Cut Angle (Kyorugi)?

A cut angle is the off-line result of a cut, slide, or switch—not merely the Cut Kick (Taekwondo) itself.

Two athletes on a straight line share the same easy counters. An angle changes which Hogu side is open and which Counter Kick is available.

There is no separate live “cut-angle footwork” Boxing page to merge. This slug stays Taekwondo.

Think geometry: after you cut or feint, your next stance should not be where their rear Dwi Chagi (Back Kick) was already aimed.

Read this beside the Taekwondo style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Cut Angle (Kyorugi) behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Cut Angle (Kyorugi) Works

Cut or Feint Kick, then Sliding Step or a small arc so your lead points at their ribs, not their centerline.

Keep stance length. Angling with your feet together is just turning in place.

Eyes and hands come with you. An angle with a turned-away head is a Head Kick (Olgul Chagi) gift.

If they follow the line, you cut again or switch. One angle is not a lifestyle.

Mechanically, successful cut angle (kyorugi) work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Taekwondo Stance with hands alive.

If distance is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you kick air; too close and you collide into clinches or jammed chambers you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: kicks need Arae Makki (Low Block)/Momtong Makki (Middle Block) literacy when drills reverse roles; sparring tools need Hogu and Electronic Scoring (PSS) awareness.

WT / ITF / Rules Notes

Under World Taekwondo (Olympic) competition, cut angle (kyorugi) is interpreted through World Taekwondo Rules, Kyorugi (Sparring) scoring zones, and often Electronic Scoring (PSS)—not bare gym aesthetics.

ITF Taekwondo schools may share Korean vocabulary while differing on sine-wave motion, hand techniques, and pattern (tul) emphasis; do not assume WT footage equals ITF syllabus.

Gym culture may drill cut angle (kyorugi) daily; contest night may shrink the toolkit based on weight class, PSS sensor thresholds, and coaching strategy.

Confirm the event agreement (WT, ITF, open, recreational) before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Lead cut, slide off, lead Body Kick (Momtong Chagi) to the newly opened trunk.

Mirror drill: partner always tries to re-center; you keep taking a half-step off.

Fight picture: cut angle (kyorugi) appears when range and timing say so, then yields to the next kick or exit back to Taekwondo Stance.

Gym test: can you apply cut angle (kyorugi) after a Feint Kick and still recover guard and balance? If not, connect it to Kick Combination work instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Cutting and staying on the same line.
  • Spinning 90 degrees and losing the target.
  • Angling into the fence or the line judge.
  • Calling every sidestep a cut kick.
  • Dropping hands to “look athletic.”
  • Treating cut angle (kyorugi) as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowkick cut angle (kyorugi) inside full rounds; return to Taekwondo Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so cut angle (kyorugi) connects to a Kick Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so Counter Kick timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether cut angle (kyorugi) appears in the right range band for Kyorugi (Sparring) or forms context.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for spinning kicks, aerials, and PSS-targeted Palding (Trunk Scoring) or Head Kick (Olgul Chagi) work.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe cut angle (kyorugi) as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor distance, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put cut angle (kyorugi) behind heavy Olympic pressure kicking; others behind traditional Poomsae precision. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Taekwondo Stance, Dollyo Chagi (Turning Kick), and Kyorugi (Sparring) into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for spinning kicks and head-height targets where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “roundhouse,” “cut kick,” “hook,” and “feint.” Martial Index’s Taekwondo entries keep cut angle (kyorugi) scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Muay Thai or Boxing articles that share casual English labels.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on cut angle (kyorugi), one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for cut angle (kyorugi) under your next card (WT vs ITF, PSS vs paper scoring, headgear) so training intensity matches the bout.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Cut angle vs cut kick?

Cut Kick (Taekwondo) is a kick. Cut angle is the footwork result you want after (or instead of) that kick.

Is there a Boxing page?

Not as a live TKD compare. This article stays in Kyorugi (Sparring).

Why it matters for PSS?

Open sides are easier legal Palding (Trunk Scoring) and Head Kick (Olgul Chagi) surfaces.

Slide or skip to angle?

Both. Sliding Step is quieter; Skip Step is faster.

ITF?

Angle still beats standing on the railroad track.

The Bottom Line

If you cut and remain on the same line, you performed a kick—not an angle.
