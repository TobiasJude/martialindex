Kata Guruma

Kata guruma is the shoulder wheel—the judo fireman’s carry: load uke across the shoulders and wheel them over. Classical forms often gripped a leg; modern IJF play adapted after direct leg grabs were banned.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Classical Nage-Waza / Te-Waza member.
  • Compare Seoi Otoshi for drop shoulder mechanics.
  • Direct leg grabs banned in modern IJF senior shiai (2010+)—adaptations exist.
  • Drop/rolling no-leg variants appear in competition.
  • Needs clear Kuzushi and loading control.
  • Ukemi and neck safety are non-negotiable.

What is Kata Guruma?

Kata guruma (肩車 / shoulder wheel) is the Kodokan fireman’s-carry throw: tori breaks uke’s balance, loads uke across the shoulders or upper back, and wheels them over to the mat. In classical teaching and nage-no-kata contexts, hand involvement with a leg was part of the loading story. Martial Index files it under Nage-Waza and lists it among live Te-Waza members.

The 2010 IJF rule changes that banned direct hand/arm attacks below the belt transformed kata-guruma’s competitive life. Research on post-2010 contests documented athletes adapting toward forms that wheel without grabbing the leg—often with drop or rolling entries using gi grips. Classical pick-up kata-guruma did not vanish from history books; it vanished from legal World Tour shortcuts.

Compare Seoi Otoshi when the conversation is drop shoulder finishes without a full wheel across both shoulders. Compare Sukui Nage and Obi Otoshi for other scoop/belt-related te-waza. BJJ/wrestling fireman’s carries are cousins in geometry—not identical rule creatures.

Searchers want legality clarity more than poetry. This page teaches history, legal modern shapes, and safety—without telling anyone to grab legs in IJF senior shiai.

How it works

Classical picture: kuzushi, level change, shoulder load, wheel. The arms and posture create a platform; the turn completes the throw onto uke’s back. Modern no-leg competition pictures often drop to knees or roll while controlling sleeves/lapels to approximate the wheel without a direct leg pick.

Control of uke’s upper body is the difference between a wheel and a dump. Head position matters for both tori and uke—diving somersaults with uke on the shoulders can be catastrophic and are forbidden in modern refereeing explanations for certain diving actions.

If the load stalls, do not wrench the neck. Disengage, switch to another Tachi-Waza attack such as Tai Otoshi, or settle into Ne-Waza if the scramble already hit the mat.

Scoring still follows throw amplitude rules for Ippon and Waza-Ari when the technique is legal in form.

Judogi vs no-gi judo

Gi grips enable many modern no-leg adaptations—sleeve and lapel replace the banned direct leg hold in competition stories. Classical kata and self-defense curricula may still teach leg involvement where rules allow (non-IJF contexts, historical study).

No-gi fireman’s carries are wrestling staples. Transfers: loading across the shoulders. Does not transfer: IJF gripping legality assumptions.

What it looks like in practice

In modern IJF film, look for drop kata-guruma shapes and rolling wheels that never palm a leg. In kata classes, you may still see classical loading for educational ukemi and tradition. Club Randori under IJF rules must follow the ban on direct leg grabs.

After a successful wheel, pin quickly—chest-to-chest into Yoko Shiho Gatame or related holds under Osaekomi.

Historical study of Morote Gari and related leg attacks explains why kata-guruma’s competitive form had to evolve after 2010.

Types / variations

Classical pick-up kata-guruma

Load with historical leg involvement—teach as history/kata/self-defense literacy; not as IJF senior contest advice.

Drop / no-leg competition kata-guruma

Knees down, gi control, wheel without direct leg grab—modern adaptation documented after the ban.

Rolling wheel variants

Sacrificial rolling finishes that approximate the shoulder wheel under grip control—coach ukemi intensively.

Entries, counters, combinations

Enter when posture and grips allow a safe level change. Counters: sprawl, heavy hips, peel sleeves, stuff the head. Combinations: threaten seoi → change to wheel; failed wheel → pin scramble.

Uchi-komi for drop variants should start slow with crash-mat culture. This is not a first-month throw for most hobbyists.

Compare

Versus Seoi Otoshi: seoi back-load pull-down versus shoulder-wheel carry. Versus Sukui Nage: scooping throw family. Versus Obi Otoshi: belt-drop mechanics. Versus wrestling fireman’s carry: shared geometry, different rules.

IJF notes & safety

Direct gripping of the legs with hands/arms is prohibited as a direct attack in modern IJF senior competition (ban era beginning 2010; verify current SOR details). Kata-guruma that depends on a direct leg grab is therefore not a World Tour tool. Drop/rolling adaptations that avoid illegal leg grips may still be used when otherwise legal and safe. Diving/somersault dangers with uke on the shoulders are separately restricted. See Judo Rules. Do not use banned direct leg grabs in IJF contests.

Under IJF 2026–28 scoring, osaekomi awards yuko at 5–9 seconds, waza-ari at 10–19 seconds, and ippon at 20 seconds. Yuko is live; it does not stack into waza-ari. Two waza-ari equal ippon. Standing scores still follow Ippon and Waza-Ari amplitude criteria in the Judo Rules.

Safety: solid Ukemi before hard nage-komi; tap early; train under qualified coaches. Build skill in Uchi-Komi, then test in Randori.

Common mistakes

  • Teaching classical leg-pick kata-guruma as current IJF contest default.
  • Loading without a clear wheel direction.
  • Diving head-first with uke on the shoulders.
  • Skipping ukemi partnerships.
  • Confusing seoi-otoshi with kata-guruma on film.

Training notes

Separate historical/kata modules from IJF contest modules explicitly on the lesson plan. Athletes deserve to know which version they are drilling and why.

For competition adaptations, prioritise grip control and drop mechanics without ever grabbing the leg in IJF-rules randori.

Study notes

Nage-no-kata still preserves classical shoulder-wheel education for ranks and teaching licenses in many federations. That educational lane is not a loophole for IJF senior contest legality—it is a parallel curriculum with different goals: ukemi, timing, and tradition.

Coaches rebuilding post-ban games often pair drop kata-guruma study with heavy seoi volume because both solve “how do I get under a tall posture?” without palm-to-leg grabs. See also the seoi hub Seoi Nage (Judo).

Safety briefing should mention that loading a person across the shoulders changes uke’s fall options. Partners need space, mats, and a veto. Volume without veto culture is how rare throws become injury stories.

The Bottom Line

Study kata-guruma as classical shoulder wheel plus modern no-leg adaptations—and never treat banned direct leg grabs as current IJF contest advice.
