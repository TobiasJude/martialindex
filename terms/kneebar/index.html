Kneebar

The kneebar is a Brazilian jiu-jitsu submission that hyperextends the knee by controlling the leg and extending your hips—available from many entanglements and top positions, generally more IBJJF-friendly than heel hooks, and fully relevant in no-gi.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Kneebar = straight hyperextension of the knee via your hips (juji-like geometry on the leg).
  • Often legal in IBJJF contexts where heel hooks are not—still verify your division.
  • Works in gi and no-gi; entries differ with pants grips.
  • Compare with heel hook (rotation vs extension).
  • Tap early—knees are not durable ego tissue.

What Is the Kneebar?

The kneebar controls the opponent’s leg (often between your legs, similar in geometry to an armbar) and extends your hips to hyperextend their knee. It is a straight lock, not a rotational heel hook.

How Kneebar Works

Isolate a leg, align their knee across your hips, control the foot/hip so they cannot turn out, and extend. Pinch and angle matter just as in armbars.

Gi vs No-Gi

Gi. Pants grips help control the leg during entries; IBJJF gi athletes often emphasize kneebars/toe holds where heel hooks are illegal.

No-gi. Same finish without pants; often secondary to heel hooks in the modern meta but still a high-percentage threat.

Rule awareness. Straight kneebars are typically treated differently from rotational heel hooks in IBJJF—read the current book.

Types / Variations

Extension mechanics

Hip extension through a controlled knee line.

Back-step, 50/50, and top entries

Common modern entries from passing failures and entanglements.

Legality relative to heel hooks

Often the “IBJJF-legal leg attack” conversation partner to heel hooks—verify rather than assume.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Enter from entanglements, turtle, and failed passes. Escape by turning the knee out early and clearing hips—tap when extended.

No-gi metaImportantOften primary

Armbar geometry transfer. If you understand juji gatame hip alignment, you already understand half of the kneebar: the joint crosses your hips, and extension does the damage. The difference is leg control and how they turn to relieve the knee.

Toe hold interactions. In IBJJF gi, toe holds and kneebars often share the “legal lower-body” conversation for adult divisions where heel hooks are banned. They are different submissions—do not conflate them when reading brackets.

Passing into kneebars. Back-steps and failed body locks frequently expose a leg. Have a kneebar reflex so a failed pass still threatens a finish—especially useful when heel hooks are off the table.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Confirm IBJJF division rules. ADCC allows a wide leg-lock game including kneebars.

Safety

Tap early. Controlled drilling only.

Common mistakes

  • Extending without controlling the hip/foot.
  • Ego rolling through knee pressure.
  • Confusing kneebar legality with heel-hook legality.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is a kneebar legal in IBJJF gi?

Straight kneebars are commonly legal in adult divisions where heel hooks are not—but belt/age exceptions exist. Check the official rules for your bracket.

Same as an Achilles lock?

No. Kneebar targets knee extension; Achilles/estola-style attacks target the ankle/Achilles differently.

Why finish kneebars if heel hooks are “better”?

Rulesets, positions, and defenses differ. A complete leg game needs both.

Gi pants required?

No, but they help control.

Dangerous?

Yes if abused—tap early.

From closed guard?

Less common than from entanglements/top; possible in transitions.

See also?

Heel hook for the rotational counterpart.

The Bottom Line

The kneebar hyperextends the knee with your hips—often more IBJJF-friendly than heel hooks, still requiring strict tapping culture. Train it beside the heel hook for a complete lower-body understanding.
