Straight Right

The straight right is the rear-hand straight—driven on a line through the Power Line, usually after a Jab in the One-Two.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Orthodox rear straight under the Cross pillar.
  • Line path beats loop when you want accuracy and recovery.
  • Pairs with the Jab in the One-Two.
  • Protect the chin and keep the lead hand alive.

What Is Straight Right?

A straight right is the rear-hand straight punch thrown from an Orthodox Stance: right foot back, right fist traveling a straight path to the target.

Searchers asking “what is a straight right” usually want the difference from a looping Overhand Right or a wild Haymaker, plus how it sits under the parent Cross.

In Boxing, “straight right” and “cross” are often used interchangeably for the rear straight; Martial Index keeps Cross as the pillar and this page as the handedness-specific orthodox label.

Parent: Cross. Compare with Straight Left (southpaw rear or orthodox lead straight, depending on context) and Overhand Right.

Martial Index files Straight Right under Boxing so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Boxing style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Straight Right behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Straight Right Works

From Boxing Stance, push off the rear foot, rotate hips and shoulder, extend the rear fist on a straight line, palm rotating toward down near impact—classic Corkscrew Punch finish on many gyms’ teaching.

Chin tucks; Lead Hand recovers or frames. Eyes stay up. Do not loop: the path is the Cross, not a baseball swing.

Common vehicle: Jab then straight right (One-Two). Also lands as a Counterpunch after a Slip outside the opponent’s jab.

Mechanically, successful straight right work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: straights need Slip/Parry awareness; hooks need Bob and Weave literacy; power shots need a clean Power Line.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, straight right lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill straight right daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Gym: mitt call “one-two” — jab sets eyes high, straight right drives through the center line.

Fight: rear straight scores when Boxing Range is mid-long; if you are too close, convert to Short Hook or Uppercut instead of forcing a long arm.

Fight picture: straight right appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply straight right after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

One-two finish

Classic: Jab occupies, straight right scores. Rhythm can be equal or light-hard.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives sparring pressure, not only pad stations.

Counter straight

Common Mistakes

  • Looping the punch into an accidental {overhand-right} or {haymaker}.
  • Dropping the {lead-hand} on delivery.
  • Planting both heels so the {power-line} dies.
  • Telegraphing with a big wind-up.
  • Treating straight right as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.

Training Notes

Shadowbox straight right inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so straight right connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether straight right appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shell defenses, check hooks, and pull counters.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe straight right as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Boxing Range, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put straight right behind heavy boxing pressure; others behind movement and counters. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is a straight right the same as a cross?

In orthodox boxing, yes for most gyms—the rear straight is the cross. Martial Index uses cross as the parent pillar and straight-right as the orthodox-facing label.

Straight right vs overhand right?

Straight right travels on a line; overhand right arcs over the top. Different paths, different setups.

When should I throw it?

After a jab that occupies the guard, as a counter off a slip, or when mid-range opens the center line.

Does it work for southpaws?

Southpaws throw a straight left as their rear straight—see straight-left and southpaw-stance.

How do I add power without looping?

Drive the power-line: feet → hips → shoulder → fist. Snap and rotate at impact rather than swinging wide.

The Bottom Line

Treat the straight right as a precise rear straight—own the Power Line, finish the One-Two, and leave the wild loops to fighters who enjoy getting countered.
