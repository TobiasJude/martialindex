Belt Tying

Belt tying is the everyday skill of securing your belt so it stays on during training and meets academy or competition presentation standards.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 16 Sep 2026 · Updated 16 Sep 2026

Key Takeaways

  • A secure knot keeps the belt on through grips, rolls, and stand-ups.
  • Even hanging ends and a centered knot are common academy expectations.
  • Competition inspectors may check knot security and belt condition—verify your event’s guide.
  • Stripe placement usually sits on the left hanging end for many schools—follow your instructor.
  • Links to gi, belt stripes, and IBJJF gi requirements.

What Is Belt Tying?

Belt tying means wrapping and knotting the colored rank belt around the gi so it stays fastened. Cultures differ on knot style (simple double wrap, “superlock,” judo-influenced knots), but the goals are security, symmetry, and respect for rank display.

How It Works

Center the belt on the waist, wrap twice (or per your coach’s method), tuck and pull a flat knot at the midline, and equalize the hanging ends. Stripes—if awarded—are typically shown on one end; ask your academy which side they prefer.

Gi vs No-Gi

Belts are a gi (and some formal no-gi rank display) convention. Pure no-gi classes usually train without a belt; some schools still bow with belts on for etiquette.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Before class you retie after putting on the jacket, check ends are even, and re-knot mid-session when grips pull it loose—without stopping the coach’s demo to fuss.

Types / Variations

Common patterns

Standard double-wrap knot; reinforced “superlock” styles for competition; quick-retie mid-roll habits.

Gym / sport variants

Tournament weigh-ins and mat checks may require a specific presentation. See IBJJF gi requirements and the current event guide—do not invent measurements from memory.

Beginner notes

Ask a teammate to show the house knot once. Practice at home so class time is not consumed by belt theater.

How to Apply / Practical Tips

Wet or stretched belts loosen—retie. Never use the belt to choke casually in training jokes. Replace frayed belts before competition.

How It Compares

Distinct from belt promotion (earning rank) and stripes (intra-belt markers). Tying is equipment hygiene and etiquette.

Special Considerations

Rules & sport context

Federations publish belt and uniform rules. Verify the current IBJJF guide for your event rather than relying on gym lore.

Safety

A flying loose belt is a tripping/grab hazard—retie between rounds.

Common mistakes

Uneven ends; knot on the hip; showing stripes on the wrong side for your school; borrowing a belt that is the wrong rank color.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

How do you tie a BJJ belt?

Wrap evenly around the waist and finish with a flat, secure midline knot—learn your gym’s preferred style.

Do ends need to be even?

Most academies expect roughly equal hanging ends.

Where do stripes go?

Follow your instructor; many schools place them on one hanging end.

Competition knot tips?

Use a secure knot and verify event uniform rules—see IBJJF gi requirements.

No-gi belts?

Usually not worn for training; some schools still use belts for formal lineup.

Common mistake?

Knot riding on the hip or ends wildly uneven.

Related pages?

Belt system, stripes, promotion, and gi sizing.

The Bottom Line

A clean belt tie is small professionalism: secure, centered, and consistent with your academy—and ready for any uniform check.
