Go-Behind

A go-behind circles you to the opponent's back—from snaps, duck unders, or sprawls—so you can score control.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Go-behind = arrive behind with control.
  • Cash-out for ducks, snaps, and sprawls.
  • Hips travel with the circle.
  • Scores takedown control when completed.
  • Chain into rides or exposure.

What Is Go-Behind?

The go-behind is a positional transition more than a single technique: you arrive behind the opponent with control of the hips or waist.

It is the cash-out for Duck Under, Snap Down, and many Sprawl (Wrestling) defenses.

In Folkstyle Wrestling, go-behinds often complete the takedown and start the ride; in freestyle they may continue to exposure.

In Wrestling, learning to go behind cleanly separates hand-fighters who score from those who only clutter ties.

Martial Index files Go-Behind under Wrestling so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Wrestling style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Go-Behind behaves across freestyle, folkstyle, and Greco-Roman contexts.

How Go-Behind Works

Create an angle (duck, snap, sprawl circle), keep chest contact, and secure a waist lock or hip control as you arrive behind.

Do not reach with arms while your hips stay in front—hips travel with you.

Finish by putting them to the mat with control or covering in folkstyle.

If they spin with you, re-attack or return to a front headlock rather than giving up the angle.

Mechanically, successful go-behind work is usually a chain: Wrestling Stance integrity → Level Change → entry or control → finish or recovery.

If stance collapses or the Penetration Step stalls short, even textbook grips fail—too high and you meet a Sprawl (Wrestling); too loose and you invite a Whizzer or scramble.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: shots need sprawl literacy; ties need hand-fighting; pins need Near Fall awareness in folkstyle.

Rules / Style Notes

Under United World Wrestling freestyle rules, go-behind is judged through takedowns, exposure, and activity—not riding time.

Under NCAA/NFHS folkstyle, control, escapes, reversals, and near falls reshape how go-behind is valued once the wrestlers hit the mat.

Greco-Roman bans holds below the waist, so leg-attack cousins of go-behind either disappear or migrate into upper-body variants.

Confirm the rule set before importing gym habits into contest intensity—folkstyle riding does not equal freestyle par terre.

Control must be clear for the score.

Illegal holds on the face while circling will be called.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Kyle Dake clinic feel: tight circle, hips in, control on arrival.

Sprawl defense: hips down, circle, go-behind, cover.

Match picture: go-behind appears when stance, ties, and timing say so, then yields to a finish, a Go-Behind, or a reset to neutral.

Room test: can you apply go-behind after a Collar Tie or Russian Tie exchange and still recover posture? If not, chain it instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Circling with hips behind your hands.
  • Arriving behind without securing control.
  • Stopping in front headlock forever when the go-behind is open.
  • Letting them turn into you after you arrive.
  • Treating go-behind as a highlight instead of a stance-and-timing skill.
  • Shooting or turning without a recovery plan when the opponent sprawls or bridges.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadow-wrestle go-behind inside full rounds; return to Wrestling Stance after every attempt.

Partner drills: technical → moving → forced follow-up so go-behind connects to a chain (shot → finish, or ride → turn).

Live situational wrestling beats drill-only fluency; feed realistic hand fights so timing is part of the skill.

Film practice weekly and ask whether go-behind appears with a real Level Change and clean hip position.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for throws, front headlocks, and pinning combinations.

Sprawl-to-go-behind situational live.

Duck under to cover drills for two minutes.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe go-behind as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under hand-fighting stress, a collapsed Wrestling Stance, or a Level Change that never gets under the opponent’s hips.

When you compare rooms, you will see stylistic accents: freestyle rooms push exposure chains; folkstyle rooms push ride-and-turn; Greco rooms push upper-body locks. The encyclopedia definition of go-behind stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Freestyle Wrestling, Folkstyle Wrestling, and Wrestling Stance into siblings, then return here after live rounds to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for throws, neck ties, and pinning combinations where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “clinch,” “sprawl,” and “single.” Martial Index’s Wrestling entries keep go-behind scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language—compare pages are labeled when useful.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Is a go-behind a takedown?

When it results in control per your rule set, it scores as a takedown (or contributes to one).

Go-behind vs back take in BJJ?

Related idea; wrestling go-behinds emphasize mat return and control criteria under wrestling rules.

Best setups?

Duck Under, Snap Down, sprawl circles.

Folkstyle next step?

Ride and turn—Cradle, Half Nelson.

Freestyle next step?

Exposure turns or lace series depending on coaching.

Common fail?

Getting stuck in front headlock without circling.

The Bottom Line

Do not collect front headlocks as trophies—when the angle is there, go behind and own the hips.
