Peek-a-Boo

Peek-a-boo is Cus D’Amato’s high-guard pressure system: gloves framing the face, deep crouch, constant bobbing, and short explosive counters.

Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • High gloves + crouch + constant movement.
  • D’Amato system; Tyson exemplar.
  • Explode short, then angle.

What Is Peek-a-Boo?

Guard sibling to High Guard (Boxing) and contrasting Philadelphia Shell. Built on Bob and Weave and Slip habits.

Accurate lineage note: D’Amato developed the system; Tyson is the most famous exemplar—not the inventor.

Martial Index files Peek-a-Boo under Boxing so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Boxing style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Peek-a-Boo behaves in stance, range, and exchanges.

How Peek-a-Boo Works

Gloves at cheek/eyebrow height with a vision gap, elbows tight, stance often squarer, knees loaded. Head never parks on the center line. Explode upward into Hook and Uppercut bursts, then angle off.

Mechanically, successful peek-a-boo work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Boxing Stance with hands alive.

If Boxing Range is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: straights need Slip/Parry awareness; hooks need Bob and Weave literacy; power shots need a clean Power Line.

Amateur vs Pro / Rules Notes

Under pro boxing rules, peek-a-boo lives inside a hands-only striking sport—no kicks or elbows—so recovery and clinch etiquette differ from Muay Thai cousins.

Amateur boxing (Olympic/AIBA-style heritage and modern open-headgear variants) historically nudged higher guards and different scoring optics; still, chin-up habits lose.

Gym culture may drill peek-a-boo daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit based on opponent style and judging.

Confirm the bout agreement and glove size before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Slip bag rhythm → burst 3–4 punches → pivot. Sparring theme: only score after a slip or weave.

Fight picture: peek-a-boo appears when Boxing Range and timing say so, then yields to the next punch in a Combination or to an exit.

Gym test: can you apply peek-a-boo after a Feint and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Classic peek-a-boo

Deep crouch, continuous weave, short hooks/uppercuts.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives sparring pressure, not only pad stations.

Modern high-guard pressure accents

Higher posture variants with active glove catches—still high hands + head movement.

Common Mistakes

  • Hands so high you blind yourself to hooks.
  • Crouching without head movement (static target).
  • Throwing long arm punches from the crouch.
  • Treating peek-a-boo as a highlight instead of a range-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Shadowbox peek-a-boo inside full rounds; return to Boxing Stance after every attempt.

Pads: technical → moving → forced follow-up so peek-a-boo connects to a Combination.

Partner drills beat bag-only fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film sparring weekly and ask whether peek-a-boo appears in the right Boxing Range band.

Progress intensity with a coach—especially for shell defenses, check hooks, and pull counters.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe peek-a-boo as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor Boxing Range, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put peek-a-boo behind heavy boxing pressure; others behind movement and counters. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Boxing Stance, Jab, and Slip into siblings, then return here after sparring to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for head movement and power punching where mistiming injures partners.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “pressure,” and “timing.” Martial Index’s Boxing entries keep peek-a-boo scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on peek-a-boo, one situational sparring theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.

If you compete, write a one-line rule note for peek-a-boo under your next card (glove size, round length, amateur vs pro judging) so training intensity matches the bout.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Did Mike Tyson invent peek-a-boo?

No—Cus D’Amato’s system; Tyson is the iconic practitioner.

Peek-a-boo vs high guard?

Peek-a-boo is a full system with crouch and bobbing; High Guard (Boxing) can be a simpler cover.

Is it only for short fighters?

It favors shorter pressure fighters but principles travel.

How do I train it safely?

Slip bag, rope weave, mitt bursts—progress intensity with a coach.

The Bottom Line

Peek-a-boo is a lifestyle of movement—not just gloves glued to cheeks.
