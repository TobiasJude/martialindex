Double Underhooks

Double underhooks are both arms inserted under the opponent’s—dominant clinch control that feeds body locks, trips, and mat returns.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 12 Sep 2026 · Updated 12 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Earned by pummeling, not wishing.
  • Elbows tight, hips close.
  • Clasp to body lock with gable grip.
  • Head position prevents front headlocks.
  • Parent path often through body lock.

How Double Underhooks Works

Swim both arms inside, clamp elbows to their ribs, drive forehead into chest, then lock hands (Gable Grip) or attack a trip while hands are still free.

Mechanically, successful double underhooks work is usually a chain: establish position or grip → remove a base or frame → apply the scoring action → recover posture with hands alive.

If distance or fence angle is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close without a pin and you collide into frames, whizzers, or submission threats.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: clinch tools need pummel awareness; GNP tools need escape recognition; get-ups need strike covers.

MMA Rules / Cage Context

Under Unified Rules MMA, double underhooks lives inside a full striking-and-grappling rule set—illegal targets, grounded-opponent knees, and fence-grab fouls change how you invest in this tool.

Amateur cards and some commissions restrict elbows or ground strikes. Gym culture may still drill double underhooks daily; fight night may shrink the toolkit.

Confirm the bout agreement before importing gym frequency into restricted rules or into pure grappling contexts where strikes disappear.

Judging rewards damage, aggression, and control differently by panel. Let the card’s eyes shape whether you emphasize volume, rides, or finishes.

Legal clinch; beware illegal holds on gloves/shorts.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Pummel clock into doubles, outside trip.

Fence doubles, lift off mesh, turn.

Fight picture: double underhooks appears when cage geometry and grip economy say so, then yields to the next tool in the MMA chain—clinch, takedown, GNP, or get-up.

Gym test: can you apply double underhooks after a realistic feint or scramble and still recover your stance or pin? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Common Mistakes

  • Hands locked with hips out.
  • Chin up (guillotine risk in transitions).
  • No finish plan.
  • Treating double underhooks as a highlight instead of a position-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down or hips light.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe double underhooks as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor fence awareness, or grips that collapse when strikes enter the round.

When you compare gyms, you will see stylistic accents: some put double underhooks behind heavy wrestling; others behind dirty boxing; submission-heavy rooms may leave GNP light. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Film and Progress Notes

In live rounds, double underhooks rarely appears in isolation: it is set by footwork, a prior grip fight, or a failed shot. Train the entry that creates the chance, not only the highlight finish.

Coaches tracking progress often use a simple metric: did double underhooks appear under fatigue in the final minute? If it only works fresh, the skill is incomplete.

When reading fight film, pause every time double underhooks shows up and ask what grip or fence angle made it free—and what the opponent failed to do. That reverse-engineering habit beats collecting more techniques.
