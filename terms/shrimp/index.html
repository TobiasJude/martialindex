Shrimp (Hip Escape)

The shrimp (hip escape) is the foundational Brazilian jiu-jitsu movement that drives the hips diagonally away from a pin to create space for frames, knee shields, and guard recovery—especially under side control and mount.

Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Push through the foot, roll to the far shoulder, and drive hips away—arms frame, hips escape.
  • Pillars: side control and mount escapes; sibling of the bridge.
  • Gi pants grips slow the shrimp; no-gi rewards sharper frames and earlier timing.
  • Chain shrimps—do not stop after one unsuccessful bump.
  • Elbow escape and frame escape are shrimp applications, not separate mysteries.

What Is Shrimp (Hip Escape)?

The shrimp—also called the hip escape—is the primary bottom movement for making space when you are pinned. You plant a foot, transfer weight through that line, turn onto the opposite shoulder, and drive the hips away from the opponent’s chest so elbows and knees can reclaim inside position.

It is a concept first and a technique second: every elbow escape, many side-control escapes, and most mount escapes that recover guard are shrimp applications. White belts drill it solo; black belts still refine the angle under real weight.

How Shrimp (Hip Escape) Works

Mechanically the shrimp solves flatness: a flat back under a pin has weak bridges and no path for the knee. Turning to a shoulder and sliding the hips creates a pocket.

Foot drive. The planted foot (often same-side as the direction you shrimp) pushes; the free hip travels diagonally back and away. Elbows stay tight so the top player cannot re-settle into the armpits.

Top player’s job. Follow the hips, cut the angle with the near knee, and re-pin before the bottom knee inserts—or abandon into a better pin when the shrimp wins.

Gi vs No-Gi

The skeleton is identical in gi and no-gi; the friction and grips change the timing.

Gi. Pants and sleeve grips let the top player slow shrimp attempts. Bottom players use collar frames and sleeve posts, but bone frames still beat muscling the fabric.

No-Gi. Without fabric anchors, shrimps land cleaner if frames are early—and fail faster if elbows float. Underhooks and head position replace collar posts on top.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Under side control, you frame the neck and hip, bridge slightly to unload weight, shrimp the hips out, and insert the near knee for half or closed guard. Under mount, the same shrimp feeds the elbow escape after a power shrimp opens the knee line.

Solo drills travel the mat on hand, elbow, and shoulder variations so the pattern survives fatigue.

Types / Variations

Basic backward shrimp

Classic diagonal hip escape off one foot and the opposite shoulder—default warm-up pattern.

Power shrimp / single-shoulder bridge

Bridge onto one shoulder while shrimping to unload a heavy pin—common mount-escape start.

Sliding shrimp

Incremental hip slides that inch the knee inside once space exists—Danaher-style mount recovery.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Drill entries: solo shrimps down the mat; partner resistance on the shoulder; positional from settled side control and low mount.

Counters (top): re-center hips, cut the near knee across, win the crossface before the shrimp completes, or switch to north-south / knee-on-belly.

Bottom contingencies: if one shrimp fails, chain another; if they float, bridge or reverse; if high mount clamps, survive first—then shrimp.

Shrimp vs Bridge

Factor Shrimp (Hip Escape) Bridge (Upa)
Primary job Create lateral/diagonal space Unload weight / reverse via roll
Typical finish Guard recovery Reversal to top or space for shrimp
Best vs Settled pins with frames available High mount / posting opponents
Sibling link Elbow escape, frame escape Upa escape, mount escape chains

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal everywhere—movement concept, not a restricted technique. In IBJJF and ADCC the shrimp is how you erase points after a pass: recover guard before the top player stabilizes.

Safety

Do not thrash the neck while shrimping under a crossface. Keep elbows in to avoid armbars and americana. Tap early if high mount or back control becomes submission-critical.

Common mistakes

  • Pushing with straight arms instead of moving the hips.
  • Stopping after one shrimp while still flat.
  • Leaving elbows wide so the top player climbs to high mount.
  • Shrimping into the opponent instead of diagonally away.

Related notes

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

What is the shrimp in BJJ?

The hip escape: a diagonal movement that creates space under a pin so you can frame and recover guard.

Is shrimping the same as the elbow escape?

No. The shrimp is the movement; the elbow escape is a mount/side application that uses shrimps plus elbow–knee connection.

Why does my shrimp fail under pressure?

Usually you push with arms, stay flat, or shrimp into them. Drive the foot, turn to a shoulder, and chain attempts.

Do I need the shrimp in no-gi?

Yes—often more, because fabric cannot stall your hips. Early frames matter more without pants grips on top.

How should beginners drill the shrimp?

Solo down the mat on hand/elbow/shoulder, then add light partner pressure from side control and mount.

What positions use the shrimp most?

Side control, mount, north-south transitions, and any pin where you need to reclaim the knee line. See side-control escape.

Shrimp or bridge first?

Both. Bridge unloads; shrimp creates lasting space.

The Bottom Line

The shrimp is how BJJ bottom players buy space: foot drive, shoulder turn, hips away, then frames and knees. Master it before collecting flashier escapes.
