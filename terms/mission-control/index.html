Mission Control (Rubber Guard)

Mission Control is the primary home position of Eddie Bravo's rubber guard: a shin across the opponent's back/shoulder line with overhook-style control—freezing posture so you can enter New York, triangles, omoplatas, and related 10th Planet attacks.

By Tobias Mesevage · Published 11 Sep 2026 · Updated 11 Sep 2026 · Plain markdown

Key Takeaways

  • Mission Control = rubber-guard home base (shin + overhook control).
  • Control before attacks—Bravo's sequencing.
  • Native to no-gi 10th Planet; gi versions exist with adjustments.
  • Feeds New York, waiting room, invisible collar paths.
  • Parent rubber guard.

What Is Mission Control?

Mission Control is the foundational rubber-guard configuration in Eddie Bravo's 10th Planet system: you bring a shin across their upper back/shoulder while maintaining an overhook (or equivalent) on the near arm, locking their posture down into your closed-guard-adjacent structure. It is the "mission control" hub from which other rubber-guard positions launch.

How Mission Control Works

The shin and overhook remove their ability to posture and punch (in MMA) or grip fight freely. From a secured Mission Control you climb to New York, enter waiting room for triangles, set omoplatas, or use invisible collar tools in no-gi. Without a tight overhook, the shin alone is just a high closed guard.

Gi vs No-Gi

No-gi. Rubber guard's native environment—Mission Control was built for rashguards and MMA.

Gi. Possible, but sleeves and collars change the overhook story; many gi athletes prefer classic high guard instead.

What Does Mission Control Look Like in Practice?

Alex breaks Jordan's posture, brings the shin across into Mission Control with a tight overhook, then climbs to New York for an omoplata threat—or pivots to waiting room for a triangle when Jordan postures an arm in.

Types / Variations

Basic Mission Control

Shin across + overhook—home base.

To New York

Climb higher for omoplata-oriented rubber control.

To triangle / waiting room

Hip out and lock the triangle path when the arm is in.

Escaping Mission Control (top)

Posture, peel the overhook, clear the shin, and extract before they climb.

Entries, Counters, and Escapes

Entries. From closed guard after posture break; from failed passes that leave them low; dedicated rubber pulls.

Bottom threats. New York omoplata paths, triangles, armbars, invisible-collar control.

Top counters. Never let the shin settle with a live overhook; posture early.

Special Considerations

IBJJF, ADCC, and sport context

Legal. Standard submission finishes. Rubber guard is less common in high-level IBJJF gi but remains a complete no-gi/MMA system.

Safety

Hip flexibility helps; force nothing cold. Tap early to triangles and omoplatas from rubber paths.

Common mistakes

  • Shin without overhook—empty Mission Control.
  • Attacking before the control is locked.
  • Forcing rubber sequences without the flexibility or drilling base.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in forums, and under instructional videos—answered in Martial Index voice.

Why is it called Mission Control?

Eddie Bravo's naming: it is the control hub that directs the rest of the rubber-guard tree.

Do I need to be extremely flexible?

More than average closed guard, yes—but progressive drilling beats forcing splits on day one.

Mission Control in the gi?

Possible. Many gi players still prefer classic closed/high guard; rubber shines more in no-gi.

What should I learn after Mission Control?

New York and waiting-room/triangle paths are the usual next branches.

How do I escape on top?

Peel the overhook, clear the shin, posture, and pass—don't linger stacked in their rubber.

Mission Control vs high guard?

High guard climbs for classic armbars; Mission Control is the rubber overhook/shin hub. See high guard.

The Bottom Line

Mission Control is rubber guard's home: shin across plus overhook control, then branch to New York, triangles, and more. See rubber guard and New York.
