Choku-Zuki

Choku-zuki is the basic straight punch: chamber, extend, kime, hikite—the template behind oi-zuki and gyaku-zuki.

  • Template for oi and gyaku.

What Is Choku-Zuki?

Choku-zuki (直突き) is the straight punch. Many beginners learn it in place before Oi-Zuki and Gyaku-Zuki add stepping and reverse mechanics.

In Karate kihon, choku-zuki isolates fist formation (seiken), Hikite, and Kime.

Often practiced in Kiba-Dachi or a natural standing position.

Martial Index files Choku-Zuki under Karate so searchers get definition, mechanics, and live batch links—not a thin glossary stub.

Read this beside the Karate style hub when you need the art’s big picture; keep the focus on how Choku-Zuki behaves in kihon, kata, and kumite.

How Choku-Zuki Works

Chamber at the hip; punch to the centerline; rotate the fist as your style requires; pull the opposite Hikite; freeze briefly with Kime; recover.

Shoulders stay down; elbows track; do not hyperextend the elbow.

Once clean, add speed and then transfer the same path into stepping punches.

Mechanically, successful choku-zuki work is usually a chain: stance integrity → timing → impact or control → recovery to Karate Stance with Kamae alive.

If distance (maai) is wrong, even perfect form fails—too far and you claw air; too close and you collide into clinches or wild exchanges you did not plan.

Pair offense with the defensive layer this tool implies: punches need Hikite and Kime; kicks need chamber and retract; blocks need a counter path into Gyaku-Zuki or Oi-Zuki.

Dojo vs Competition / Rules Notes

In traditional dojo training, choku-zuki lives inside the kihon–kata–kumite triangle—form quality matters as much as fight utility.

Under WKF sport karate, scoring and contact limits reshape how choku-zuki appears in matches versus full-contact styles such as Kyokushin.

Gym culture may drill choku-zuki daily; grading and competition may shrink the toolkit based on syllabus and ruleset.

Confirm the dojo syllabus and bout agreement before importing sparring volume into contest intensity.

Some organizations specify exact fist rotation timing for exams.

What Does It Look Like in Practice?

Fifty choku-zuki in Kiba-Dachi while a coach fixes fist angle.

Makiwara or pad: same straight path with impact feedback.

Dojo picture: choku-zuki appears when Karate Stance and timing say so, then yields to the next technique in a combination or to a reset of Kamae.

Gym test: can you apply choku-zuki after a feint or Kizami-Zuki setup and still recover your guard? If not, connect it to combinations instead of isolating forever.

Types / Variations

Stationary choku-zuki

Feet still—pure arm path and hikite.

Drill this variation with movement—step in, step out, and reset—so it survives kumite pressure, not only line kihon.

Common Mistakes

  • Corkscrewing the shoulder.
  • Open fist on impact.
  • Dead hikite.
  • Leaning torso to reach.
  • Locking the elbow harshly.
  • Treating choku-zuki as a highlight instead of a distance-and-timing skill.
  • Skipping recovery—landing then posing with hands down and no hikite.
  • Ignoring sibling tools that set or cover the technique.

Training Notes

Line-drill choku-zuki inside full kihon sets; return to Karate Stance and Kamae after every attempt.

Pads or makiwara: technical → moving → forced follow-up so choku-zuki connects to a combination.

Partner drills beat solo fluency; feed realistic rhythm so counter timing is part of the skill.

Film kata and kumite weekly and ask whether choku-zuki appears with honest Kime.

Progress intensity with a sensei—especially for kicks, spins, and full-power Oi-Zuki.

Slow five-second punches: two seconds out, one second kime, two seconds back.

Study Notes

Coaches often describe choku-zuki as “simple to show, hard to own.” The gap is usually not a missing secret—it is missing reps under stress, poor maai, or a stance that collapses when pressure arrives.

When you compare ryuha, you will see stylistic accents: some put choku-zuki behind heavy Kihon repetition; others behind application-minded Kata or sport Kumite. The encyclopedia definition stays stable even when accents change.

Use internal links in this entry as a study path—move from pillars like Karate Stance, Kihon, and Kata into siblings, then return here after class to re-read mistakes with fresh context.

Safety note: encyclopedia pages are reference material, not live coaching. Train under qualified instructors, especially for kicks, joint stress in deep stances, and impact training.

Keep vocabulary precise. Fighters overload words like “guard,” “stance,” and “power.” Martial Index’s Karate entries keep choku-zuki scoped so you are not dumped into unrelated Boxing or BJJ articles that share casual language.

A practical weekly plan: two technical rounds focused on choku-zuki, one situational kumite theme that forces it, and one film review asking where timing failed.
