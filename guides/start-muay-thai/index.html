How to start Muay Thai

Prepare for your first Muay Thai class, learn the stance and distance concepts, and build a useful practice-and-review routine.

Martial Index editorial guide · Updated 20 September 2026

In this guide
  1. Find an introduction that explains the basics
  2. Bring what the class actually requires
  3. Learn stance and distance together
  4. Start with a small set of tools
  5. Treat partner work as a shared task
  6. Review one exchange after class

Find an introduction that explains the basics

Ask whether the gym has a beginner class and how it introduces partner work. Tell the coach your experience level and ask what equipment the trial requires. A class should give you a clear task, such as keeping your stance while stepping or returning your hands after a strike. You can learn a great deal through technique and pad work before deciding whether you want to spar.

Bring what the class actually requires

Ask about gloves, wraps, and any loan equipment before buying a full kit. Wear clean clothing that lets you move comfortably and follow the gym’s footwear policy. Have the coach check how you wrap your hands and how you hold pads. Equipment requirements for competition are separate from what a particular beginner class asks you to bring.

Learn stance and distance together

Your stance is a base you can move from, not a pose to hold rigidly. Notice whether you can step, stop, and recover without crossing your feet or losing balance. Then connect that stance to distance: an opponent who is reachable with a long straight punch is not necessarily at the right distance for a short elbow. Read the technique you are practising together with its setup and recovery.

Start with a small set of tools

A useful initial vocabulary includes the jab, cross, teep, round kick, and clinch. Let the coach choose the order. For each new tool, ask what starts the movement, where the target is, and how you return to a position from which you can defend. Add speed and resistance only within the exercise the coach has set. Landing a strike is only one part of the exchange.

Treat partner work as a shared task

Agree on the drill, target, and intensity before the round. Pad holding is a skill too: ask for a demonstration and tell your partner when you need to reset. Sparring should have a clear purpose and agreed boundaries. You can ask to watch first or practise a simpler version. A productive session gives both partners something specific to improve.

Review one exchange after class

Write down a short sequence such as stance, jab, return to guard, step away. Identify which part broke down rather than adding more techniques. Next time, ask the coach about that moment. To check your understanding, explain why a teep and a round kick solve different problems: the teep commonly manages forward distance, while a round kick travels around an angle. The exact use depends on the exchange.
