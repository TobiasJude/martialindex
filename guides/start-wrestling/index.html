How to start wrestling

Choose a beginner wrestling class, understand the ruleset, and learn stance, hand fighting, takedowns, and controlled positional practice.

Martial Index editorial guide · Updated 20 September 2026

In this guide
  1. Know which wrestling rules you are learning
  2. Ask about the first session
  3. Build a base you can move from
  4. Connect hand fighting to an entry
  5. Learn what happens after contact
  6. Review both sides of the problem

Know which wrestling rules you are learning

Wrestling includes several rulesets. Folkstyle, freestyle, and Greco-Roman do not reward every position in the same way, and their legal techniques differ. Ask the club what it teaches and which age and experience groups it serves. You can learn transferable movement skills while still being precise about the rules you are using.

Ask about the first session

Tell the coach that you are new and ask whether the class introduces stance, movement, and controlled partner drills. Confirm the footwear and clothing requirements and whether beginners are paired separately. You do not need to arrive with a favorite takedown. Look for clear explanations, appropriate partner matching, and a way to stop or simplify an exercise.

Build a base you can move from

Start by noticing your stance, head position, and balance. Practise moving without leaving your feet behind your upper body or standing upright unintentionally. Your coach may introduce a level change before a penetration step. Those are related ideas, but lowering your body and travelling into a takedown are not the same action.

Connect hand fighting to an entry

A tie or grip is useful because it changes position, posture, or access. Instead of memorizing several takedowns at once, learn one setup and one entry that belong together. Ask where your head and hands should be before you move and how your partner should respond during the drill. Controlled repetitions make it easier to identify which connection you lose.

Learn what happens after contact

A takedown is not the end of every exchange. Depending on the rules, control, exposure, turns, escapes, or pins may become the next task. Learn the relevant stopping conditions and boundary rules. Start from an assigned position when that helps isolate the lesson; there is no need for every practice round to begin as an unrestricted match.

Review both sides of the problem

After a lesson, name the setup, the entry, and the intended finish. Then describe the defender’s first response. If you can only remember the attacker’s steps, ask to repeat the defender’s role. A useful self-check is whether you can distinguish a level change from simply reaching with your arms. Ask your coach to watch that distinction rather than judging yourself only by whether a partner went down.
