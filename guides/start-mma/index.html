How to start MMA

A beginner’s route into mixed martial arts: choose a suitable class, connect striking and grappling, and use deliberate practice to learn transitions.

Martial Index editorial guide · Updated 20 September 2026

In this guide
  1. Start with a class designed for beginners
  2. Ask how contact is introduced
  3. Learn the main ranges
  4. Study one transition at a time
  5. Treat the wall as its own lesson
  6. Use the index to prepare and remember

Start with a class designed for beginners

MMA combines striking, clinch work, takedowns, and ground positions. A beginner does not need to learn the whole sport in one session. Ask the coach how the curriculum introduces each area and when students begin combining them. IMMAF publishes participant pathways and coaching information that can help you understand a structured approach to development.

Ask how contact is introduced

Explain your experience and goals. Ask what the trial class includes, which equipment it requires, and how partner intensity is controlled. Separate learning the shape of a movement from practising it with resistance. You can ask to drill a position or watch a round before taking part. A clear progression is more useful than being told to figure everything out during open sparring.

Learn the main ranges

Begin by distinguishing distance striking, the clinch, takedown exchanges, and ground positions. Moving between these ranges changes what is available. A stance that feels comfortable for a single striking drill may need adjustment when a takedown is possible. Read techniques in the context of the range they occupy instead of assuming that a movement transfers unchanged from another sport.

Study one transition at a time

Pick the transition being taught in class: for example, closing distance into a clinch, or standing up from a grounded position. Identify the starting position, what prevents the movement, and what a successful end position looks like. Practise the attacker’s and defender’s roles. That makes it easier to see why isolated striking and grappling lessons need connecting practice.

Treat the wall as its own lesson

A wall or fence changes movement, balance, and available space. Learn it through the gym’s supervised progression rather than treating it as ordinary open-mat grappling. Ask which grips and actions are allowed in the exercise. Competition formats also differ; the fact that something appears in a professional fight does not establish that it belongs in a beginner or amateur session.

Use the index to prepare and remember

Before the next class, read one relevant entry and its parent concept. Afterwards, write down the range, the transition, and one question. Keep the sequence short enough to explain without watching a video. For a self-check, describe why escaping a bottom position matters differently when strikes are included. The answer should mention control, space, and exposure rather than simply naming a submission.
