Key Takeaways

  • Olympic fencing is three weapons with three conventions: foil, épée, sabre.
  • Right of way (foil and sabre) is a rules idea, not a physics idea.
  • Jeet Kune Do cites fencing’s intercept; that does not make JKD a fencing class.

What Is Fencing?

Fencing is the Olympic combat sport of hitting with a blunted blade under electric scoring. Three weapons share a piste and not a rulebook. Foil: torso target, right of way. Épée: whole body, no right of way, double touches allowed. Sabre: cutting weapon, from the waist up, right of way.

It is not HEMA and it is not kendo. It is a modern sport with a nineteenth- and twentieth-century French/Italian pedagogical core and a global competitive circuit.

How Fencing Works

Footwork on the piste creates a distance you can steal. Attacks, parries, ripostes, and counterattacks are the sentence structure. In foil and sabre, referees (and now software plus humans) award priority — right of way — so two lights are not automatically two points.

Jeet Kune Do borrowed the intercept / stop-hit idea. HEMA reconstructs earlier European systems from books. Keep the citations honest.

Three weapons, one sport

A fencer often specializes. Club nights still share footwork. When Martial Index later files fighter pages, the weapon will be part of the name.

FAQ

Questions we hear on the mats, in gyms, and when fighters compare styles.

Why three weapons?

Different historical dueling and pedagogical lines collapsed into three Olympic events. They feel like related sports, not three costumes.

Is épée “more realistic”?

It has no right of way and a whole-body target. That is a rules choice, not a time machine.

Fencing vs HEMA?

Olympic fencing is a living sport. HEMA is source-based reconstruction. People cross over. The pages stay separate.

The Bottom Line

Fencing is the Olympic blade: foil, épée, sabre. Read HEMA for historical European steel and kendo for Japanese shinai in armor.
